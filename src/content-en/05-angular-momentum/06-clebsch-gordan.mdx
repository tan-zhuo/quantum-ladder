The question posed at the end of the previous section: put two angular momenta together — what is the total?

First, why the question is inescapable. The electron in hydrogen carries both orbital $\hat{\vec L}$ and spin
$\hat{\vec S}$; helium has the spins of two electrons; in the deuteron, the proton and neutron each carry spin.
The moment there is more than one share of angular momentum, "how much altogether" becomes compulsory —
how spectral lines split, whether two electrons can squeeze into the same orbital, where hydrogen's 21 cm
radio line comes from: all of it hangs on the answer.

Classical mechanics answers with primary-school arithmetic: $\vec J=\vec J_1+\vec J_2$, vector addition,
with the length continuously adjustable between $|J_1-J_2|$ and $J_1+J_2$ depending on the angle.
In quantum mechanics that road is blocked from the first step: no single "vector" even has three
simultaneously sharp components ([section 5.1](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/01-orbital-angular-momentum/)),
so "add them component by component" is a non-starter. We need a different question.

## The right question: two bases

Work the problem to death on the smallest non-trivial example: **two spin 1/2 particles**
(say, the electron's spin and the proton's spin in hydrogen). Each spin has two states, so the combined
space is four-dimensional, and the most natural basis is "each particle reports its own state":

$$
\ket{\uparrow\uparrow},\quad\ket{\uparrow\downarrow},\quad
\ket{\downarrow\uparrow},\quad\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}
$$

(first arrow = particle 1, second = particle 2). This **product basis** consists of the joint eigenstates of
$(\hat S_{1z},\hat S_{2z})$ and answers "which way does **each one** point".

Now define the total spin operator:

$$
\hat{\vec S}\equiv\hat{\vec S}_1+\hat{\vec S}_2
$$

(operators acting on different particles commute, $[\hat S_{1i},\hat S_{2j}]=0$).
It is easy to check that $\hat{\vec S}$ obeys the standard angular momentum commutation relations —
so the whole machinery of section 5.2 applies automatically:
$(\hat S^2,\hat S_z)$ have joint eigenstates $\ket{S,M}$ with eigenvalues $S(S+1)\hbar^2$ and $M\hbar$.
This **total angular momentum basis** answers "how much **altogether**, and which way does the total point".

Each basis answers its own question, but in general you **cannot have both**: $\hat S^2$ contains terms like
$\hat S_{1x}\hat S_{2x}$ that flip individual spins, and it does not commute with $\hat S_{1z}$.
"Definite total length" and "definite individual orientations" are an incompatible pair of facts —
this is exactly where quantum addition parts ways with classical addition. What remains is a pure change of basis:
expand $\ket{S,M}$ in the product basis. The expansion coefficients are called
**Clebsch–Gordan (CG) coefficients** — an intimidating name for nothing more than a table of basis-change coefficients.

## Building them by hand: singlet and triplet

<Derivation title="Two spin 1/2 particles: constructing every |S, M⟩ from the product basis" level="基础" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Step 1: sort by M first.** $\hat S_z=\hat S_{1z}+\hat S_{2z}$ is diagonal in the product basis —
the two z components simply add (the one part that does behave like classical addition):

| Product state | $M$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}$ | $+1$ |
| $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow},\ \ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$ | $0$ |
| $\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ | $-1$ |

The largest $M$ is $1$, so the largest $S$ is $1$; and $M=0$ holds two states, meaning one dimension
does not belong to the $S=1$ family — a preview that an $S=0$ is also hiding here.

**Step 2: pin down the top state.** The $M=+1$ state is unique, so it can only be the top of the $S=1$ family:

$$
\ket{1,1}=\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}
$$

(One can verify directly that $\hat S^2\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}=2\hbar^2\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}$,
i.e. $S(S+1)=2$.)

**Step 3: climb down with the lowering operator.** The total lowering operator is the sum of the two particles' operators:
$\hat S_-=\hat S_{1-}+\hat S_{2-}$. On the left, the section 5.2 formula gives
$\hat S_-\ket{1,1}=\hbar\sqrt{1\cdot2-1\cdot0}\ket{1,0}=\hbar\sqrt2\ket{1,0}$;
on the right, act particle by particle (for spin 1/2, $\hat S_-\ket{\uparrow}=\hbar\ket{\downarrow}$):

$$
(\hat S_{1-}+\hat S_{2-})\ket{\uparrow\uparrow}
=\hbar\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}+\hbar\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}
$$

Setting the two sides equal:

$$
\ket{1,0}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt2}
$$

**Step 4: one more rung.** Repeat for $\ket{1,0}$: the left side gives
$\hbar\sqrt2\ket{1,-1}$; acting on the expression above,
$\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}\to\hbar\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$ (only particle 1 can be lowered),
$\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}\to\hbar\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$, totalling
$\frac{2\hbar}{\sqrt2}\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}=\hbar\sqrt2\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}$:

$$
\ket{1,-1}=\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}
$$

Bottom reached. The three members of the $S=1$ family are all present — the **triplet**.

**Step 5: recover the missing dimension.** Three of the four dimensions are spent; the remaining one
must lie in the $M=0$ plane, orthogonal to $\ket{1,0}$. There is only one orthogonal combination
(up to a phase convention):

$$
\ket{0,0}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Check that it really has $S=0$: $\hat S_-\ket{0,0}
=\frac{\hbar\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}-\hbar\ket{\downarrow\downarrow}}{\sqrt2}=0$,
and likewise $\hat S_+$ gives zero — it can climb neither up nor down, so it can only be a lone $S=0$.
This is the **singlet**.

**Summary**: the four product states regroup as "$3+1$":

$$
\underbrace{\ket{1,1},\ \ket{1,0},\ \ket{1,-1}}_{\text{triplet }S=1}
\qquad
\underbrace{\ket{0,0}}_{\text{singlet }S=0}
$$

Written $\tfrac12\otimes\tfrac12=1\oplus0$. Dimension check: $2\times2=3+1$. ✓

</Derivation>

The expansion coefficients in that derivation — $1$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, $-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ — are the entire
true identity of the CG coefficients $\braket{m_1\,m_2}{S\,M}$:
"the amplitude of product state $\ket{m_1 m_2}$ inside total state $\ket{S,M}$".
Example of use: the system is in $\ket{1,0}$ and you measure particle 1's $S_{1z}$;
the probability of "up" is the squared modulus of the amplitude $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, i.e. $50\%$.
The page-long CG tables in textbook appendices are nothing but steps 2 through 5 repeated for
every $(j_1,j_2)$ — the method is identical each time: pin the top, climb with the lowering operator,
find the new family in the orthogonal complement.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: why "three symmetric, one antisymmetric" of all things.**

Swap the two particles: all three triplet members are unchanged (symmetric), while the singlet flips sign
(antisymmetric). That symmetry rides along with total spin by no coincidence: $\hat S^2$ is invariant
under exchange, so its eigenstates must have definite exchange symmetry.

A crude but useful picture: in the triplet the two spins "cooperate, aligned";
the $M=0$ member $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}+\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}$
is "aligned but lying sideways" — the total length is still full ($S=1$), just not along z.
The singlet is two spins "in total opposition", cancelling cleanly:
not only is $M=0$, but the total spin measures zero along every direction.

This "$3+1$" grouping takes centre stage in chapter 8:
the Pauli principle demands an antisymmetric total electron wavefunction, so the spin symmetry
dictates the spatial symmetry in turn — the starting point of covalent bonds and magnetism.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Formula: the rotational invariance of the singlet.**

$S=0$ means the singlet's total spin component vanishes along **every** direction; in other words,
it does not budge under rotations. Verify directly: rewrite in the x basis
$\ket{\pm x}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow}\pm\ket{\downarrow}}{\sqrt2}$, and expansion gives

$$
\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt2}
=-\,\frac{\ket{+x\,{-}x}-\ket{-x\,{+}x}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Change to the x basis and the form is untouched (up to an overall phase).
**Whichever axis you measure along, the two particles' results are strictly opposite.**

This state is also **entangled**: it cannot be written as "state of particle 1 $\otimes$ state of particle 2"
(if it could, the expansion would have a single term or factorable coefficients, contradicting the formula above).
The EPR paradox and the Bell experiments use exactly this singlet — see chapter 9.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The most common mistake: |↑↓⟩ is not an eigenstate of total spin">
"One up and one down, so the total spin is zero" — wrong.
$\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ does have $M=0$, but it is an equal-amplitude superposition of both families:

$$
\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}=\frac{\ket{1,0}+\ket{0,0}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Measure total spin $S^2$ on it and you get $2\hbar^2$ ($S=1$) half the time and 0 the other half.
"z components cancelling" and "vectors cancelling" are different things —
$\ket{1,0}$ is a living example of cancelled z components with an uncancelled total length.
</Callout>

## The general rule and dimension counting

For arbitrary $j_1,j_2$ the method is exactly as above, and the conclusion just as clean.
The allowed values of the total angular momentum $J$ are

$$
J=|j_1-j_2|,\ |j_1-j_2|+1,\ \dots,\ j_1+j_2\qquad(\text{each }J\text{ appearing once})
$$

— from "antiparallel" to "parallel" in steps of 1. The classical "length continuously adjustable
between difference and sum" becomes "between difference and sum, adjustable in discrete integer steps".
Each $J$ family has $2J+1$ members, and the total dimension must balance:

$$
(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)=\sum_{J=|j_1-j_2|}^{j_1+j_2}(2J+1)
$$

A quick self-check tool. One example we will need immediately: an electron in a hydrogen p orbital,
$l=1$ coupled to spin $s=\tfrac12$:

$$
1\otimes\tfrac12=\tfrac32\oplus\tfrac12,\qquad
3\times2=4+2\ \checkmark
$$

The six states of a p electron regroup into four with $j=\tfrac32$ and two with $j=\tfrac12$.
Remember this "$4+2$" — next section it becomes two real lines in a spectrum.

<Callout type="insight" title="CG coefficients are the price list for changing the question">
This whole section compresses into one sentence: **the product basis and the total angular momentum basis
are two coordinate systems for the same four-dimensional room, and the CG coefficients are the rotation matrix
between them.**

Which basis you want depends on which one the Hamiltonian favours. When the two spins ignore each other
(say, each sitting in its own magnetic field), the product basis is the eigenbasis — use it.
When two angular momenta couple through a dot product
(hydrogen's hyperfine $\hat{\vec S}_1\cdot\hat{\vec S}_2$, next section's spin–orbit $\hat{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\vec S}$),
the square of the total angular momentum enters the Hamiltonian and the total basis is the eigenbasis.
The singlet–triplet splitting of hydrogen's ground state is about $5.9\ \mu\text{eV}$,
corresponding to a wavelength of 21 cm — radio astronomy maps the spiral-arm structure of the Milky Way
using exactly this "visible only after the change of basis" line.
</Callout>

## What comes next

We can now add $\hat{\vec L}$ and $\hat{\vec S}$ into a total angular momentum $\hat{\vec J}$,
and we know a p electron's six states regroup into a $j=\tfrac32$ family and a $j=\tfrac12$ family.

But so far this is only a mathematical regrouping — the two families still have exactly the same energy.
The next section divides the estate between them: as the electron moves around the nucleus,
its own magnetic moment feels an internally generated magnetic field, and the coupling term
$\hat{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\vec S}$ pushes the energies of different-$j$ states apart.
The two D lines in a sodium lamp's yellow glow, 0.6 nm apart, are exactly this inheritance being split.
