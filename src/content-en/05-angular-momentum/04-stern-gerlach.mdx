The previous section handed spin a crisp set of mathematics: measure along any direction and there are
only two answers, $\pm\tfrac\hbar2$. That sounds far too tidy to be how the real world behaves.
This section turns to experiment — the best case in the history of quantum mechanics of
"one apparatus teaching an entire course".

## 1922: a beam of silver atoms and two traces

Here is what Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach did in Frankfurt:
heat silver in an oven until it evaporates, let the silver atoms stream through a slit to form a beam,
fly it past a magnet with a knife-edge pole above and a flat pole below (the key point: the field is
**inhomogeneous**, stronger near the sharp pole), and let the atoms land on a glass plate to be developed.

Why inhomogeneous? The energy of a magnetic moment $\vec\mu$ in a field is $U=-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$.
A uniform field only twists the moment (a torque) and exerts no net force; only a non-uniform field pulls:

$$
F_z=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}=\mu_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}
$$

The direction of the force is set by the **sign** of $\mu_z$, its size by the value of $\mu_z$.
So the landing position on the glass plate is a readout of $\mu_z$ —
and a silver atom's magnetic moment is proportional to the spin of its lone valence electron
(of silver's 47 electrons, 46 pair off and cancel; the remaining one happens to sit in an $l=0$ orbital,
so orbital angular momentum can neither help nor interfere —
silver is nature's gift of a pure spin sample). Hence: **this apparatus is an $\hat S_z$ meter**,
with the landing spot as its needle.

Now compare two predictions:

- **Classical prediction**: atoms leave the oven with randomly oriented moments, $\mu_z$ distributed
  continuously from $-\mu$ to $+\mu$, so the glass plate should show one continuous vertical **smear**.
- **Actual result**: no smear. The silver atoms split neatly into **two clusters**, with empty space between.

Two clusters — no more, no fewer. Compare with the previous section: $2s+1=2$ gives $s=\tfrac12$,
each atom's $S_z$ can only be $+\tfrac\hbar2$ or $-\tfrac\hbar2$, with nothing in between.
Orientation in space is quantised — which is why the phenomenon was christened "space quantisation".

<Callout type="note" title="A historical joke">
Stern and Gerlach knew nothing of spin at the time (the concept arrived only in 1925).
They believed they were confirming the Bohr model's quantisation of orbital angular momentum,
saw two beams, and declared success. By today's understanding, the silver valence electron has $l=0$ —
its orbital angular momentum is exactly zero. They had "successfully confirmed" a wrong theory,
while actually measuring a quantity that would not be invented for another three years.
The experiment was right, the interpretation was wrong — and the experiment outlived the interpretation.
</Callout>

## Chaining the devices: three configurations

A single device is merely evidence of quantisation. The genuine textbook-grade shock comes from **cascading**:
feed one output beam of an SG device into the next. Notation: SGz is a device with its field along z,
SGx along x; each has two exits, labelled $+$ and $-$.

<Derivation title="The three cascaded configurations, computed one by one" level="基础" minutes={10} open={true}>

Only three tools are needed: the $\hat S_z$ basis $\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}$; the $\hat S_x$ eigenstates
solved in the previous section,

$$
\ket{+x}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow}}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\ket{-x}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow}-\ket{\downarrow}}{\sqrt2}
$$

(set $\theta=\tfrac\pi2$ and $\phi=0$ or $\pi$ in the arbitrary-direction formula);
and the projection postulate of [section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/):
probabilities are squared amplitudes, and the post-measurement state is the corresponding eigenstate.

**Configuration 1: SGz → SGz. A consistency check on the instrument.**

Atoms from the oven enter the first SGz and split randomly into two beams; keep only the $+$ exit
(the state is projected to $\ket{\uparrow}$) and feed it into a second SGz. Probabilities:

$$
P(+)=|\braket{\uparrow}{\uparrow}|^2=1,\qquad
P(-)=|\braket{\downarrow}{\uparrow}|^2=0
$$

**Output: $100\%$ from the $+$ exit.** Repeating a measurement of the same observable repeats the result —
the "repeatability" clause of the projection postulate. The instrument is self-consistent, not a random-number generator.

**Configuration 2: SGz → SGx. Ask the question along a different axis.**

Take the same $\ket{\uparrow}$ beam and send it into SGx. Rewrite it in the $\hat S_x$ basis:

$$
\ket{\uparrow}=\frac{\ket{+x}+\ket{-x}}{\sqrt2}
$$

(add the two defining equations of $\ket{\pm x}$ to verify). Then

$$
P(+x)=\left|\braket{+x}{\uparrow}\right|^2=\left|\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right|^2=\frac12,
\qquad P(-x)=\frac12
$$

**Output: $50\%:50\%$, completely random atom by atom.** A state with definite $S_z$
has **no answer** to the question "what is $S_x$" — only probabilities —
because $[\hat S_z,\hat S_x]\ne0$: the two share no common eigenstates.

**Configuration 3: SGz → SGx → SGz. The knockout punch.**

Continue from configuration 2, keeping only the $+$ exit of SGx — this beam's state **has been projected to** $\ket{+x}$.
Now send it into a third device, SGz. The naive expectation: these atoms were already "certified" back at
the first device as $S_z=+\tfrac\hbar2$; in between we merely measured an unrelated $S_x$, so surely
$100\%$ should exit through $+$.

Compute it. The current state is $\ket{+x}=(\ket{\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow})/\sqrt2$:

$$
P(+)=\left|\braket{\uparrow}{+x}\right|^2=\frac12,\qquad
P(-)=\left|\braket{\downarrow}{+x}\right|^2=\frac12
$$

**Output: $50\%:50\%$. Half the atoms have $S_z=-\tfrac\hbar2$** —
even though every atom in this beam was once confirmed by the first device to have $S_z=+\tfrac\hbar2$,
and nothing touched their z direction along the way.

Tallying the survival fractions across all three stages: oven → SGz keep $+$ ($\tfrac12$) → SGx keep $+$ ($\tfrac12$) →
SGz exit $-$ ($\tfrac12$): in the end $\tfrac18$ of the atoms complete this "impossible" journey.

</Derivation>

Configuration 3 is non-commutativity made flesh: **the very act of measuring $S_x$ erases the memory of $S_z$.**
After projection onto $\ket{+x}$, not one bit of "used to be $\ket{\uparrow}$" survives in the state —
$\ket{+x}$ is $\ket{+x}$, regardless of which beam it was projected from.
The definiteness of $S_z$ and $S_x$ works like a seesaw: press one end down and the other must rise.

## "Maybe the atoms just carry labels?" — the rout of naive hidden variables

Faced with configuration 3, a natural line of resistance is: perhaps the randomness is merely our ignorance.
Imagine each atom leaves the factory with a label listing its answer to every possible direction,
say "if asked z answer $+$, if asked x answer $-$". Measurement merely **reads** the label,
and the 50:50 of configuration 3 just reflects the statistics of the labels. No "measurement changes the state" required.

This model can explain the two clusters of a single measurement, and can even fake the 50:50 of configuration 2.
But there is one upgraded experiment it cannot survive.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The recombination experiment (look at neither path).**

Modify the SGx: block **neither** the $+x$ nor the $-x$ beam,
and use a reversed field to fold the two beams losslessly back into one, with no detector anywhere along the way —
the device splits the beam but never "looks" at the outcome. Then send the result into SGz.

The label model's prediction is unambiguous: each atom took one path or the other,
and whichever it took has no bearing on the label's "answer for z";
moreover every incoming atom was filtered by the first device as $z:+$.
So the output **should be $100\%$ up**… wait — in the label model, measuring x does not
rewrite labels either, so configuration 3 should also give 100%?
The label theorist patches it: "passing through the SGx field randomly rewrites the z label".
Fine — but in the recombination experiment the atoms traverse those same fields,
so the patched model predicts 50:50 after recombination too.

**Quantum mechanics predicts: $100\%$ up.**

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The quantum calculation: splitting a beam is not measuring it.**

No detector, no projection. The whole process is unitary evolution, and the **amplitudes** of both paths survive:

$$
\ket{\uparrow}
=\frac1{\sqrt2}\ket{+x}+\frac1{\sqrt2}\ket{-x}
\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{split, then recombine}\ }\
\frac1{\sqrt2}\ket{+x}+\frac1{\sqrt2}\ket{-x}=\ket{\uparrow}
$$

The two paths superpose coherently; on recombination they interfere back into the original state, and $P(+)=1$.

Only when you place a detector on one of the paths (even without reading it out)
does the superposition collapse into either/or at the moment of entanglement with the environment,
and the output falls back to 50:50.

Experiment sides with quantum mechanics. What destroys the z memory is not the physical disturbance of the field,
but the fact that **path information gets recorded**.

</div>
</DualView>

Models of this kind — the atom carrying a pre-written answer sheet for every question, i.e.
**non-contextual hidden variables** — are already limping badly here; chapter 9's Bell inequalities
will give them, together with all their local cousins, a formal burial.
For now, the experimental fact is enough: the values of non-commuting observables
**do not pre-exist, waiting to be read off**.

<Callout type="insight" title="The SG apparatus: the measurement postulate cast in iron">
Chapter 3's measurement postulate is abstract: spectrum, projection, Born rule, post-measurement state.
The SG apparatus makes each clause a physical object —

- **Spectrum**: the number of clusters on the glass plate = the number of eigenvalues (two clusters, $\pm\hbar/2$).
- **Born rule**: the ratio of atom counts in the clusters = the ratio of squared amplitudes.
- **Projection**: a beam leaving a given exit is in the corresponding eigenstate — blocking the other exits is one act of "measure and select".
- **Repeatability**: chain two along the same axis and the second reproduces the first, 100%.

Whenever the measurement postulate feels murky, translate the question into a string of SG devices
and the answer tends to surface by itself. This trick — the SG apparatus as a thinking tool —
is the shared opening move of both Feynman's and Sakurai's classic textbooks.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two common misreadings">
**"The magnet smashes each atom into two halves."** No. Every atom lands whole, in the upper cluster or the lower one;
no atom was ever "half up, half down". What splits is the beam's destination, not the atom.

**"Measurement disturbs the spin, so the result changes."** Half right, entirely wrong. The memory loss of
configuration 3 cannot be blamed on mechanical disturbance by the field — in the recombination experiment
the fields and paths are identical, and as long as no record is kept of which path was taken,
the z memory survives intact. What erases the memory is the acquisition of information, not a collision-style kick.
</Callout>

## What comes next

So far, the SG apparatus has only answered "which way does the spin point at this instant".
But **between** two measurements, what is the spin doing?

Put the atom in a uniform magnetic field (no gradient needed this time — we are not measuring, only evolving),
and the spin's expectation value circles the field like a gyroscope. This rotation can be calculated, can be measured,
and is the entire working principle of nuclear magnetic resonance and hospital MRI — the subject of the next section.
