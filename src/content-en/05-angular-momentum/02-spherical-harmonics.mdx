The previous section placed a bet: the commutation relations $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z$
(and their cyclic partners) alone can determine every eigenvalue. This section pays it off. The whole argument
uses just three ingredients: the commutation relations, the Hermiticity of the operators,
and the plain truth that "a squared length cannot be negative".

## Building the ladder

The lesson of the harmonic oscillator: to "hop rungs" among the eigenvalues, you need an operator $\hat A$
whose commutator with $\hat L_z$ is **proportional to $\hat A$ itself** — then acting with $\hat A$ on an eigenstate
produces another eigenstate, with the eigenvalue shifted by one rung. Try this combination:

$$
\hat L_\pm\equiv\hat L_x\pm\ii\hat L_y
$$

They are not Hermitian ($\hat L_+^\dagger=\hat L_-$) and represent no observable — they are tools, not objects of study.
Compute the two key commutators (both expand directly from the fundamental relations):

$$
[\hat L_z,\hat L_\pm]=[\hat L_z,\hat L_x]\pm\ii[\hat L_z,\hat L_y]
=\ii\hbar\hat L_y\pm\ii(-\ii\hbar\hat L_x)=\pm\hbar\hat L_\pm
$$

$$
[\hat L^2,\hat L_\pm]=0\quad(\hat L^2\ \text{commutes with every component})
$$

The first line says: $\hat L_+$ raises the $L_z$ eigenvalue by $\hbar$ and $\hat L_-$ lowers it by $\hbar$ — that is the ladder.
The second line says: while you climb, the eigenvalue of $\hat L^2$ **does not budge** — the ladder stands on a cone of fixed length.

## Climbing out the full spectrum

<Derivation title="The spectrum by pure algebra: from commutators to l(l+1)ħ²" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Set up notation.** Let $\ket{\lambda,m}$ be a joint eigenstate:

$$
\hat L^2\ket{\lambda,m}=\lambda\hbar^2\ket{\lambda,m},\qquad
\hat L_z\ket{\lambda,m}=m\hbar\ket{\lambda,m}
$$

(Factoring out $\hbar$ makes $\lambda$ and $m$ pure numbers. At this point we know nothing about either.)

**Step 1: check that the ladder really climbs.** Ask what $\hat L_+\ket{\lambda,m}$ is an eigenstate of:

$$
\hat L_z\big(\hat L_+\ket{\lambda,m}\big)
=\big(\hat L_+\hat L_z+[\hat L_z,\hat L_+]\big)\ket{\lambda,m}
=\big(m\hbar+\hbar\big)\hat L_+\ket{\lambda,m}
$$

So $\hat L_+\ket{\lambda,m}$ is either the zero vector or an eigenstate with $m+1$.
Likewise $\hat L_-$ gives $m-1$. And $[\hat L^2,\hat L_\pm]=0$ guarantees $\lambda$ stays put.

**Step 2: the ladder must have a top.** The crucial physical input: a component cannot exceed the total length. Precisely,

$$
\lambda\hbar^2-m^2\hbar^2
=\bra{\lambda,m}\hat L^2-\hat L_z^2\ket{\lambda,m}
=\bra{\lambda,m}\hat L_x^2+\hat L_y^2\ket{\lambda,m}\ge0
$$

The last step holds because the expectation of $\hat L_x^2$ is $\|\hat L_x\ket{\lambda,m}\|^2$, a squared length, hence non-negative.
So $m^2\le\lambda$: $m$ is trapped in a finite interval and the ladder **cannot climb forever**.
There must be a top rung $m_{\max}$ with

$$
\hat L_+\ket{\lambda,m_{\max}}=0
$$

(Not "acts and survives" but outright annihilated — that is the only way a ladder can terminate.)

**Step 3: solve for λ from the top-rung condition.** We need an identity, obtained by direct expansion:

$$
\hat L_-\hat L_+=(\hat L_x-\ii\hat L_y)(\hat L_x+\ii\hat L_y)
=\hat L_x^2+\hat L_y^2+\ii[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]
=\hat L^2-\hat L_z^2-\hbar\hat L_z
$$

Apply it to the top-rung state: the left side is zero, and the right side gives

$$
0=\big(\lambda-m_{\max}^2-m_{\max}\big)\hbar^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lambda=m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)
$$

**Step 4: treat the bottom the same way.** With $\hat L_-\ket{\lambda,m_{\min}}=0$, the identity
$\hat L_+\hat L_-=\hat L^2-\hat L_z^2+\hbar\hat L_z$ gives

$$
\lambda=m_{\min}(m_{\min}-1)
$$

Setting the two expressions equal: $m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)=m_{\min}(m_{\min}-1)$.
Read as a quadratic in $m_{\min}$, the two roots are $m_{\min}=-m_{\max}$ and
$m_{\min}=m_{\max}+1$. The latter is absurd (a bottom above the top), so

$$
m_{\min}=-m_{\max}
$$

The ladder is symmetric about zero.

**Step 5: integers or half-integers?** Climbing from bottom to top in steps of $+1$ takes some whole number of steps $N$:

$$
m_{\max}-m_{\min}=2m_{\max}=N
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m_{\max}=\frac{N}{2}=0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ 2,\ \dots
$$

**Conclusion.** Writing $l\equiv m_{\max}$ (the conventional symbol):

$$
\hat L^2\ket{l,m}=l(l+1)\hbar^2\ket{l,m},\qquad
\hat L_z\ket{l,m}=m\hbar\ket{l,m}
$$

where $l$ is a non-negative integer or half-integer, and $m$ runs from $-l$ to $+l$ one rung at a time — $2l+1$ values in all.
Not one differential equation solved, and the whole spectrum is in hand.

</Derivation>

While we are at it, fix the ladder-operator coefficients (we will need them for building matrices and for Clebsch–Gordan coefficients).
From the identity above, $\|\hat L_\pm\ket{l,m}\|^2=\big[l(l+1)-m(m\pm1)\big]\hbar^2$; with the positive-real phase convention:

$$
\hat L_\pm\ket{l,m}=\hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)-m(m\pm1)}\;\ket{l,m\pm1}
$$

Substitute $m=\pm l$ and the square root vanishes on the spot — the formula seals the top and bottom of the ladder all by itself.

<Callout type="insight" title="Note that it is l(l+1), not l²">
$|\vec L|=\sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$ is strictly larger than the maximum of $L_z$, namely $l\hbar$ (whenever $l\neq0$):
the angular momentum vector **can never lie down fully along the z axis** — if it could, $L_x=L_y=0$ would both be sharp,
violating last section's uncertainty relation. The excess is exactly the quantum fluctuation that can never be scrubbed out of
$L_x,L_y$: in the $m=l$ state, $\langle\hat L_x^2\rangle+\langle\hat L_y^2\rangle=l\hbar^2$.
The "cone" of the cone picture never closes up into a line; its minimum opening angle satisfies $\cos\theta=l/\sqrt{l(l+1)}$.
</Callout>

## Back to the sphere: orbital angular momentum drops half the values

The algebra allows half-integer $l$. But **orbital** angular momentum — the kind genuinely built from
$\vec r\times\vec p$ — realises only the integer half. The reason lives in the wavefunction. In spherical coordinates
$(r,\theta,\phi)$ (with $\theta$ the angle from the z axis and $\phi$ the azimuthal angle around it),
$\hat L_z$ takes a remarkably clean form:

$$
\hat L_z=-\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}
$$

Its eigenfunctions are $\ee^{\ii m\phi}$. Now the decisive blow:
$\phi$ and $\phi+2\pi$ are **the same point in space**, and a wavefunction must have a single value at a single point:

$$
\ee^{\ii m(\phi+2\pi)}=\ee^{\ii m\phi}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\ee^{2\pi\ii m}=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m\ \text{must be an integer}
$$

Were $m=\tfrac12$, the wavefunction would change sign after one full turn — two values at the same point,
which no function on space is allowed to do. So orbital angular momentum has only $l=0,1,2,\dots$.
The half-integer seats stand empty: the algebra plainly permits them, but a position wavefunction cannot accommodate them.
Hold on to this loose end — next section it finds its owner.

## Spherical harmonics: what the eigenstates look like

The joint eigenfunctions of $(\hat L^2,\hat L_z)$ depend only on the angles. Written $Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$,
they are the **spherical harmonics** — "harmonics on the sphere", playing the role that $\sin$ and $\cos$ play on a line.
The construction copies the algebra: the top-rung condition $\hat L_+Y_l^l=0$ is a first-order differential equation
with solution $Y_l^l\propto\sin^l\theta\,\ee^{\ii l\phi}$, and $\hat L_-$ walks you down from there. The first few:

$$
Y_0^0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\qquad
Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta,\qquad
Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,\ee^{\pm\ii\phi}
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**How to read the shapes.**

$l=0$ (the s states of chemistry): $Y_0^0$ is constant — the probability is perfectly uniform over all directions, a round ball.
No angular momentum means no "rotation axis" available to break spherical symmetry, which makes perfect sense.

$l=1$ (p states): $|Y_1^0|^2\propto\cos^2\theta$ is a dumbbell along the z axis —
$m=0$ means no rotation about z, the angular momentum lies sideways, and the particle prefers the poles;
$|Y_1^{\pm1}|^2\propto\sin^2\theta$ is a doughnut around the equator — full-speed rotation about z, with the particle concentrated at the equator.
The general rule: the closer $|m|$ is to $l$, the more the distribution hugs the equatorial plane; $m=0$ hugs the z axis instead —
just like a classical spinning top: whatever axis you spin about, the mass flings out into the plane perpendicular to it.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Node structure and basic properties.**

$Y_l^m$ has $l$ nodal lines (curves where the function vanishes) on the sphere:
$|m|$ of them run along meridians, from the oscillation of the real/imaginary parts of $\ee^{\ii m\phi}$,
and $l-|m|$ run along circles of latitude, from the zeros of the $\theta$ part.
**$l$ counts the total nodal lines, $m$ counts the windings around the axis** — the direct heir of the one-dimensional
"number of nodes = n − 1" rule.

Orthonormality and completeness:

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi (Y_{l'}^{m'})^*\,Y_l^m\,\sin\theta\,\dd\theta\,\dd\phi
=\delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}
$$

Any well-behaved function on the sphere can be expanded in the $Y_l^m$ —
they are to the sphere what plane waves are to the line.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="l fixes the length, m fixes the tilt — nothing more">
A common misreading: "the $m=1$ state is rotating clockwise about the z axis".
$Y_l^m$ is the angular part of a **stationary state**; the probability distribution does not change in time,
and nothing is "in the middle of rotating".
What $m$ tells you is that measuring $L_z$ yields $m\hbar$,
and whether the probability cloud leans towards the poles or the equator — it is not a movie of rotation.
Likewise, $l(l+1)\hbar^2$ is the value of $L^2$; do not write $l^2\hbar^2$.
That difference enters the hydrogen level formula of the next chapter, where it is compared against experiment in cold hard numbers.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The algebra allows $l=\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\dots$, yet wavefunctions on the sphere cannot house them.
Mathematics reserved the seats; space cannot fill them — so were those seats reserved for nothing?

In 1922, two German physicists fired a beam of silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field
and found **two** separated traces on a glass plate — precisely the value of $2l+1$ at $l=\tfrac12$.
The half-integer seat has an owner, and the angular momentum it carries has nothing to do with motion through space.
