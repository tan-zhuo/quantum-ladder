The previous section's SG apparatus answers "which way does the spin point right now". This section asks
the next natural question: between two measurements, what is the spin **doing**?

This is not mere theoretical fussiness. Every hydrogen nucleus in your body is a spin $\tfrac12$,
and a hospital's magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machine spends its whole day talking to them —
the physics it relies on is precisely the answer to "how does spin evolve between measurements".

## The Hamiltonian: one term is all that survives

Place a particle with magnetic moment $\vec\mu$ in a **uniform** field $\vec B=B\hat z$.
Uniform means no force (last section's force came from the gradient), so the particle does not move;
the position ledger is perfectly quiet, and only the spin ledger evolves. The energy is just the classical orientation energy:

$$
\hat H=-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B=-\gamma B\,\hat S_z
$$

Here $\vec\mu=\gamma\hat{\vec S}$, and the proportionality constant $\gamma$ is the **gyromagnetic ratio** —
one fixed value per particle species. For the electron $|\gamma_e|=g_e\mu_B/\hbar$ (with $g_e\approx2.002$);
the proton's is about 660 times smaller (larger mass, smaller moment).

This is arguably the simplest non-trivial Hamiltonian in quantum mechanics: a $2\times2$ diagonal matrix.
The eigenstates are just $\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}$, with energies

$$
E_\uparrow=-\frac{\gamma\hbar B}{2},\qquad E_\downarrow=+\frac{\gamma\hbar B}{2}
$$

(We narrate with $\gamma$ positive; the electron's $\gamma$ is actually negative, which merely reverses the sense of rotation.)
The gap between the two levels, $\Delta E=\gamma\hbar B$, opens linearly with the field — this is the **Zeeman splitting**
in its simplest two-level form. Converting that gap to a frequency, write

$$
\omega\equiv\frac{\Delta E}{\hbar}=\gamma B
$$

This frequency is about to become the star of the show.

## Solving the evolution: the spin circles the field

<Derivation title="Evolution of an arbitrary initial state and the precession of ⟨S⟩" level="基础" minutes={9} open={true}>

**Step 1: the initial state.** Let the spin at $t=0$ point "up" along some direction $(\theta,\phi=0)$
(using the general formula of [section 5.3](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/03-spin-half/); taking $\phi=0$ loses no generality):

$$
\ket{\chi(0)}=\cos\frac\theta2\ket{\uparrow}+\sin\frac\theta2\ket{\downarrow}
$$

**Step 2: apply the stationary-state expansion.** The eigenstates of $\hat H$ are ready-made;
just hang a phase clock $\ee^{-\ii E t/\hbar}$ on each component (see [section 3.9](/en/ch/03-formalism/09-time-evolution-pictures/)):

$$
\ket{\chi(t)}=\cos\frac\theta2\,\ee^{+\ii\omega t/2}\ket{\uparrow}
+\sin\frac\theta2\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t/2}\ket{\downarrow}
$$

(substituting $E_\uparrow=-\hbar\omega/2$, $E_\downarrow=+\hbar\omega/2$).
Pull out the overall phase $\ee^{+\ii\omega t/2}$ and discard it:

$$
\ket{\chi(t)}\ \sim\ \cos\frac\theta2\ket{\uparrow}
+\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\sin\frac\theta2\ket{\downarrow}
$$

**Step 3: recognise who this is.** Compare with the general spin state
$\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket{\uparrow}+\ee^{\ii\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket{\downarrow}$ —
this is exactly the "up" state along the direction $(\theta,\ \phi(t)=-\omega t)$!
The polar angle $\theta$ does not budge; the azimuthal angle rotates uniformly.
**Evolution = circling the z axis** — a line of latitude on the Bloch sphere.

**Step 4: check the expectation values.** Compute each (sandwich with the Pauli matrices):

$$
\langle\hat S_z\rangle=\frac\hbar2\cos\theta=\text{const}
$$

$$
\langle\hat S_x\rangle=\frac\hbar2\sin\theta\cos\omega t,\qquad
\langle\hat S_y\rangle=-\frac\hbar2\sin\theta\sin\omega t
$$

$\langle\vec S\rangle$ is a vector of constant length rotating uniformly about the field direction
at angular velocity $\omega=\gamma B$ — exactly like a classical gyroscope precessing under gravity.
This rotation is called **Larmor precession**, and $\omega$ the Larmor frequency.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: precession is the beat of two tuning forks.**

The spin state is a superposition of $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$,
and the two components' phase clocks tick at different rates (different energies). The relative phase accumulates
steadily at $\omega=\Delta E/\hbar$ — seen macroscopically, the transverse components go round in a circle.

This is **the same phenomenon** as the superposition of two eigenstates in
[section 2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/), where the probability cloud sloshes at $\omega_{21}$:
in any two-level superposition, observables oscillate at the frequency of the level gap.
Spin merely draws the picture on a sphere.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Numbers: each particle gets its own radio channel.**

$\omega=\gamma B$; divide by $2\pi$ for an ordinary frequency:

- Electron, $B=1\ \text{T}$: $f=g_e\mu_B B/h\approx28.0\ \text{GHz}$ (microwave).
- Proton, $B=1\ \text{T}$: $f\approx42.6\ \text{MHz}$ (shortwave radio band).
- Proton, $B=3\ \text{T}$ (a mainstream MRI magnet): $f\approx127.7\ \text{MHz}$.

The frequency depends only on $\gamma$ and $B$, **not on the spin's initial direction**
($\theta$ sets the radius of the circle, never the rate). So each nuclear species, at a given field,
has its own immovable "channel" — to talk to a particular nucleus, tune the radio to its frequency.

</div>
</DualView>

## Resonance: how to flip a spin

Watching a spin go round in circles is not much use by itself; we also want to **control** it —
say, flip an up spin to down. The level gap is $\hbar\omega$, so intuition says:
apply a perturbation at exactly the frequency $\omega$.

Concretely: on top of the large static field $B_0\hat z$, add a small field
$B_1$ **rotating** in the xy plane ($B_1\ll B_0$) at frequency $\omega_{\text{rf}}$. The qualitative results
(the full solution uses the rotating-frame trick of section 3.9; here we make the physics clear):

- **Off resonance** ($\omega_{\text{rf}}$ away from $\omega_0=\gamma B_0$): the small field rotates
  "out of step" with the spin; its push helps one moment and hinders the next, averaging to almost nothing.
- **On resonance** ($\omega_{\text{rf}}=\omega_0$): view things from a frame rotating along with the spin.
  The effect of the big field $B_0$ is exactly cancelled by the rotation, leaving only a **stationary**
  small field $B_1$ — so the spin turns slowly about $B_1$ at the gentle rate $\omega_1=\gamma B_1$,
  arcing from the north pole down to the south and back. The probability swings back and forth between
  $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$:

$$
P_{\downarrow}(t)=\sin^2\frac{\omega_1 t}{2}\quad(\text{on resonance})
$$

This swing is the **Rabi oscillation**. Time the shutdown of the small field precisely and you can park the spin
anywhere you like: run for $t=\pi/\omega_1$ (a "$\pi$ pulse") and the flip is complete; run half as long
(a "$\pi/2$ pulse") and the spin lies flat on the equator — the key move NMR uses below.

<Callout type="insight" title="Every two-level system is a spin in disguise">
Nothing in this section's derivations truly used the physics of a "magnetic moment" — only:
two levels, an energy gap $\hbar\omega_0$, and an oscillating coupling.
So the conclusions apply to **every** two-level system: two atomic levels driven by a laser,
two current states of a superconducting circuit driven by microwaves, two internal states of a trapped ion…
In the language of quantum computing, Larmor precession on the Bloch sphere is a Z-rotation gate
and a Rabi pulse is an X/Y-rotation gate — "running magnetic resonance" and "running quantum gates"
are mathematically the same activity.
Spin 1/2 is not just a property of particles; it is the universal grammar of two-level physics.
</Callout>

## NMR and MRI: turning this section into a machine

The workflow of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), in the language of this section, takes three sentences:

1. **Polarise.** Put the sample in a strong field $B_0$. In thermal equilibrium slightly more spins point up
   than down (the lower-energy state is a touch more populated — only about ten parts per million at room
   temperature and 3 T, but with $10^{23}$ nuclei in the sample the net magnetisation is plenty),
   giving the sample a net magnetic moment along z.
2. **Flip.** Fire a radio-frequency $\pi/2$ pulse tuned to $\omega_0=\gamma B_0$,
   laying the net moment flat into the xy plane.
3. **Listen.** The flattened moment precesses at the Larmor frequency like a spinning bar magnet,
   inducing an oscillating voltage at $\omega_0$ in a pickup coil. Measure that signal's frequency and decay.

Why is it worth listening to? Because the $B$ in $\omega_0=\gamma B$ is **the actual field at the nucleus**,
slightly shielded by the surrounding electron cloud — the same nucleus in different chemical environments
shifts in frequency by a few parts per million (the "chemical shift"). From one spectrum a chemist reads off
molecular structure; that is NMR spectroscopy.

MRI runs the trick in reverse: deliberately make $B_0$ a gradient field that varies with position,
so that **frequency becomes a barcode for location** — receive a signal at 127.68 MHz and you know it came
from the slice of tissue where the field matches that frequency. Add the fact that signals decay at different
rates in different tissues (water and fat have different relaxation times), and a three-dimensional image
of soft tissue can be reconstructed.
The hammering noise you hear inside an MRI machine is the gradient coils rapidly switching that "barcode".

<Callout type="warning" title="What precesses is the expectation value, not 'the arrow'">
"The spin vector circles the field" describes the motion of the **expectation value** $\langle\vec S\rangle$.
Measure any component along any direction at any moment and you still get only the two outcomes $\pm\tfrac\hbar2$ —
every conclusion of the SG experiment stands intact. What goes round is the centre of gravity of the probabilities,
not a physically existing arrow.
(The coil in NMR really does hear a smooth sine wave, but that is the collective average of $10^{23}$ spins —
a single spin still answers only "up" or "down".)
</Callout>

## What comes next

Up to now we have been playing with a **single** spin. But in real systems angular momenta rarely come alone:
the electron in hydrogen has both orbital angular momentum $\hat{\vec L}$ and spin $\hat{\vec S}$;
helium has the spins of two electrons; protons and neutrons assemble into nuclei.

Put two angular momenta side by side — what is the total? The classical answer, "add the vectors",
runs into immediate trouble in quantum mechanics: not even one vector's three components can be simultaneously sharp,
so what exactly would you add? The next section gives the correct addition, and its answer will also explain
"singlet" and "triplet" — words that will keep returning in chemical bonds and entangled states.
