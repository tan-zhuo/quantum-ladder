The previous section left two loose threads: the algebra allows half-integer angular momentum but wavefunctions
on the sphere cannot house it; and in one experiment a beam of silver atoms split into exactly two.
This section ties the threads together.

First, the experimental evidence that forced spin into existence. By 1925, spectroscopy had piled up
a stack of debts that "orbits + integer $l$" could not pay:

- **Spectral lines come in pairs.** Under a spectrometer, sodium's yellow D line is actually **two** lines,
  at wavelengths 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm, split by a mere 0.002 eV. Hydrogen's lines show the same "doublet" fine structure.
  It was as if something unknown was gently snapping every level in two.
- **The anomalous Zeeman effect.** Lines in a magnetic field often split into an even number of components.
  But we just counted in the previous section: orbital angular momentum gives $2l+1$ lines with integer $l$,
  and that number is **always odd**. An even count means some angular momentum has an even $2j+1$ —
  and $j$ must be a half-integer.

Goudsmit and Uhlenbeck (both graduate students at the time) proposed that the electron itself carries
an angular momentum $s=\tfrac12$, along with a magnetic moment to match. They named it spin.

## First, defuse the name: the electron is not a little top

"Spin" is a historically misleading name. It suggests the electron is a little ball rotating about its own axis.
**That picture is wrong**, and wrong in a way you can demonstrate quantitatively:

<Callout type="warning" title="The quantitative bankruptcy of the spinning-ball picture">
Even granting the electron the largest radius imaginable — the classical electron radius
$r_e\approx2.8\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ (any larger contradicts scattering experiments) —
for this uniform ball to accumulate $\hbar/2$ of angular momentum by rotation,
solving $L=\tfrac25 m r^2\omega=\hbar/2$ for the equatorial speed gives

$$
v=\omega r=\frac{5\hbar}{4m_e r_e}\approx5.1\times10^{10}\ \text{m/s}\approx170\,c
$$

One hundred and seventy times the speed of light. And in reality the electron is a **point particle**
to within experimental precision (smaller $r$, even larger $v$).
Conclusion: spin angular momentum comes from no rotation in space whatsoever.
</Callout>

Then what is spin? This is exactly where the reversal at the end of
[section 5.1](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/01-orbital-angular-momentum/) earns its keep:
**angular momentum is defined by the commutation relations, not by $\vec r\times\vec p$**.
Spin is three Hermitian operators $\hat S_x,\hat S_y,\hat S_z$ satisfying

$$
[\hat S_x,\hat S_y]=\ii\hbar\hat S_z\ \text{and its cyclic partners}
$$

Nothing more. It is attached to no position wavefunction, so the single-valuedness argument of
[section 5.2](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/02-spherical-harmonics/) — "one full turn must return the original value" —
has no grip on it: the half-integer seats stand open. The electron occupies the smallest non-trivial one:
$s=\tfrac12$, with eigenvalues $\hat S^2=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32\hbar^2=\tfrac34\hbar^2$ and
$\hat S_z=\pm\tfrac\hbar2$ — just $2s+1=2$ states.

Spin is an **intrinsic property** of the electron, on the same footing as charge and mass: you cannot
"slow down" or "speed up" the electron's spin. $s=\tfrac12$ is a factory setting; all that can change
is how its components along various directions come out.

## A two-dimensional space and the Pauli matrices

A system with only two basis vectors is the smallest stage quantum mechanics can offer. Write

$$
\ket{\uparrow}=\ket{s=\tfrac12,m=+\tfrac12}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\ket{\downarrow}=\ket{s=\tfrac12,m=-\tfrac12}
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
$$

A general state is a two-component complex vector $\ket{\chi}=a\ket{\uparrow}+b\ket{\downarrow}$ with $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$.
Chapter 3's abstract language — "states are vectors, observables are matrices" — here becomes for the first time
**concrete enough to compute by hand**: every operator is a $2\times2$ matrix.

<Derivation title="Building the Pauli matrices from the ladder-operator matrix elements" level="基础" minutes={8} open={true}>

**Step 1: $\hat S_z$ comes for free.** The basis vectors are its eigenstates; put the eigenvalues on the diagonal:

$$
\hat S_z=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 2: write $\hat S_\pm$ with last section's general formula.** The formula
$\hat S_\pm\ket{s,m}=\hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)-m(m\pm1)}\ket{s,m\pm1}$
has only one non-zero matrix element when $s=\tfrac12$. For $\hat S_+$: acting on
$\ket{\downarrow}$ ($m=-\tfrac12$) gives the coefficient
$\hbar\sqrt{\tfrac34-(-\tfrac12)(\tfrac12)}=\hbar\sqrt{\tfrac34+\tfrac14}=\hbar$;
acting on $\ket{\uparrow}$ hits the top of the ladder and annihilates. So

$$
\hat S_+=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat S_-=\hat S_+^\dagger=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 3: solve back for $\hat S_x,\hat S_y$.** Inverting the definition $\hat S_\pm=\hat S_x\pm\ii\hat S_y$:

$$
\hat S_x=\frac{\hat S_++\hat S_-}{2}=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\hat S_y=\frac{\hat S_+-\hat S_-}{2\ii}=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&-\ii\\ \ii&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Step 4: factor out the common piece.** All three matrices carry $\hbar/2$; give the remaining skeletons their own names:

$$
\hat{\vec S}=\frac\hbar2\vec\sigma,\qquad
\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\ii\\ \ii&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

These are the **Pauli matrices**.

**Step 5: inspection.** Direct multiplication verifies $[\hat S_x,\hat S_y]=\ii\hbar\hat S_z$
(i.e. $\sigma_x\sigma_y=\ii\sigma_z$), and

$$
\hat S^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}(\sigma_x^2+\sigma_y^2+\sigma_z^2)
=\frac{3\hbar^2}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

— the identity matrix times $\tfrac34\hbar^2$: **every** two-component state is an eigenstate of $\hat S^2$,
with eigenvalue exactly $s(s+1)\hbar^2$ at $s=\tfrac12$. The algebra is fully self-consistent.

</Derivation>

The Pauli algebra is useful enough to memorise outright. Each matrix squares to the identity,
any two distinct ones anticommute, and both facts fold into one master product formula:

$$
\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}\,\mathbb{1}+\ii\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}\,\sigma_k
$$

($\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the cyclic symbol: $+1$ for the order $xyz$ and its cycles, $-1$ for the reverse, 0 with any repeated index.)
An immediate corollary: the spin along any direction $\hat n$, $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$, satisfies
$(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2=\mathbb{1}$, so its eigenvalues can only be $\pm1$, i.e.
$\hat S_n=\pm\tfrac\hbar2$. **No matter which direction you measure along, the answer is always just "up" or "down",
always of size $\hbar/2$.** The next section will watch this play out in the laboratory again and again.

## Spin states along an arbitrary direction

The state "up" along $\hat n=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$
(the eigenstate of $\hat n\cdot\hat{\vec S}$ with eigenvalue $+\tfrac\hbar2$) works out to be:

$$
\ket{+\hat n}=\cos\frac\theta2\,\ket{\uparrow}+\ee^{\ii\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\,\ket{\downarrow}
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: one sphere holds every spin state.**

After normalisation, and with the overall phase irrelevant, a two-component complex vector has two real parameters left —
exactly the two angles $(\theta,\phi)$ on a sphere. Every spin state is a point on this sphere, called the **Bloch sphere**:
the north pole is $\ket{\uparrow}$, the south pole is $\ket{\downarrow}$,
and the equator carries the various "sideways" superpositions.

Check the least familiar case, the equator: $\theta=\tfrac\pi2,\phi=0$ gives
$(\ket{\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow})/\sqrt2$ — which is an eigenstate of $\hat S_x$.
"Up along x" is nothing new: it is just the equal-amplitude superposition in the z basis.

A qubit in quantum computing is precisely a spin 1/2 (or any two-level system),
and a single-qubit gate is a rotation of this point on the Bloch sphere.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Formula: why θ/2.**

Note that the state contains the **half-angle** $\theta/2$, not $\theta$. This is no typo:

$$
\theta=\pi\ \text{(opposite direction)}\Longrightarrow
\ket{-\hat z}=\ket{\downarrow}
$$

Turn $180°$ in space and the state vector turns only "half-way"; after a full $360°$ in space,

$$
\cos\pi\,\ket{\uparrow}+\dots=-\ket{\uparrow}
$$

the state vector has **changed sign**! It takes $720°$ to truly come back to itself.
This is exactly the old half-integer disease of "one turn flips the sign" — a death sentence
for a spatial wavefunction (section 5.2), but a spin state does not live in space,
so the sign flip is a mere overall phase: acquitted and released.
(Yet let two spin beams interfere coherently and that minus sign becomes genuinely measurable —
the neutron interferometry experiments of 1975 did exactly that.)

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Even 'up' spin does not point fully up">
The name $\ket{\uparrow}$ oversells itself. Its $S_z=\tfrac\hbar2$,
but its total length is $|\vec S|=\sqrt{s(s+1)}\hbar=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\hbar\approx0.87\hbar$ —
considerably more than $\tfrac\hbar2$. The angle between the "spin vector" and the z axis is

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\hbar/2}{\sqrt3\hbar/2}=\frac1{\sqrt3}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\theta\approx54.7^\circ
$$

Even in its most "up" state, the spin leans at $54.7°$ — the fluctuations of the transverse
components $\hat S_x,\hat S_y$ are pinned there by the uncertainty relation and cannot be squeezed away.
This is section 5.1's cone picture in its most extreme, minimal-system form: the $s=\tfrac12$ cone opens widest.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Spin and position are separate ledgers">
The full electron state is "the position ledger" times "the spin ledger":
$\Psi(\vec r)\,\ket{\chi}$, one two-component object (a spinor).
The Schrödinger equation governs the former; with no magnetic field, spin is a quiet bystander.
When do the two ledgers couple? The moment a magnetic field switches on —
that is the story of sections 5.5 and 5.7.
</Callout>

## What comes next

We now hold all the mathematics of spin: a two-dimensional space, three matrices,
and only "up" or "down" as answers along any direction.

But these conclusions sound frankly bizarre — exactly $\pm\hbar/2$ from any direction,
with not a single value in between? Time to go back to that Frankfurt laboratory in 1922.
One magnet, one beam of silver atoms, one glass plate:
the apparatus not only demonstrates every claim above directly, it turns chapter 3's measurement postulate
into something you can see. The next section is devoted entirely to this one experiment.
