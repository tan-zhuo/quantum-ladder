Every problem we have solved so far has been one-dimensional: a particle moving along a line.
But real atoms are three-dimensional — the electron orbits the nucleus.
And the moment anything "goes around", classical mechanics hands us a leading character: **angular momentum**.

First, why does it matter classically? A planet around the Sun, an electron around a nucleus —
these are central-force problems: the force always points at the centre. In that situation
$\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ is conserved — its direction locks the orbital plane in place,
and its magnitude sets how "fat" or "thin" the orbit is. Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times)
is nothing but angular momentum conservation in disguise.

Meanwhile, experiment had long been hinting that angular momentum in atoms is **not continuous**.
Put a glowing atom in a magnetic field and its spectral lines split into a finite number of components
(the Zeeman effect), as if angular momentum could only point in a few discrete directions in space.
The Bohr model simply postulated $L=n\hbar$ — the specific values later turned out to be wrong,
but the intuition of "quantised angular momentum" was pointing exactly the right way.

This chapter answers the questions: what does angular momentum actually look like in quantum mechanics,
which values can it take, and why does it drag in something with no classical counterpart whatsoever — spin.

## From classical quantity to operator

The [postulates](/en/ch/03-formalism/07-postulates/) of chapter 3 give us the recipe for building operators:
take the classical expression and replace $x$ and $p$ by operators. For angular momentum:

$$
\hat{\vec L}=\hat{\vec r}\times\hat{\vec p}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\begin{aligned}
\hat L_x&=\hat y\hat p_z-\hat z\hat p_y\\
\hat L_y&=\hat z\hat p_x-\hat x\hat p_z\\
\hat L_z&=\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x
\end{aligned}
$$

The three formulas are really one formula: cycle $x\to y\to z\to x$ and each line turns into the next.

<Callout type="note" title="No ordering trap here">
Translating a classical quantity into an operator usually raises the ordering worry: $xp$ and $px$ are not the same thing.
But every pair that appears in $\hat L_z=\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x$ is a coordinate and a momentum along **different directions** —
$\hat x$ commutes with $\hat p_y$ ($[\hat x,\hat p_y]=0$, see [section 3.6](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/)),
so any ordering gives the same operator and the translation is unambiguous. All three components also come out automatically Hermitian.
</Callout>

## The key calculation: do the components commute?

The lesson of [section 3.6](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/) was that whether two observables
can be simultaneously sharp is decided entirely by their commutator. So the first thing to do
with three new operators is to compute their commutators.

<Derivation title="Computing [L̂ₓ, L̂ᵧ]" level="基础" minutes={6} open={true}>

**Goal**: $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=[\hat y\hat p_z-\hat z\hat p_y,\;\hat z\hat p_x-\hat x\hat p_z]$.

**Step 1: expand into four terms.** The commutator is linear in both slots, so

$$
[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]
=[\hat y\hat p_z,\hat z\hat p_x]
-[\hat y\hat p_z,\hat x\hat p_z]
-[\hat z\hat p_y,\hat z\hat p_x]
+[\hat z\hat p_y,\hat x\hat p_z]
$$

**Step 2: throw away the trivial terms first.** The operators appearing in the second term are $\hat y,\hat p_z,\hat x$ —
every pair among them commutes (coordinates and momenta along different directions leave each other alone), so the whole term vanishes.
The third term contains $\hat z,\hat p_y,\hat p_x$ and dies for the same reason. Only the first and fourth terms survive.

**Step 3: the first term.** Grinding through the product rule for $[\hat A\hat B,\hat C\hat D]$ would be tedious.
Better to notice that the only pair with any "spark" here is $\hat p_z$ and $\hat z$ ($[\hat z,\hat p_z]=\ii\hbar$);
every other operator is a bystander. Pull the bystanders out front:

$$
[\hat y\hat p_z,\hat z\hat p_x]=\hat y\,[\hat p_z,\hat z]\,\hat p_x
=\hat y(-\ii\hbar)\hat p_x=-\ii\hbar\,\hat y\hat p_x
$$

(Using $[\hat p_z,\hat z]=-[\hat z,\hat p_z]=-\ii\hbar$.)

**Step 4: the fourth term.** Here the sparking pair is $\hat z$ and $\hat p_z$:

$$
[\hat z\hat p_y,\hat x\hat p_z]=\hat p_y\,[\hat z,\hat p_z]\,\hat x
=\ii\hbar\,\hat x\hat p_y
$$

**Step 5: put it together.**

$$
[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=-\ii\hbar\,\hat y\hat p_x+\ii\hbar\,\hat x\hat p_y
=\ii\hbar(\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x)=\ii\hbar\hat L_z
$$

Cycling $x\to y\to z\to x$ immediately yields the other two. All three together:

$$
[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z,\qquad
[\hat L_y,\hat L_z]=\ii\hbar\hat L_x,\qquad
[\hat L_z,\hat L_x]=\ii\hbar\hat L_y
$$

</Derivation>

The result is not zero. That one small line of algebra has enormous consequences.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: the angular momentum vector cannot be pinned down.**

Classical angular momentum is an honest arrow: all three components have definite values at once, and the arrow points in a definite direction.

Quantum angular momentum cannot manage that. $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z\neq0$ says that
$L_x$ and $L_y$ generally **cannot both be sharp**: nail down $L_z$,
and $L_x$ and $L_y$ dissolve into a cloud of probabilities.

The right picture is not an arrow but **a cone**: $L_z$ is definite (the cone's height is fixed),
the length $|\vec L|$ is definite (the cone's slant is fixed), but on the surface of the cone
the arrow is "everywhere and nowhere".

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Formula: a direct application of the generalised uncertainty relation.**

Apply the generalised uncertainty relation of [section 3.6](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/),
$\sigma_A\sigma_B\ge\frac12|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|$, to this pair:

$$
\sigma_{L_x}\sigma_{L_y}\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\langle\hat L_z\rangle\right|
$$

Note that the right-hand side is an **expectation value**, not a constant: unlike $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$,
this bound changes from state to state. In a state with $\langle\hat L_z\rangle=0$,
$L_x$ and $L_y$ are allowed to vanish simultaneously — for instance a spherically symmetric state with zero angular momentum altogether.

</div>
</DualView>

## Is anything simultaneously sharp?

The three components fight each other — so is there any quantity that lives at peace with them?
There is: the **squared magnitude** of the angular momentum,

$$
\hat L^2\equiv\hat L_x^2+\hat L_y^2+\hat L_z^2
$$

<Derivation title="Verifying [L², L̂_z] = 0" level="基础" minutes={4}>

Work term by term. First a small general-purpose tool (the product rule):

$$
[\hat A^2,\hat B]=\hat A[\hat A,\hat B]+[\hat A,\hat B]\hat A
$$

**First term** $[\hat L_x^2,\hat L_z]$: using $[\hat L_x,\hat L_z]=-\ii\hbar\hat L_y$,

$$
[\hat L_x^2,\hat L_z]=\hat L_x(-\ii\hbar\hat L_y)+(-\ii\hbar\hat L_y)\hat L_x
=-\ii\hbar(\hat L_x\hat L_y+\hat L_y\hat L_x)
$$

**Second term** $[\hat L_y^2,\hat L_z]$: using $[\hat L_y,\hat L_z]=\ii\hbar\hat L_x$,

$$
[\hat L_y^2,\hat L_z]=\ii\hbar(\hat L_y\hat L_x+\hat L_x\hat L_y)
$$

**Third term** $[\hat L_z^2,\hat L_z]=0$ (any operator commutes with its own powers).

Add the three: the first two are **equal and opposite and cancel exactly**:

$$
[\hat L^2,\hat L_z]=0
$$

By symmetry (the three directions enter on an equal footing), $\hat L^2$ commutes with $\hat L_x$ and $\hat L_y$ as well.

</Derivation>

So the largest combination quantum mechanics lets us fix simultaneously is: **the length plus one component**.
The conventional choice is $(\hat L^2,\hat L_z)$ — there is nothing physically special about $z$; it is pure notational tradition
(in a real experiment, "the $z$ direction" is simply wherever your magnetic field points).
This is the algebraic origin of the cone picture: the slant of the cone ($|\vec L|$) and its height ($L_z$)
can be fixed together; the azimuthal angle cannot.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't take the cone picture literally">
"The vector sweeps around the cone" is a convenient metaphor, but do not over-trust it:
there is no "real arrow" whizzing around the surface.
In a joint eigenstate of $(\hat L^2,\hat L_z)$, $L_x$ and $L_y$ simply **have no values** (except for $l=0$, where all three components are zero) —
measuring either gives a random outcome whose average happens to be zero.
The cone depicts "all directions consistent with what is known", not a trajectory.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="The commutation relations themselves define angular momentum">
Look back over this section: the only place we actually used "$\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$" was to derive the commutation relations.
Everything after that — the uncertainty relation, the cone picture, the $(\hat L^2,\hat L_z)$ partnership — depends only on

$$
[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z\ \text{and its cyclic partners}
$$

That suggests a bold reversal: **take the commutation relations themselves as the definition of angular momentum**.
Any three Hermitian operators satisfying this algebra count as an angular momentum —
whether or not they have the form $\vec r\times\vec p$.

This reversal is not a formal game. Later in this chapter we will meet an angular momentum (spin)
that has **no** $\vec r\times\vec p$ about it at all, yet satisfies the algebra perfectly.
Putting the definition on the algebra is precisely how we reserve a seat for it.
</Callout>

## Why the commutator can determine the spectrum

The natural next question: what are the eigenvalues of $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_z$?

The chapter-2 approach would be to write down a differential equation and solve it. But here there is an astonishing shortcut:
**from the commutation relations alone, without solving a single differential equation, every eigenvalue can be pinned down.**

We have already seen the seed of the idea: in the algebraic solution of the harmonic oscillator, the single
commutation relation $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$ forces the levels into an evenly spaced ladder of $\hbar\omega$ —
the commutator sets the rung spacing, positivity sets the bottom, and the spectrum is locked in.
The angular momentum algebra is more intricate (the right-hand side is an operator, not a constant), but the same strategy works:
build "ladder operators" $\hat L_x\pm\ii\hat L_y$ that climb the $L_z$ eigenvalues one rung at a time,
then use the finiteness of $\hat L^2$ to cap the ladder at both ends.

## What comes next

We have placed our bet on the commutation relations: the claim is that

$$
[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z\ \text{and its cyclic partners}
$$

alone determine every eigenvalue of $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_z$.

The next section makes good on that promise — climbing out the entire spectrum rung by rung with ladder operators.
And the answer hides a surprise: the algebra allows **half again as many** eigenvalues as orbital motion actually uses.
That extra half is the seat reserved for spin.
