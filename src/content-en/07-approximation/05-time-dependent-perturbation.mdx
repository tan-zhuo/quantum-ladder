Up to now, every Hamiltonian in this chapter has been independent of time. That makes for an excessively quiet world:
as [section 2.5](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/05-stationary-states/) told us, a stationary state only spins its phase in place,
its probability distribution frozen solid. An electron that moves into $\ket{2p}$ ought to live there forever.

Reality objects at once: put sodium vapour in white light and it precisely devours the two yellow lines near 589 nm —
not a bit more, not a bit less; and a sodium lamp, conversely, glows with the very same pair of yellow lines.
The atom **jumps back and forth** between two levels, and it is fussy about the light's frequency to a punishing degree.

Where is the contradiction? In "the Hamiltonian does not depend on time". Light is an electromagnetic wave; when it strikes the atom,
the electron feels an electric field oscillating in time, and the Hamiltonian picks up a time-dependent piece:

$$
\hat H(t)=\hat H_0+\hat V(t),
\qquad \hat V(t)=e\mathcal E z\cos\omega t
$$

$\hat H_0$ is the isolated atom (whose levels $E_n$ and eigenstates $\ket{n}$ we have already solved),
and $\hat V(t)$ is the light field's shoving. The moment the Hamiltonian depends on time, the very concept of an "energy eigenstate" loses its footing —
there are no stationary states to solve for, and every static method of the last four sections is out of a job. We must return to the equation of motion itself.

## Moving the problem into the coefficients

The idea: the eigenstates of $\hat H_0$ still form a complete basis at every instant, so any state can be expanded.
We cannot solve the time-dependent Schrödinger equation outright, but we can squeeze all the time dependence into the **expansion coefficients**:

$$
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_n c_n(t)\,\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\,\ket{n}
$$

Note that we write the "boring" phase $\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$ out explicitly — with no perturbation it is the whole of the evolution,
and the $c_n$ are constants. This bookkeeping is precisely the **interaction picture** of
[section 3.9](/en/ch/03-formalism/09-time-evolution-pictures/): let $\hat H_0$'s evolution spin its own phases, while $c_n(t)$ records only **the changes caused by the perturbation**.
$|c_n(t)|^2$ is then the probability of finding the system in $\ket{n}$ at time $t$.

<Derivation title="The coefficient equations and the first-order transition amplitude" level="基础" minutes={10} open={true}>

**Step 1: substitute into the Schrödinger equation.**

Insert the expansion into $\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\ket{\Psi}=(\hat H_0+\hat V)\ket{\Psi}$.
Differentiating the left side in $t$ produces two kinds of terms: $\dot c_n$ terms and $E_nc_n$ terms;
the latter cancel term by term against the $\hat H_0$ contribution on the right — exactly the payoff for writing the phase factor explicitly. What remains is

$$
\ii\hbar\sum_n\dot c_n\,\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}
=\sum_n c_n\,\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\,\hat V(t)\ket{n}
$$

**Step 2: project.**

Multiply from the left by $\bra{f}$ and use orthogonality:

$$
\dot c_f=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\sum_n c_n(t)\,V_{fn}(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{fn}t},
\qquad
V_{fn}\equiv\bra{f}\hat V(t)\ket{n},\quad
\omega_{fn}\equiv\frac{E_f-E_n}{\hbar}
$$

Everything so far is **still exact** — we have merely rewritten one partial differential equation as infinitely many coupled ordinary ones.
The trouble has not vanished, only changed address: the rate of change of each coefficient depends on all the others.

**Step 3: the first-order approximation.**

Suppose at $t=0$ the system is in the initial state $\ket{i}$: $c_i(0)=1$, all others zero. For a weak perturbation and not too long a time,
the coefficients have not had time to run far: in the sum on the right keep $c_i\approx1$ and set the rest to zero —
**replace the evolved values by the initial values**, the same spirit as time-independent perturbation theory's "average with the old wavefunction". Hence

$$
c_f(t)\approx-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int_0^t V_{fi}(t')\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{fi}t'}\,\dd t'
\qquad(f\neq i)
$$

**The first-order transition amplitude: the perturbation matrix element, dressed with a phase factor, integrated over history.** This one line is the master plan for this section and the next.

**How to read it**: the integral is exactly the Fourier transform of $V_{fi}(t')$ evaluated at the frequency $\omega_{fi}$.
In other words, **the system uses the transition frequency as its radio tuning dial and listens to only that one station in the perturbation's spectrum**.
If the perturbation contains a component at frequency $\omega_{fi}$, the transition happens; if not, the system barely stirs.
The atom's fussiness about colour — laid bare in a single line.

</Derivation>

## Periodic perturbations and resonance

Insert the light field: $\hat V(t)=\hat V\cos\omega t=\frac{\hat V}{2}(\ee^{\ii\omega t}+\ee^{-\ii\omega t})$.
The integral is elementary:

$$
c_f(t)=-\frac{\ii V_{fi}}{2\hbar}
\left[\frac{\ee^{\ii(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t}-1}{\ii(\omega_{fi}+\omega)}
+\frac{\ee^{\ii(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t}-1}{\ii(\omega_{fi}-\omega)}\right]
$$

The two denominators become small at $\omega=-\omega_{fi}$ and $\omega=+\omega_{fi}$ respectively.
Take $E_f$ above $E_i$ (absorption); then near $\omega\approx\omega_{fi}$ the second term stands alone,
and we drop the first (the **rotating-wave approximation**: the non-resonant term oscillates rapidly and averages to zero). Take the modulus squared:

$$
P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,
\frac{\sin^2\!\left[(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t/2\right]}{(\omega_{fi}-\omega)^2}
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Pushing a swing.** Think of $\omega_{fi}$ as the swing's natural frequency, and the perturbation as your pushing.
Push in rhythm with the swing ($\omega=\omega_{fi}$) and every shove does positive work — the amplitude grows steadily;
get the rhythm slightly off, and the pushes help one moment and sabotage the next, cancelling each other once enough has accumulated —
the greater the detuning, the sooner the cancellation, and the swing never gets high.

Plotted against $\omega$, the transition probability is a sharp peak: centred at $\omega_{fi}$, ringing and decaying on both sides.
The peak's **width narrows with time** (roughly $2\pi/t$): the longer the illumination, the pickier the atom about frequency.
This is the energy–time uncertainty relation showing its concrete face in the transition problem —
for an interaction lasting a time $t$, the energy match need only be good to $\hbar/t$.

</div>
<div slot="right">

At fixed $t$, as a function of the detuning $\Delta\equiv\omega_{fi}-\omega$:

$$
P(\Delta)=\frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{t^2}{4}\,
\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)
$$

- Peak value ($\Delta=0$): $P=\dfrac{|V_{fi}|^2t^2}{4\hbar^2}$, growing as $t^2$;
- First zero: $\Delta=2\pi/t$, main-peak width $\sim2\pi/t$;
- Peak height $\times$ peak width $\propto t$: the total "amount" of transition grows linearly in time — the seed that sprouts into a rate next section.

</div>
</DualView>

**Plug in numbers**: sodium's $3s\to3p$ transition sits at 589 nm,
$\omega_{fi}=2\pi c/\lambda\approx3.2\times10^{15}\ \text{rad/s}$ (photon energy 2.1 eV).
If the atom interacts with the light for $1\ \mu\text{s}$ (flying through a laser beam, say),
the main-peak width is $2\pi/t\approx6\times10^6\ \text{rad/s}$ —
a relative width of order $10^{-9}$. Atoms really are punishing listeners;
atomic clocks push this punishing taste to the limit: the longer the interaction time, the more precisely the frequency is recognised.

<Callout type="warning" title="The first-order formula's expiry date">
At resonance $P\propto t^2$, and left to grow it will sooner or later **exceed 1** — a probability above 1 is of course absurd.
The absurdity is not quantum mechanics but our approximation: the derivation replaced the true $c_i(t)$ by $c_i\approx1$,
and once the initial state is visibly drained, that step no longer stands. **The first-order result is valid only while $P\ll1$.**

At resonance the full answer can in fact be found exactly (ignore every state except the two levels — the two-state problem is solvable):

$$
P_{i\to f}(t)=\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\Omega t}{2}\right),\qquad
\Omega=\frac{|V_{fi}|}{\hbar}
$$

The probability **oscillates back and forth** between 0 and 1 — Rabi oscillations, the physical basis of laser control, nuclear magnetic resonance, and the single-qubit gates of quantum computers. First-order perturbation theory is exactly its parabolic opening at $\Omega t\ll1$:
$\sin^2(\Omega t/2)\approx\Omega^2t^2/4$, dovetailing perfectly with the peak formula above.
So the criterion can be stated concretely: use first order for $t\ll1/\Omega$; at $t\sim1/\Omega$ you must use Rabi.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Stimulated emission: the other half of the same formula">
Look back at the term we threw away: it resonates at $\omega=-\omega_{fi}$, i.e. at $E_f=E_i-\hbar\omega$ —
the system **gives up** an energy $\hbar\omega$ and drops to the lower level, and it is the incoming light that "coaxes" it down.
This is **stimulated emission**, the process Einstein predicted in 1917 from a thermal-equilibrium argument, and the working principle of the laser.
Time-dependent perturbation theory hands out absorption and stimulated emission even-handedly — same matrix element, same rate —
and that symmetry is precisely the microscopic origin of the equality of Einstein's B coefficients.
</Callout>

## What comes next

For a **single** discrete final state, the first-order formula predicts either oscillation or $t^2$ growth —
yet what experiments actually measure is different behaviour altogether: excited atoms decay at a constant **rate**,
their number falling off as $\ee^{-t/\tau}$, with each 2p hydrogen atom living 1.6 nanoseconds on average.
Where does a constant rate come from? The key is that when an atom emits light, the photon may fly in any direction with any polarisation —
the final state is not one state but **a whole continuum**. Sum the sinc² peak over that continuum,
and the oscillations vanish as if by magic, leaving one clean rate formula: Fermi's golden rule.
