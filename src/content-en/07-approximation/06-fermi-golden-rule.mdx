The last section left a crack. For a single final state, first-order theory gives a probability that either grows madly as $t^2$ (on resonance)
or oscillates back and forth (off resonance). Yet excited atoms in the laboratory wear an entirely different face:
a population of hydrogen atoms in the 2p state loses a fixed **fraction** every nanosecond, its number sliding smoothly down as $\ee^{-t/\tau}$ with
$\tau=1.6\ \text{ns}$ — no oscillation, no acceleration. "A fixed fraction per unit time" means there exists a
constant **transition rate** $\Gamma$. Where does this constant rate spring from?

The difference hides in the nature of the final states. Last section we tacitly assumed the transition's destination was **one** discrete state;
but when an atom emits light, the destination is "the atom drops to 1s **plus** a photon flies away" —
and the photon can fly in any direction, carry either of two polarisations, and take any frequency within the resonance width.
**The final state is not a point; it is a continuum.** When we measure "has the atom decayed yet?", we do not care
which way the photon went, so the probability must be summed over the whole continuum. It is this summation that flattens the oscillations into a rate.

## Counting the final states: the density of states

To sum over a continuum, we first need to answer "how many states are there per unit energy interval near $E$?"
That quantity is the **density of states**, written $\rho(E)$: there are
$\rho(E)\,\dd E$ states with energy between $E$ and $E+\dd E$. It is a pure counting problem, with no dynamics in it.

The most common example: a particle (or photon) placed in a large box of volume $V=L^3$,
with momentum quantised onto the lattice $\vec k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y,n_z)$.
Each state occupies a volume $(2\pi/L)^3$ of $k$-space; count the lattice points in a spherical shell of radius $k$,
convert to energy, and out comes $\rho(E)$. For photons ($E=\hbar ck$, two polarisations):

$$
\rho_{\text{photon}}(E)=\frac{V E^2}{\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}
$$

The box is only scaffolding: in any physical answer, $V$ always cancels against the $1/V$ normalisation factor in the matrix element,
and the final rate is independent of the box size.

## From sinc² to a rate

<Derivation title="Deriving the golden rule" level="基础" minutes={10} open={true}>

**Step 1: sum the single-state probability over the continuum of final states.**

Take the cleanest case first: a perturbation $\hat V$ switched on suddenly at $t=0$ and held constant thereafter
(the periodic perturbation $\hat V\cos\omega t$, after the rotating-wave approximation, is completely isomorphic — just replace
$V_{fi}\to V_{fi}/2$ and the detuning by $\Delta=\omega_{fi}-\omega$).
The last section's first-order amplitude integral gives (writing $\Delta\equiv\omega_{fi}$):

$$
P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,t^2\,
\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right),
\qquad \mathrm{sinc}\,x\equiv\frac{\sin x}{x}
$$

The total transition probability is the sum over all final states; use the density of states to write it as an integral ($\dd E_f=\hbar\,\dd\Delta$):

$$
P(t)=\int P_{i\to f}(t)\,\rho(E_f)\,\dd E_f
=\frac{t^2}{\hbar}\int|V_{fi}|^2\rho\,
\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\dd\Delta
$$

**Step 2: see what sinc² looks like at long times.**

As a function of $\Delta$, $\mathrm{sinc}^2(\Delta t/2)$ has a main peak of height 1 and width about $4\pi/t$.
The larger $t$, the narrower the peak — and once it is narrower than any scale over which $|V_{fi}|^2\rho$ varies with energy,
the factor $|V_{fi}|^2\rho$ is essentially constant across the peak and can be pulled out at its central value:

$$
P(t)\approx\frac{t^2}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\rho\,\Big|_{\Delta=0}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)\dd\Delta
$$

The remaining integral is the standard result $\int\mathrm{sinc}^2u\,\dd u=\pi$, which after the change of variable equals $2\pi/t$.

**Step 3: the miracle.**

$$
P(t)\approx\frac{t^2}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{t}\cdot|V_{fi}|^2\rho
=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\rho\;t
$$

The peak width's $1/t$ shaves one power off the $t^2$: **the total probability grows linearly in time**.
Linear growth is the very definition of "constant rate", so

$$
\boxed{\ \Gamma=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)\Big|_{E_f=E_i+\hbar\omega}\ }
$$

This is **Fermi's golden rule** (it was actually Dirac who derived it; Fermi called it
"golden rule number two" in his lecture notes, and the name stuck — yet another miscarriage of justice in the history of physics naming).

**What happened, retold in pictures**: the probability for each single final state oscillates,
but final states at different detunings oscillate with different periods. In the sum, the states sitting exactly on the resonance shell grow steadily,
while those slightly off rise and fall out of phase with one another — **summing over the continuum averages the oscillations away**,
leaving only the net outflow through the resonance shell. Mathematically this is the limit

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{t}{2\pi\hbar}\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega)t}{2\hbar}\right)
=\delta(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega)
$$

The sinc² peak sharpens into a δ function: **in the long-time limit energy conservation is strictly enforced**,
which is why the golden rule is often written with an explicit δ function (after integrating over final states the two forms agree).

</Derivation>

<div slot="left">

**Rate = the channel's "bandwidth" times the "traffic".** The golden rule splits the transition rate into two factors:

- $|V_{fi}|^2$ — how tightly the perturbation ties the initial and final states together (how wide each lane is);
- $\rho(E_f)$ — how many lanes there are on the energy-conserving shell.

However large the matrix element, with nowhere to go ($\rho=0$) the rate is zero:
put an excited atom inside the band gap of a photonic crystal, where the photon states at the relevant frequency have been "emptied out",
and spontaneous emission really is suppressed — the density of states is not a bookkeeping device but a piece of physical reality you can engineer.

$$
\Gamma=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|\bra{f}\hat V\ket{i}|^2\,\rho(E_f)
$$

or, equivalently, with the final-state sum written out:

$$
\Gamma=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_f|V_{fi}|^2\,\delta(E_f-E_i-\hbar\omega)
$$

A constant rate yields exponential decay: $\dot N=-\Gamma N\Rightarrow N(t)=N_0\ee^{-\Gamma t}$,
lifetime $\tau=1/\Gamma$, natural linewidth $\hbar\Gamma$ (the energy–time uncertainty relation showing yet another face).

## An order-of-magnitude calculation: why the atom lives 1.6 nanoseconds

The golden rule's most famous client: spontaneous emission. A hydrogen atom falls from 2p back to 1s, releasing a 10.2 eV photon.
The matrix element comes from the electric-dipole interaction ($\hat V\sim e\vec r\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}$,
dipole moment of order $ea_0$); the density of states is the photon formula above. Feed both into the golden rule,
sum over photon directions and polarisations, and the rate tidies up to

$$
\Gamma\approx\frac{4\alpha}{3}\,\frac{\omega^3a_0^2}{c^2}\times|\text{matrix element}/ea_0|^2
$$

($\alpha\approx1/137$ is the fine-structure constant.) The arithmetic: $\omega=1.55\times10^{16}\ \text{rad/s}$,
taking the matrix-element factor as 1 for now:

$$
\Gamma\approx\frac{4}{3\times137}\times\frac{(1.55\times10^{16})^3\times(5.29\times10^{-11})^2}{(3\times10^8)^2}
\approx1.1\times10^{9}\ \text{s}^{-1}
$$

The exact matrix element $|\bra{1s}z\ket{2p_0}|=0.745\,a_0$ squeezes in a further factor of 0.55,
giving $\Gamma=6.3\times10^8\ \text{s}^{-1}$, i.e.

$$
\tau=\frac{1}{\Gamma}\approx1.6\ \text{ns}
$$

in exact agreement with experiment. Take a moment to feel what this number means: the light wave's period is $2\pi/\omega\sim0.4\ \text{fs}$,
so the atom oscillates through **four million periods** before emitting one photon — the coupling of atom to light field (powers of $\alpha$)
is genuinely feeble, which in turn explains why first-order perturbation theory is so trustworthy here.

Push the same rule outward and you get the whole of spectroscopy and scattering physics: photoabsorption cross-sections, beta-decay rates
(Fermi's own use for it), electron–phonon scattering in solids, the resistance of metals —
for any problem where "an initial state flows through a weak coupling into a continuum of final states", step one is always to write down the golden rule.

<Callout type="insight" title="Where does irreversibility come from?">
The Schrödinger equation is reversible, and the transition probability to any single final state really does oscillate back and forth.
But after summing over a continuum of final states, the return paths drift out of phase with one another and never realign —
the irreversibility of exponential decay **is not an added assumption but the statistical consequence of "infinitely many final states"**.
(In cavity quantum electrodynamics, lock the atom into a tiny cavity supporting a single mode: the continuum collapses back to one state,
and the oscillation instantly revives — atom and photon swap the excitation back and forth to the Rabi beat.)
This is a trailer for the open systems of Chapter 10: reversible microscopic laws + a vast environment = apparently irreversible behaviour.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="The golden rule's window in time">
"Constant rate" holds only in an **intermediate stretch of time**, bounded at both ends:

- **Lower bound**: $t$ must be large enough that the sinc² peak width $\sim\hbar/t$ is far smaller than the energy scale over which $|V|^2\rho$ varies,
  or the δ-function approximation is illegitimate. At very early times the decay actually goes as $t^2$ (which underlies the "quantum Zeno effect":
  sufficiently frequent measurement can freeze a decay).
- **Upper bound**: the derivation assumed $\Gamma t\ll1$, the initial state not yet visibly drained.
  Happily, extrapolating the constant rate to $\ee^{-\Gamma t}$ is usually correct (the rate has no memory of history),
  but when $\hbar\Gamma$ approaches the level spacing or the structural scale of the density of states, the exponential law itself starts to warp.

In real systems this window is enormous: for hydrogen it opens at the femtosecond scale and closes at the nanosecond scale — six orders of magnitude —
and that is what makes the golden rule "golden".
</Callout>

## What comes next

Time-dependent perturbation theory handles disturbances that are "fast but weak": the light field oscillates millions of periods before triggering a single transition.
Now twist the dial to the opposite end — what if the disturbance is **extremely slow**? Compress the well gradually, rotate the magnetic field gently:
will the system jump away, or follow along obediently? The answer (the adiabatic theorem) is useful in its own right,
but in 1984 Berry discovered a character in the story that had been overlooked for half a century:
a system carried slowly around a closed loop comes back with a phase imprinted on its wavefunction that depends **only on the geometry of the path**.
This "geometric phase" went on to thread together the Aharonov–Bohm effect and the entire field of topological matter.
