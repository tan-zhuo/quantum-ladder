The light field of the last section oscillates $10^{15}$ times a second — an "extremely fast" disturbance. This section twists the dial the other way:
what happens when the Hamiltonian changes **extremely slowly**?

Start with an experiment within arm's reach. Put a spin-1/2 particle in a magnetic field; its two levels are
the "aligned" and "anti-aligned" states along the field ([section 5.3](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/03-spin-half/)).
Now change the field direction **gently** — sweep it once around a cone, say, taking a few seconds.
What happens to a spin in the "aligned" state?

Intuition says: the field turns far more slowly than the spin precesses, so the spin should trail it like a dog on a leash,
hugging the field direction throughout and coming home after one full turn. That intuition is essentially right — this is the **adiabatic theorem**.
But in 1984 Michael Berry noticed that "coming home" misses one detail:
after the full turn, the wavefunction carries an extra phase — a phase that is neither the dynamical phase of accumulated energy
nor removable by redefining phase conventions. It depends only on **the solid angle the field direction sweeps out on the sphere** —
a purely geometric quantity, indifferent to how slowly you turned or how high the levels sat.

Why should something that is "just a phase" cause a fuss? Because phases interfere.
This geometric phase turned out to have been hiding all along in chemistry (the sign puzzle of molecular wavefunctions),
in optics (polarisation rotation in optical fibres), and in the Aharonov–Bohm effect;
today its generalisations (Berry curvature, Chern numbers) are the grammar of the entire field of topological matter.

## The adiabatic theorem

**Statement**: if the Hamiltonian $\hat H(t)$ changes slowly enough with time, and the system starts in
an instantaneous eigenstate $\ket{n(0)}$ (where $\hat H(t)\ket{n(t)}=E_n(t)\ket{n(t)}$,
and that level never crosses any other), then the system tracks the instantaneous eigenstate $\ket{n(t)}$ throughout,
with vanishing probability of jumping to any other level.

"Slowly enough" compared with what? With the **energy gap**. The quantitative condition is

$$
\frac{\hbar\,\bigl|\bra{m}\partial_t\hat H\ket{n}\bigr|}{(E_n-E_m)^2}\ll1
\qquad\text{for all } m\neq n
$$

The numerator is the Hamiltonian's rate of change (converted to energy per second); the denominator is the gap squared.
How to read it: the system has an intrinsic beat $\hbar/(E_n-E_m)$ (the quantum beat period of neighbouring levels);
as long as the Hamiltonian's change within one beat is far smaller than the gap, the system "does not feel" the change happening
and has time to adjust itself continuously. An analogy: carry a bowl of water and turn around slowly — the surface stays level,
provided the turning period is far longer than the water's sloshing period.

<Callout type="warning" title="The gap is adiabaticity's lifeline">
In the condition the gap enters the denominator **squared**: shrink the gap tenfold and the allowed rate of change must slow a hundredfold.
As the gap tends to zero (a level crossing or near-crossing), no finite speed remains adiabatic —
the system "takes a shortcut" at the crossing and jumps to the other level (a Landau–Zener transition).

This is not a pedantic footnote but a present-day engineering problem: the runtime of adiabatic quantum computing / quantum annealing
is priced by the **smallest gap** along the evolution path, $T\propto1/\Delta_{\min}^2$;
and in chemistry, the near-crossings of electronic states (conical intersections) are photochemistry's most violent channels.
Wherever the adiabatic approximation fails, that is where the physics is liveliest.
</Callout>

## Tracking along — with two phases in tow

The adiabatic theorem says "the system follows $\ket{n(t)}$", but quantum states can differ by a phase. What phase does the tracking carry?

<Derivation title="The phases of adiabatic evolution: dynamical + geometric" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

Let the Hamiltonian depend on time through a set of external parameters $\vec R(t)$ (field direction, internuclear distance, …):
$\hat H(t)=\hat H(\vec R(t))$. Expand the state in instantaneous eigenstates, **peeling off the energy phase in advance**:

$$
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_n c_n(t)\,
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int_0^tE_n(t')\,\dd t'\right]\ket{n(t)}
$$

The exponential factor is the **dynamical phase** $\theta_n=-\frac1\hbar\int E_n\dd t$ —
the natural generalisation of the stationary phase $-E_nt/\hbar$ to slowly varying energies; nothing new there. The news is in the $c_n$.

**Step 1: substitute into the Schrödinger equation.**

Completely parallel to section 7.5, the energy terms cancel term by term — but now **the basis vectors themselves are moving**,
and $\partial_t\ket{n(t)}$ contributes new terms. Projecting onto $\bra{m}$:

$$
\dot c_m=-c_m\braket{m}{\dot m}
-\sum_{n\neq m}c_n\,\frac{\bra{m}\partial_t\hat H\ket{n}}{E_n-E_m}\,
\ee^{\ii(\theta_n-\theta_m)}
$$

(The second line uses the identity
$\bra{m}\dot n\rangle=\bra{m}\partial_t\hat H\ket{n}/(E_n-E_m)$, obtained by differentiating $\hat H\ket{n}=E_n\ket{n}$.)

**Step 2: the adiabatic limit.**

The summed terms have slowly varying numerators multiplying the rapidly oscillating phase difference $\ee^{\ii(\theta_n-\theta_m)}$ —
a slow envelope times a fast oscillation integrates to approximately zero (this is exactly where the adiabatic condition above comes from). Drop them:

$$
\dot c_n=-c_n\braket{n}{\dot n}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
c_n(t)=c_n(0)\,\ee^{\ii\gamma_n(t)},
\qquad
\gamma_n(t)=\ii\int_0^t\braket{n}{\dot n}\,\dd t'
$$

($\braket{n}{\dot n}$ is pure imaginary — differentiate $\braket{n}{n}=1$ to see it — so $\gamma_n$ is real
and $|c_n|$ is constant: the system really does stay on level $n$, and the adiabatic theorem is proved.)

**Step 3: see that it is geometric.**

Use the chain rule to trade the time derivative for a parameter derivative: $\ket{\dot n}=\dot{\vec R}\cdot\nabla_{\vec R}\ket{n}$, so

$$
\gamma_n=\ii\int_{\vec R(0)}^{\vec R(t)}
\braket{n}{\nabla_{\vec R}\,n}\cdot\dd\vec R
$$

**Time has vanished.** The integral runs only along the path through parameter space: take one second or one year to walk it,
$\gamma_n$ comes out identical to the last digit. It is a property of the path, not of the process — hence **geometric phase**.

**Step 4: why it was overlooked for half a century.**

The phase of an instantaneous eigenstate was always our arbitrary convention: change conventions,
$\ket{n}\to\ee^{\ii\chi(\vec R)}\ket{n}$, and $\gamma_n$ shifts by $\chi(\vec R(0))-\chi(\vec R(t))$.
For an **open path** you can always choose the convention to absorb $\gamma_n$ entirely — it is "not physical",
and textbooks threw it away for fifty years on that basis. But for a **closed loop** ($\vec R(T)=\vec R(0)$),
the endpoint corrections from any change of convention cancel, and what remains,

$$
\gamma_n(C)=\ii\oint_C\braket{n}{\nabla_{\vec R}\,n}\cdot\dd\vec R
$$

is **independent of the phase convention** — a measurable physical quantity. This is the **Berry phase**.
Berry's contribution was not the integral itself, but seeing that "on a closed loop it cannot be dodged".

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The misalignment angle of parallel transport.** On a globe, carry an arrow around a closed route "translating it without ever rotating it":
start at the north pole, go down a meridian to the equator, walk a quarter of the way around the equator, return up a meridian to the pole —
the arrow comes back **rotated by 90°**, even though you never turned it at any step. The rotation angle equals exactly the solid angle the route encloses.
Parallel transport on a curved surface fails to return to itself around a closed loop, with a misalignment depending only on the enclosed area — this is called "holonomy".

The Berry phase is the holonomy of a quantum state in parameter space: the eigenstate is adiabatically "parallel-transported",
and after one loop its direction (phase) no longer matches its former self. The deflection of a Foucault pendulum's swing plane after a day
is the same geometry's incarnation in classical mechanics.

</div>
<div slot="right">

Give the integrand a name — the **Berry connection** (a "vector potential" defined on parameter space):

$$
\vec A_n(\vec R)=\ii\braket{n}{\nabla_{\vec R}\,n},
\qquad
\gamma_n(C)=\oint_C\vec A_n\cdot\dd\vec R
$$

It changes with the phase convention ($\vec A_n\to\vec A_n-\nabla\chi$), just as the electromagnetic vector potential changes with gauge;
but its "magnetic field" — the **Berry curvature** $\vec\Omega_n=\nabla\times\vec A_n$ — is gauge-independent.
Stokes' theorem writes the loop phase as a flux of curvature:

$$
\gamma_n(C)=\iint_S\vec\Omega_n\cdot\dd\vec S
$$

completely isomorphic to "phase = loop integral of the vector potential = magnetic flux". That is not a figure of speech: below we will see
that the electromagnetic AB phase is precisely a special case of this structure.

</div>
</DualView>

## A worked example: spin 1/2 in a slowly turning field

The parameter space is the sphere of field directions. For a spin 1/2 in a field $\vec B=B\hat n$,
the Berry connection of the "aligned" state $\ket{+;\hat n}$ can be computed explicitly from the spin states of section 5.3
(write $\ket{+;\hat n}$ in spherical coordinates $\theta,\varphi$, take the gradient in the parameters, do the loop integral).
The result is remarkably clean:

$$
\gamma_\pm(C)=\mp\frac{\Omega_C}{2}
$$

where $\Omega_C$ is **the solid angle enclosed** on the direction sphere by the loop $C$ the field direction traces out.
In equivalent curvature language: the spin's Berry curvature is a "monopole field" radiating outward from the centre of the sphere
($B=0$ — the degeneracy point!), $\vec\Omega_\pm=\mp\hat R/2R^2$ —
the degeneracy point is a magnetic monopole in parameter space, and the Berry phase is its flux.

**Plug in a number**: sweep the field once around a cone of half-angle $60^\circ$. The cone encloses a solid angle

$$
\Omega=2\pi(1-\cos60^\circ)=\pi
$$

so the "aligned" state collects $\gamma_+=-\pi/2$. To measure it: prepare the spin in a superposition of aligned and anti-aligned;
the two branches acquire geometric phases differing by $\Omega=\pi$, so after one turn the superposition's interference fringes shift bodily by half a period —
nuclear magnetic resonance experiments saw this shift in 1987, independent of the turning speed, answering only to the solid angle.

**A famous corollary**: sweep the field direction around a full great circle (half-angle $90^\circ$): $\Omega=2\pi$,
$\gamma=-\pi$ — the spin wavefunction **changes sign**. A sign flip under a 360° rotation — this is precisely
the geometric-phase version of section 5.3's "a spin 1/2 needs 720° to come back to itself".

## Aharonov–Bohm: a Berry phase twenty-five years early

In 1959 Aharonov and Bohm pointed out something strange: split an electron beam in two
and route the branches around the **two sides** of a long thin solenoid before recombining them. Outside the solenoid the magnetic field is strictly zero;
the electrons travel entirely through the region where $\vec B=0$ — as far as classical mechanics is concerned, the solenoid does not exist.
Yet when the two branches reunite they carry a phase difference

$$
\Delta\phi=\frac{q}{\hbar}\oint\vec A\cdot\dd\vec l=\frac{q\Phi}{\hbar}
$$

where $\Phi$ is the flux **inside** the tube. The interference fringes shift with $\Phi$ — even though the electrons never touched the field.
In 1986 Akira Tonomura ran the experiment with the flux completely shielded inside a superconducting ring; the fringes shifted exactly as predicted.

Seen through this section's eyes: the electron's position is the parameter, the vector potential $q\vec A/\hbar$ plays exactly the role of the Berry connection,
and the flux is the enclosed "Berry curvature flux". The AB phase is the geometric phase's special case in electromagnetism —
and it tells us the vector potential is not mere mathematical scaffolding: **where the local field is zero, geometry (topology) is still doing work**.

<Callout type="insight" title="From one phase to a whole discipline">
Replace "parameter space" by a crystal's momentum space: every energy band is an instantaneous eigenstate of $\vec k$,
each with its own Berry curvature. The curvature's flux over the whole Brillouin zone must be quantised to an integer (the Chern number) —
an integer that cannot change continuously under impurities or deformation: it is the band's **topological fingerprint**.
The quantum Hall conductance plateaus, accurate to $10^{-9}$, and the protected edge states of topological insulators
are that integer showing itself in experiment. The 1984 observation that was "just a phase"
is, forty years later, half the real estate of condensed-matter physics — probably the best advertisement ever for "taking a small oddity seriously".
</Callout>

## What comes next

The adiabatic approximation holds down the "infinitely slow" end of the scale. The other end — the Hamiltonian **switching instantaneously** —
turns out to be even simpler: the change is so fast that the wavefunction has no time to react at all, and the old wavefunction is
"frozen" untouched into the new Hamiltonian. The next section uses this "sudden approximation" on a beautiful real problem:
at the instant a tritium nucleus beta-decays, the nuclear charge jumps from 1 to 2 — where does its electron land?
It is also the final section of this chapter — after which we face head-on a fact the whole chapter has been dodging:
the two electrons of helium are not "electron 1" and "electron 2"; they are indistinguishable identical particles.
