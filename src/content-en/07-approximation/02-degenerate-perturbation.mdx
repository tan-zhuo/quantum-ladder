At the end of the last section we buried a landmine: both the first-order wavefunction and the second-order energy have $E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}$ in the denominator, and the moment two states are degenerate the formulas divide by zero on the spot. This section defuses the mine.

Start with a real experiment from 1913. Stark placed a hydrogen discharge tube in a strong electric field and found that the Balmer lines (the blue $n=4\to2$ line, for instance) **split into several symmetric components**, with a splitting **proportional** to the field strength. That is actually rather strange: we just said in the last section that the first-order Stark shift of the hydrogen ground state is zero (parity!), so the shift starts at second order and should go as $\mathcal E^2$. Why do the excited states, of all things, respond linearly?

The answer, in one word: degeneracy.

## What the zero denominator is telling us

Four states crowd onto the hydrogen $n=2$ level: $\ket{2s}$, $\ket{2p,m=0}$, $\ket{2p,m=\pm1}$,
all with exactly the same energy ([section 6.4](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/04-energy-levels/)).
Now switch on a field $\vec{\mathcal E}=\mathcal E\hat z$; the perturbation is

$$
\hat H'=e\mathcal E z
$$

Try the last section's formula: the corrected $\ket{2s}$ wants to mix in $\ket{2p_0}$, with coefficient

$$
\frac{\bra{2p_0}\hat H'\ket{2s}}{E_{2s}^{(0)}-E_{2p}^{(0)}}=\frac{\text{non-zero}}{0}
$$

It diverges. But this divergence is not mathematics throwing a tantrum — it is delivering a physical message:

<Callout type="insight" title="What the divergence really means: we picked the wrong zeroth-order states">
Degeneracy means the zeroth-order problem's solution is **not unique**. Any linear combination
$\alpha\ket{2s}+\beta\ket{2p_0}$ is a perfectly legal eigenstate of $\hat H_0$, all with the same energy —
$\hat H_0$ has no opinion whatsoever about "which basis to use".

But the perturbation has an opinion. The instant the field switches on, the four-fold degeneracy breaks, and the true eigenstates tend toward **one particular set of combinations**.
If you happen to start perturbation theory from that "right" set, all is well;
start from any other combination, and the perturbation is forced to wrench the state around with infinite coefficients — that is where the zero denominator comes from.

So the fix is not to dodge the division, but to **find the "right" zeroth-order states first**.
How to find them: let the perturbation choose — diagonalise $\hat H'$ inside the degenerate subspace.
</Callout>

## The secular equation

<Derivation title="The general framework of degenerate perturbation theory" level="基础" minutes={8} open={true}>

Suppose a level $E^{(0)}$ is $g$-fold degenerate, its subspace spanned by the orthonormal states
$\ket{\phi_1},\dots,\ket{\phi_g}$. A "good" zeroth-order state is some combination of them:

$$
\ket{\psi^{(0)}}=\sum_{a=1}^{g}c_a\ket{\phi_a}
$$

**Step 1: write the first-order equation.** Just as in the last section, the $\lambda^1$ order gives

$$
\hat H_0\ket{\psi^{(1)}}+\hat H'\ket{\psi^{(0)}}
=E^{(0)}\ket{\psi^{(1)}}+E^{(1)}\ket{\psi^{(0)}}
$$

**Step 2: project into the degenerate subspace.** Hit both sides with $\bra{\phi_b}$.
Acting to the left, the Hermitian $\hat H_0$ gives $E^{(0)}\braket{\phi_b}{\psi^{(1)}}$,
which cancels the first term on the right — exactly the same cancellation as last section, except this time what survives is a **matrix equation**:

$$
\sum_{a=1}^{g}\bra{\phi_b}\hat H'\ket{\phi_a}\,c_a=E^{(1)}c_b
$$

The left side is the $g\times g$ matrix $W_{ba}\equiv\bra{\phi_b}\hat H'\ket{\phi_a}$ acting on the column vector of coefficients.
This is a standard eigenvalue problem:

$$
W\vec c=E^{(1)}\vec c
$$

**Step 3: solve the eigenvalue problem.** A non-trivial solution exists only if

$$
\det\!\left(W-E^{(1)}I\right)=0
$$

This determinant equation is called the **secular equation** (the name comes from celestial mechanics: the same mathematics was once used to compute the "secular", long-term perturbations of planetary orbits). Its $g$ roots are the $g$ first-order energy shifts — the degeneracy splits into at most $g$ levels —
and the eigenvector $\vec c$ belonging to each root gives one "good" zeroth-order state.

**Boiled down to an operating manual:**
1. Identify the degenerate subspace and pick any basis for it;
2. Compute the $g\times g$ perturbation matrix $W$ in that basis;
3. Diagonalise $W$: eigenvalues = first-order shifts, eigenvectors = good zeroth-order states.

Note that this step is **not an approximation**: diagonalising within the subspace is exact.
The "perturbative" approximation lies in neglecting the coupling between the subspace and the levels outside it (that is second-order business).

</Derivation>

## A worked example: the Stark splitting of hydrogen at n=2

Follow the manual. Take the basis $\ket{2s},\ket{2p_0},\ket{2p_{+1}},\ket{2p_{-1}}$;
we need the $4\times4=16$ matrix elements of $\hat H'=e\mathcal E z$. Sounds intimidating,
but symmetry will kill nearly all of them for us.

**Parity.** $z$ is an odd function. The $s$ state has even parity, the $p$ states odd,
so $\bra{2s}z\ket{2s}=0$, and the three $\bra{2p}z\ket{2p'}$ elements all vanish too
(odd × odd × odd = odd; the integral is zero). **Every diagonal element is dead.**

**Angular momentum.** $z$ does not depend on the azimuthal angle $\varphi$, so $[\hat L_z,z]=0$:
the perturbation does not change the magnetic quantum number $m$. Hence $z$ can only connect states of equal $m$ —
$\ket{2p_{\pm1}}$ cannot connect to anyone (there are no other $m=\pm1$ states within $n=2$),
so they are spectators whose energy does not move at first order.

Only one pair survives: $\bra{2s}z\ket{2p_0}$ and its complex conjugate.

<Derivation title="The matrix element and the splitting" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

Integrate directly with the Chapter 6 hydrogen wavefunctions (radial part × angular part):

$$
\bra{2s}z\ket{2p_0}
=\int R_{20}R_{21}\,r^3\dd r\int Y_0^{0*}\cos\theta\,Y_1^0\,\dd\Omega
=-3a_0
$$

(No need to memorise the number — just remember the scale is the Bohr radius $a_0$: that is simply how big the $n=2$ orbitals are.)
So within the $\ket{2s},\ket{2p_0}$ block, the $2\times2$ matrix $W$ is

$$
W=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3e\mathcal E a_0\\-3e\mathcal E a_0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

The secular equation $\det(W-E^{(1)}I)=(E^{(1)})^2-(3e\mathcal E a_0)^2=0$ solves to

$$
E^{(1)}=\pm3e\mathcal E a_0,\qquad
\ket{\psi_\mp^{(0)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{2s}\mp\ket{2p_0}\right)
$$

$n=2$ splits into three lines: $-3e\mathcal E a_0$, $0$ (doubly: $2p_{\pm1}$), $+3e\mathcal E a_0$ —
matching the symmetric splitting pattern Stark saw, and **proportional to $\mathcal E$**.

**Plug in numbers**: at $\mathcal E=10^5\ \text{V/cm}=10^7\ \text{V/m}$ (a strong laboratory field),

$$
3e\mathcal E a_0=3\times(1.6\times10^{-19}\,\text{C})\times10^7\,\text{V/m}\times5.29\times10^{-11}\,\text{m}
\approx1.6\ \text{meV}
$$

corresponding to about 13 cm⁻¹ in wavenumbers — easy work for a spectrometer.

**Why is the response linear here?** The "good" states $(\ket{2s}\mp\ket{2p_0})/\sqrt2$
are superpositions of opposite-parity states — they **carry a built-in electric dipole moment** ($\langle z\rangle=\pm3a_0\neq0$),
with no need to wait for the field to polarise them. With a ready-made dipole, the energy is naturally proportional to $\mathcal E$;
the ground state has no degenerate partner to combine with, so it can only be polarised slowly by the field, hence $\mathcal E^2$.
The linear splitting of the Stark lines is direct evidence that "a ready-made dipole is hiding in the degenerate subspace".

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The perturbation is the judge; the degenerate states are the contestants.** With no perturbation, everyone in the subspace is equal — any orthogonal basis is a legitimate answer. Once the perturbation enters, the scoring begins: the combinations it selects
(the eigenvectors of $W$) pass smoothly into the true eigenstates the instant the perturbation switches on;
every other combination gets violently churned. The "good" in "good zeroth-order wavefunctions"
means precisely **the set of states that the true eigenstates approach in the limit $\mathcal E\to0$**.

</div>
<div slot="right">

The criterion for "good" can be stated crisply: if you can find a Hermitian operator $\hat A$ with

$$
[\hat A,\hat H_0]=0,\qquad[\hat A,\hat H']=0
$$

and the degenerate states are eigenstates of $\hat A$ with different eigenvalues, then they are automatically good states, and $W$ is already diagonal in that basis.
In the Stark problem $\hat A=\hat L_z$ does exactly this job: it removes the $m=\pm1$ states up front
and whittles the $4\times4$ problem down to $2\times2$.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Symmetry is diagonalisation for free">
Before computing any matrix element, ask two questions: is the perturbation odd or even under parity? Which conserved quantities does it commute with?
Each answer rules out a whole batch of matrix elements in advance. Above, symmetry chopped 16 matrix elements down to a single independent quantity —
not a coincidence, but the norm. In atomic physics this manoeuvre has a name: **selection rules**
(for a $z$-type perturbation, the rules are $\Delta\ell=\pm1$, $\Delta m=0$).
Prune the matrix with symmetry first, integrate second — that is the standard posture of degenerate perturbation theory.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="'Near-degeneracy' is just as dangerous">
The criterion was never "trouble only at exactly equal energies". The last section's validity condition was that matrix elements be far smaller than level spacings —
so the moment two levels get **close enough** (spacing comparable to the matrix element), the non-degenerate formulas are already untrustworthy,
and the near-degenerate levels must be diagonalised together inside one subspace.

Example: hydrogen's 2s and 2p are actually separated by the Lamb shift, about 4 μeV — not strictly degenerate.
When the field is very weak ($3e\mathcal E a_0\ll4\ \mu\text{eV}$) the response is quadratic;
once the field grows strong enough that the Stark matrix element swamps the Lamb gap, the linear splitting appears.
"Which set of formulas to use" depends on which is bigger, the perturbation or the spacing — and that is the soul of every approximation method.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Both sections of perturbation theory share one premise: the Hamiltonian must contain "a big solvable piece", and the perturbation must be small.
But look back at helium — the electron repulsion is a third of the main part, and first-order perturbation theory only squeezes the 30 eV error down to 4 eV:
neither here nor there. What if the system simply has no natural small parameter?
The variational method of the next section takes an entirely different tack: no expansion at all — just bid on the energy directly.
Guess a wavefunction; the energy it yields can never dip below the true ground state, and whoever guesses lowest wins.
