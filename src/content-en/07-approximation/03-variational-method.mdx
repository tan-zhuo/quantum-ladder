At the end of the last section, helium still owed us money: the electron repulsion is a third of the main part — hardly "small" — and first-order perturbation theory gives $-74.8\ \text{eV}$, 4 eV away from the experimental $-79.0\ \text{eV}$. To do better, grinding out second order means summing over infinitely many intermediate states — sheer misery. The root difficulty: **perturbation theory needs a small parameter, and helium has none.**

This section takes a completely different road. It makes no expansion whatsoever and asks nothing about the size of the perturbation. Its only weapon is a single inequality — and it is an inequality we already know how to prove.

## The variational principle

<Derivation title="Why no guess can break below the ground state" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

Let the true eigenstates of $\hat H$ be $\ket{n}$ with eigenvalues $E_0\le E_1\le E_2\le\cdots$. Take any normalised "trial state" $\ket{\psi}$ — a pure guess, not required to be any eigenstate. Since the eigenstates are complete ([section 3.7](/en/ch/03-formalism/07-postulates/)), it can always be expanded:

$$
\ket{\psi}=\sum_n c_n\ket{n},\qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1
$$

Compute the energy expectation:

$$
\braket{\psi}{\hat H\psi}=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n
\ \ge\ \sum_n|c_n|^2E_0=E_0
$$

The middle step uses only $E_n\ge E_0$: replacing each $E_n$ by the smaller $E_0$ can only shrink the sum. Hence

$$
\boxed{\ \braket{\psi}{\hat H\psi}\ \ge\ E_0\ }
$$

valid for **any** normalised state, with equality exactly when $\ket{\psi}$ is the ground state itself.

In plain words: the energy expectation is the average of the eigenenergies weighted by $|c_n|^2$, and **an average can never dip below the minimum**. That is all there is to it.

(If the trial state is not normalised, use $\braket{\psi}{\hat H\psi}/\braket{\psi}{\psi}\ge E_0$ — same content.)

</Derivation>

This inequality instantly becomes a machine for computing energies:

1. Guess a family of parametrised wavefunctions $\psi_\alpha$ ($\alpha$ can be one or several parameters);
2. Compute $E(\alpha)=\braket{\psi_\alpha}{\hat H\psi_\alpha}$;
3. Minimise over $\alpha$.

The minimum is still an upper bound — but it is the **tightest** upper bound in that family of guesses. The smarter the family, the closer the answer gets to the truth. This is the **variational method**: we "vary" over a family of wavefunctions to minimise the energy functional.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why the method is surprisingly robust: the energy error is second-order small">
Suppose the trial state misses the true ground state by a little: $\ket{\psi}=\ket{0}+\epsilon\ket{\delta}$ (with $\ket{\delta}\perp\ket{0}$). Expand the energy expectation, and you find the first-order-in-$\epsilon$ term is proportional to $\bra{0}\hat H\ket{\delta}=E_0\braket{0}{\delta}=0$ — it vanishes automatically:

$$
E(\psi)=E_0+O(\epsilon^2)
$$

**Err in the wavefunction at first order, and the energy errs only at second order.** Even a sloppy guess for the wavefunction can deliver quite a good energy — this is the deep reason the variational method runs riot across physics and chemistry. A large share of today's quantum chemistry packages and ab initio materials codes (Hartree–Fock, configuration interaction, density functional theory) are, at heart, variational calculations on a colossal scale.
</Callout>

## Warm-up: deliberately guessing the wrong shape

First, test the blade somewhere the exact answer is known, to see "what happens when the guess is wrong". For hydrogen (true ground state: the exponential $\ee^{-r/a_0}$, $E_0=-13.6\ \text{eV}$), deliberately pick the **wrong shape** as a trial — a Gaussian:

$$
\psi_b(r)\propto\ee^{-br^2}
$$

with $b$ the variational parameter. Work out the kinetic and potential expectation values (both standard Gaussian integrals),

$$
E(b)=\frac{3\hbar^2b}{2m}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,2\sqrt{\frac{2b}{\pi}}
$$

Set the derivative in $b$ to zero and substitute back:

$$
E_{\min}=-\frac{4}{3\pi}\,\frac{me^4}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2\hbar^2}
\approx-11.5\ \text{eV}
$$

The true value is $-13.6\ \text{eV}$. Two observations: the upper-bound promise held ($-11.5$ really is above $-13.6$); and although the shape was completely wrong (a Gaussian is too flat at the origin and falls off too fast at large $r$), the energy still captured 85%. That is the power of the "error is second-order small" theorem — and also its trap: **an accurate energy does not mean an accurate wavefunction**;
$\psi_b$ is badly wrong both near the origin and far away.

## The main course: the helium ground state

Time to settle the debt owed for two sections. The craft of building a trial wavefunction is **writing your physical intuition into the parameters**:

Each electron, besides being pulled by the nucleus ($Z=2$), is pushed away by the other electron. Seen from one electron's viewpoint, the other's cloud of negative charge "shades" part of the nucleus — the effective nuclear charge it feels should be **less than 2, greater than 1** (the other electron is sometimes inside it and sometimes outside, so the shading is imperfect). This effect is called **screening**.

So guess: each electron sits in a hydrogen-like ground state of nuclear charge $Z_e$, with $Z_e$ the variational parameter,

$$
\psi_{Z_e}(\vec r_1,\vec r_2)=\frac{Z_e^3}{\pi a_0^3}\,
\ee^{-Z_e(r_1+r_2)/a_0}
$$

and let energy minimisation decide "how much screening" for us.

<Derivation title="The screened variational calculation: from E(Zₑ) to −77.5 eV" level="进阶" minutes={12}>

Keep the books in units of $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a_0)=27.2\ \text{eV}$ (twice the Rydberg). The Hamiltonian splits into three pieces.

**Kinetic energy.** The trial state is a hydrogen-like ground state at charge $Z_e$, so the one-electron kinetic energy follows from the hydrogen-like virial theorem:
$\langle T\rangle=+Z_e^2/2$ (in the units above). Two electrons:

$$
\langle T_1+T_2\rangle=Z_e^2
$$

**Nuclear attraction.** The hydrogen-like ground state has $\langle1/r\rangle=Z_e/a_0$. The true nuclear charge is $Z=2$ (not $Z_e$!), so

$$
\left\langle-\frac{Z}{r_1}-\frac{Z}{r_2}\right\rangle=-2ZZ_e
$$

**Electron repulsion.** This is the only integral that genuinely has to be computed — the electrostatic interaction energy of two exponential charge clouds of charge $Z_e$ — and the result is a famous number:

$$
\left\langle\frac{1}{r_{12}}\right\rangle=\frac{5}{8}Z_e
$$

(Method: expand $1/r_{12}$ in spherical harmonics; only the spherically symmetric term survives, and what remains is elementary.
The 34 eV used in the first-order calculation of section 7.1 is just this at $Z_e=2$: $\tfrac58\times2\times27.2=34.0\ \text{eV}$.)

**Total:**

$$
E(Z_e)=\left[Z_e^2-2ZZ_e+\frac58Z_e\right]\times27.2\ \text{eV}
$$

Sanity check: set $Z_e=Z=2$ (no screening) and get $(4-8+1.25)\times27.2=-74.8\ \text{eV}$ — exactly the first-order perturbation result. **First-order perturbation theory = the variational method with its parameter left unoptimised**; the two roads meet right here.

**Minimise.** $\dd E/\dd Z_e=2Z_e-2Z+\tfrac58=0$:

$$
Z_e=Z-\frac{5}{16}=\frac{27}{16}=1.6875
$$

The screening is $5/16\approx0.31$: on average the other electron shades about a third of one nuclear charge — squarely in the intuitively expected "between 0 and 1, on the small side" range. Substituting back (the bracket at the minimum equals exactly $-Z_e^2$):

$$
E_{\min}=-\left(\frac{27}{16}\right)^2\times27.2\ \text{eV}=-77.5\ \text{eV}
$$

**The scoreboard:**

| Method | Result | Gap to experiment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ignore repulsion entirely | $-108.8$ eV | 29.8 eV |
| First-order perturbation (section 7.1) | $-74.8$ eV | 4.2 eV |
| One-parameter variation | $-77.5$ eV | 1.5 eV |
| Experiment | $-79.0$ eV | — |

One parameter, one page of algebra, and the error drops to 2%. And this road has no end: in 1929 Hylleraas wrote $r_{12}$ explicitly into the trial function and reached 0.01 eV with a handful of parameters; modern variational calculations pin the helium ground state to a dozen-plus significant figures. **The accuracy is limited only by how much imagination you are willing to pack into the trial function** — a promise perturbation theory can never make.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The variational method is an auction.** The true ground-state energy is a reserve price hidden under the floor; every trial wavefunction is a bid, and the rules guarantee each bid stays above the reserve. You cannot know how far above the reserve you are, but any two bids can be compared: **lower is better**. So the method is iterative by nature — add a parameter and the energy can only drop or stay put, never get worse.

</div>
<div slot="right">

Nested parameter families tighten the bound monotonically: if family $A\subset B$ ($B$ contains all the functions of $A$), then

$$
E_0\ \le\ \min_{\psi\in B}\frac{\braket{\psi}{\hat H\psi}}{\braket{\psi}{\psi}}\ \le\ \min_{\psi\in A}(\cdots)
$$

Limiting case: expand the family to all of Hilbert space and the minimum equals $E_0$ exactly — the variational principle itself is **exact**; the approximation comes only from the finiteness of the family.

</div>
</DualView>

## When to use it, and where to be careful

The variational method applies startlingly widely: no small perturbation required, no nearby solvable model required — only the ability to compute $\braket{\psi}{\hat H\psi}$. But its blind spots are just as sharp.

<Callout type="warning" title="The four blind spots of the variational method">
**1. No error bar.** The upper bound is the entire promise — you know the truth lies below, not how far below. (The Gaussian trial for hydrogen missed by 15%, the screened trial for helium by 2%, and neither was knowable in advance.)

**2. Accurate energy ≠ accurate wavefunction.** The second-order-error theorem is a double-edged sword: the energy is insensitive to wavefunction mistakes, which conversely means a good energy guarantees nothing about wavefunction detail. For quantities sensitive to the wavefunction (probability density at the origin, dipole moments), the variational wavefunction can be badly off.

**3. Directly applicable only to the ground state.** The proof used "$E_0$ is the minimum". To do variational excited states, the trial state must stay **orthogonal** to every lower state; otherwise a ground-state component leaking into the trial state drags the energy below the excited level, and the upper-bound property fails for it. The reliable trick is symmetry: in hydrogen, vary over $\ell=1$ trial functions — automatically orthogonal to the $\ell=0$ ground state — and what you get is an upper bound on the $2p$ energy.

**4. Few parameters can mean bias.** Minimisation optimises exactly one number, the energy; any prejudice built into the trial family (forced spherical symmetry, say) stays in the answer untouched.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Perturbation theory and the variational method both answer the same kind of question: **where are the energy levels?** But some questions are not about levels at all — how does an alpha particle escape the nucleus? How does an electron cross a barrier it could classically never climb? The protagonist of those questions is **the wavefunction under the barrier**, and there the wavefunction decays exponentially — no polynomial-style correction can keep up with it. The WKB approximation of the next section is built for exactly this "semiclassical" situation: when the potential varies slowly enough, the wavefunction is locally a plane wave at every point — and with that we will compute a tunnelling probability for the first time, and explain the lifetime law in nuclear physics that spans thirty orders of magnitude.
