The entanglement protocols of the past few sections solved **communication** problems.
This section switches battlefields: **computation**. We will watch quantum mechanics beat the
classical world at computing for the first time, cleanly — and the way it wins is not
"calculating faster" but "asking fewer questions".

## The problem at the door: can one question identify both faces of a coin?

Someone hands you a black box (an **oracle**) containing a function
$f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ — feed it an $n$-bit string, it spits out one bit. They promise $f$ is one
of exactly two kinds:

- **Constant**: every input gives the same output (all 0 or all 1);
- **Balanced**: exactly half the inputs give 0, the other half give 1.

The task: decide which. The only thing you can do is **query** — feed one input, see one output.
Question: how few queries suffice?

**The classical bill.** With luck, 2 queries (two different outputs ⇒ balanced).
But to declare "constant" **beyond all doubt**, the worst case requires

$$
\frac{2^n}{2}+1=2^{n-1}+1\ \text{queries}
$$

— if half the inputs have come back identical, only query number $2^{n-1}+1$ can settle it.
For $n=100$ that is about $6.3\times10^{29}$ queries; the age of the universe would not cover it.
The quantum algorithm queries **once**.

## Why naive "quantum parallelism" is not enough

The quantum black box must be reversible (section 9.2); the standard construction adds an ancilla wire:

$$
U_f\ket{x}\ket{y}=\ket{x}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}
$$

($\oplus$ is addition mod 2; applying it twice undoes it, so it really is unitary.)
The first move anyone thinks of: use a row of $H$'s to build the superposition of all inputs,
then query once:

$$
U_f\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x\ket{x}\right)\ket{0}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x\ket{x}\ket{f(x)}
$$

One call, and all $2^n$ function values have been computed — that is **quantum parallelism**.
But hold the celebration: measure this state and it collapses randomly onto a **single**
$(x, f(x))$ pair, no different from one classical query. Section 9.1's old lesson: information
stored in a state ≠ information you can take out. Quantum parallelism is the necessary fuel;
**interference is the spark** — the information of all the $f(x)$'s must be funnelled into the
probability distribution of the measurement outcome. The wrench that does the funnelling is called
**phase kickback**.

<Derivation title="Phase kickback: moving the function value into the phase" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

Prepare the ancilla in $\ket{-}=\frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}$ and feed it to $U_f$:

$$
U_f\ket{x}\ket{-}
=\ket{x}\otimes\frac{\ket{0\oplus f(x)}-\ket{1\oplus f(x)}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Two cases:

- $f(x)=0$: the ancilla is $\frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{-}$, untouched;
- $f(x)=1$: the ancilla is $\frac{\ket{1}-\ket{0}}{\sqrt2}=-\ket{-}$, an extra minus sign.

Both cases in one line:

$$
U_f\ket{x}\ket{-}=(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}\ket{-}
$$

The ancilla stays $\ket{-}$ throughout, and **the function value has become a phase**
$(-1)^{f(x)}$ **on the $\ket{x}$ branch**. For a single $x$, a global phase means nothing;
but in a superposition, each branch collects its own sign — they become **relative phases**,
and relative phases are the control lever of interference (section 9.2's lesson from $Z\ket{+}=\ket{-}$).

</Derivation>

## Hand-compute the smallest case first: the Deutsch algorithm (n = 1)

For $n=1$ there are four possible $f$'s: constant ($f\equiv0$, $f\equiv1$),
balanced ($f(x)=x$, $f(x)=1-x$). The decision problem boils down to one bit: $f(0)\oplus f(1)=?$
(0 ⇒ constant, 1 ⇒ balanced). The circuit:

```text
|0⟩ ──[H]──┬──────[H]──[measure] → 0: constant / 1: balanced
           │Uf
|−⟩ ───────┴────────────────── (ancilla stays |−⟩ throughout)
```

**Step by step** (the ancilla stays $\ket{-}$; we write only the first qubit):

$$
\ket{0}
\ \xrightarrow{H}\ \frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}
\ \xrightarrow{U_f}\ \frac{(-1)^{f(0)}\ket{0}+(-1)^{f(1)}\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Pull out the global phase $(-1)^{f(0)}$ (unobservable — discard):

$$
=\frac{\ket{0}+(-1)^{f(0)\oplus f(1)}\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}
=\begin{cases}\ket{+},&\text{constant}\\[2pt]\ket{-},&\text{balanced}\end{cases}
$$

The final $H$ rotates $\ket{\pm}$ back to the computational basis:

$$
\ket{+}\xrightarrow{H}\ket{0}\ (\text{constant}),\qquad
\ket{-}\xrightarrow{H}\ket{1}\ (\text{balanced})
$$

Measure the first qubit and read the answer **deterministically**. One query has extracted
$f(0)\oplus f(1)$, which classically takes two — note that we did **not** obtain the individual
values of $f(0)$ and $f(1)$, only one **global property** of theirs. Quantum speedup always plays
this game: surrender pointwise information, gain global information.

## The general case: n-qubit Deutsch–Jozsa

```text
|0⟩ ──[H]──┬─────[H]──[measure]┐
|0⟩ ──[H]──┤Uf   [H]──[measure]├─ all 0: constant / otherwise: balanced
 ⋮    ⋮    │      ⋮     ⋮      │
|−⟩ ───────┴───────────────────┘ (ancilla not measured)
```

<Derivation title="How interference concentrates the answer onto the all-zero string" level="进阶" minutes={8}>

**Step 1: prepare and query.** $n$ copies of $\ket{0}$ go through a row of $H$'s, then phase kickback:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x}\ket{x}
\ \xrightarrow{U_f}\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}
$$

**Step 2: another row of $H$'s.** We need the formula for $H^{\otimes n}$ acting on basis states.
For one qubit, $H\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sum_{y}(-1)^{xy}\ket{y}$ (check by substituting $x=0,1$);
for $n$ bits, multiply bit by bit:

$$
H^{\otimes n}\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{y}(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket{y},
\qquad x\cdot y\equiv x_1y_1\oplus\cdots\oplus x_ny_n
$$

Substituting gives the final state:

$$
\ket{\Psi_{\text{final}}}=\sum_{y}\left[\frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)+x\cdot y}\right]\ket{y}
$$

**Step 3: look only at the amplitude of the all-zero string $y=0\cdots0$.** There $x\cdot y=0$:

$$
c_{0}=\frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{x}(-1)^{f(x)}
$$

- **Constant**: all $2^n$ terms share one sign, $c_0=\pm1$. Probability $|c_0|^2=1$ —
  every branch **interferes constructively** and floods into the all-zero string;
  the measurement is certain to give $0\cdots0$.
- **Balanced**: half $+1$'s, half $-1$'s, cancelling in pairs, $c_0=0$ —
  the all-zero string is emptied by **destructive interference**; the measurement is certain to
  give a non-zero string.

The verdict: an all-zero readout ⇒ constant; any non-zero bit ⇒ balanced. One query, zero error rate.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The speedup in three acts.** Looking back over the whole run, the quantum advantage is a relay of
three things: (1) **superposition** lets one query touch every input;
(2) **phase kickback** transcribes each function value into a branch phase;
(3) **interference** (the final row of $H$'s) lets the phases decide the measurement distribution by
"collective vote" — a constant function passes unanimously into the all-zero string, a balanced
function's votes cancel half against half. Remove any link and you fall back to classical:
without interference it is a lottery, without phase kickback the branches are indistinguishable,
without superposition there are no votes to cast.

</div>
<div slot="right">

Query-complexity comparison (for an error-free decision):

$$
\text{classical (deterministic)}:\ 2^{n-1}+1
$$

$$
\text{quantum}:\ 1
$$

Exponential versus constant. But honesty requires a footnote: if the classical algorithm may
**sample randomly** $k$ times and tolerate error probability $2^{-k}$, it too needs only a constant
number of queries. So DJ's exponential advantage strictly holds in the "exact decision" sense —
it is a demonstration of principle, not a practical algorithm.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="DJ's true legacy: a reusable skeleton">
The problem Deutsch–Jozsa decides has no practical value in itself (who ever gets handed such a
black box?), but its three-movement skeleton — **open with a row of H's, let the oracle write
phases, close with interference** — became the standard floor plan of quantum algorithms.
Bernstein–Vazirani swaps in a different oracle and reads out a hidden string directly;
Simon's algorithm uses it to find a hidden period, achieving a genuine exponential separation and
directly inspiring Shor (section 9.9); Grover (section 9.8) replaces "one big interference" with
"the accumulation of many small ones". Learn DJ, and every later algorithm is a variation on it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't mistake 'parallelism' for the cause of the speedup">
Popular accounts often say "a quantum computer tries all answers at once, so it's fast".
This section's computation shows that is only half true: superposition really does let $U_f$ act on
all $2^n$ inputs in one shot, but **a measurement can carry out only one result** — if the story
ended there, quantum would have no advantage at all. The entire credit for the speedup belongs to
interference: compressing $2^n$ values that cannot be read out one by one into a single
**global property** that can be read in one go (here, whether $f$ is "uniform").
Which is also why quantum speedup serves only problems whose answer happens to be some global
structure — it is not a universal accelerator.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Deutsch–Jozsa is an exhibition match tailor-made for quantum mechanics: the problem is artificial,
and classical randomised algorithms handle it easily too. A real-world problem looks like this:
one of $N$ drawers hides a key, opening a drawer costs one look, and classically you open $N/2$ on
average. Can quantum open fewer? Next section, Grover's algorithm answers $\sqrt N$ — and we will
see it with perfect clarity in a purely geometric picture: two mirrors squeezing out a rotation.
