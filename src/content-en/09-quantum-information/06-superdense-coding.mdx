At the end of the last section we read teleportation's trade slip backwards:
pre-share 1 ebit, then **mail 1 qubit** — how many classical bits does that exchange for?
Section 9.1's Holevo argument says one qubit yields at most 1 bit on readout,
yet this section will legally read out 2. Watch entanglement cut the postage in half.

## The concrete problem: one particle per shipment, four things to say

Alice needs to send Bob a two-bit message — one of 00, 01, 10, 11
(say "buy / sell / hold / liquidate"). But the channel is expensive, and this month's quota is
down to **one qubit**.

## Without entanglement, one qubit is worth one bit

First, the ceiling on the naive scheme. Alice encodes the message into a single qubit's state and
mails it; Bob measures on arrival. Section 9.1 told us: a measurement has only two exits, and
distinct non-orthogonal states cannot be reliably distinguished. To send 2 bits reliably, Bob must
separate the four states **unambiguously**, which requires four mutually orthogonal states —
but a single qubit's Hilbert space is only two-dimensional, with room for at most 2 orthogonal
states. Dead end. (This is exactly a special case of Holevo's theorem: with no auxiliary resource,
$n$ qubits ≤ $n$ classical bits.)

The way out: **Bob is allowed to hold something in advance.** As long as the two share a Bell pair
beforehand, the "room for manoeuvre" of Alice's half doubles.

## The full protocol, by hand

Beforehand: the two share $\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}$,
particle A with Alice, particle B with Bob.

```text
            encode (Alice, local)     mail A          decode (Bob, local)
 A ────────[ I / X / Z / ZX ]────────────────●──[H]──[measure]→ m₁
   ╲                                         │
    Φ⁺                                       │
   ╱                                         │
 B ──────────────────────────────────────────⊕──────[measure]→ m₂
```

<Derivation title="Four Pauli gates dial up four Bell states" level="基础" minutes={7} open={true}>

**Step 1: encode.** According to the message, Alice applies one gate to **her own half**:

- Message 00: do nothing ($I$). The state is unchanged:

$$
(I\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\Phi^+}
$$

- Message 01: apply $X$ (flip A). Acting term by term on the first qubit:

$$
(X\otimes I)\,\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{\ket{10}+\ket{01}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\Psi^+}
$$

- Message 10: apply $Z$ (a minus sign on A's $\ket{1}$):

$$
(Z\otimes I)\,\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\Phi^-}
$$

- Message 11: first $X$, then $Z$:

$$
(ZX\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+}
=(Z\otimes I)\,\frac{\ket{10}+\ket{01}}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{\ket{01}-\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}=\ket{\Psi^-}
$$

Four messages $\mapsto$ four Bell states, and the Bell states are **mutually orthogonal** —
the orthogonality needed for reliable discrimination is all there; it just lives in the two-qubit space.

**Step 2: mail it.** Alice sends particle A to Bob. Both particles are now in Bob's hands.

**Step 3: decode = Bell measurement.** Run the circuit from the end of section 9.5 in reverse
reading: CNOT (A controlling B) then $H$ on A maps the four Bell states back onto the four
computational basis states. Verify one case (take $\ket{\Psi^-}$; the rest go the same way):

$$
\ket{\Psi^-}\xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{CNOT}\ }
\frac{\ket{01}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{\ket{0}-\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket{1}
\xrightarrow{\ H\otimes I\ }\ket{1}\otimes\ket{1}=\ket{11}
$$

The complete table of all four cases:

| Message | Alice's gate | State in the channel | Readout $m_1m_2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 00 | $I$ | $\ket{\Phi^+}$ | 00 |
| 01 | $X$ | $\ket{\Psi^+}$ | 01 |
| 10 | $Z$ | $\ket{\Phi^-}$ | 10 |
| 11 | $ZX$ | $\ket{\Psi^-}$ | 11 |

Bob reads out two bits deterministically. **No probabilities, no guessing** — because the four
candidate states are orthogonal.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="Why touching only one half is enough">
It feels deeply wrong: Alice never lays a finger on particle B, yet switches the **whole** system
freely among four orthogonal states. The secret is section 9.3's old friend: in a maximally
entangled state all the information lives in the **correlations**, not in either half.
$X$ rewrites "are the two particles alike or opposite" ($\Phi$-type versus $\Psi$-type),
$Z$ rewrites "is the relative phase plus or minus" (superscript $+$ versus $-$) —
both switches are properties of the correlation, and can be flipped from either end.
The local view feels nothing: under all four encodings, particle A's reduced state is $I/2$,
so an eavesdropper who intercepts A sees only white noise. Superdense coding comes with built-in
secrecy: without B's half, not one of the 2 bits can be extracted.
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Mirror image of teleportation.** The two protocols burn the same fuel (1 ebit),
in exactly opposite directions:

- Teleportation: spend 2 classical bits to **ship 1 qubit**;
- Superdense coding: spend 1 qubit to **ship 2 classical bits**.

One hauls quantum cargo over a classical channel; the other hauls classical cargo over a quantum
channel. This mirror pair shows there is no fixed exchange rate between qubits and cbits —
the rate is set by the entanglement reserves on hand.
The very notion of "entanglement as a consumable communication resource"
was established by these two protocols of 1992/1993.

</div>
<div slot="right">

The resource equations side by side (consumed on the left of the arrow, produced on the right):

$$
1\ \text{ebit}+2\ \text{cbit}\ \longrightarrow\ 1\ \text{qubit}
$$

$$
1\ \text{ebit}+1\ \text{qubit}\ \longrightarrow\ 2\ \text{cbit}
$$

Neither can be reverse-engineered into a free lunch: substitute the second into the first and one
round cancels on each side, giving $2\,\text{ebit} + \text{a round trip of channel} \to$ back where
you started — entanglement only dwindles with use, never appreciates out of thin air
(LOCC cannot increase entanglement, section 9.3).

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The ceiling is 2 — don't dream up 'dense coding squared'">
Can we press on and use more entanglement to make one qubit carry 3 bits, 4 bits? No.
With pre-shared entanglement the Holevo bound doubles to **2** bits per qubit, and superdense
coding already maxes it out. Just count degrees of freedom: what Bob finally holds is the
four-dimensional space of **two** qubits, with room for at most 4 orthogonal states — 2 bits, full
stop. Alice mails one particle yet uses the whole four-dimensional space only because Bob's half
was in place all along; the particle she mails is "the second half of the key", not the whole safe.
Bennett and Wiesner proposed the protocol in 1992; Innsbruck demonstrated it with entangled photons
in 1996 (their Bell measurement could resolve only three classes, giving a measured capacity of
about 1.58 bits; later experiments closed in on 2).
</Callout>

## What comes next

So far, entanglement has helped us **move** information: sending states, carrying messages.
The next question is more ambitious: can quantum mechanics help us **compute** faster?
The Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm of 1992 delivered the first clean "yes" —
a question that classically takes exponentially many queries, answered quantumly in one.
Next section: how superposition and interference become computing power.
