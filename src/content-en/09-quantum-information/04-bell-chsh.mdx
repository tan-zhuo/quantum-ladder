The last section ended on a dangling question: could the Bell state's perfect correlations
just mean the two particles copied the same answer sheet before parting?
This section is the intellectual climax of the chapter — we will watch this seemingly untestable
philosophical question be turned by John Bell, in 1964, into a piece of **arithmetic**,
and then voted down by experiment, ballot by ballot, over the following sixty years.

## EPR: a thoroughly reasonable objection

In 1935, Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen stared at entangled states and made the following argument.
Take the spin singlet (the Bell state $\ket{\Psi^-}$, total spin zero):

$$
\ket{\Psi^-}=\frac{\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Send the two particles to a far-apart Alice and Bob. Alice measures along $z$ and gets $\uparrow$;
she **immediately knows** that Bob, measuring along $z$, must get $\downarrow$ — one hundred percent,
no exceptions. EPR argue: Bob's particle is worlds away from Alice, so her measurement cannot
instantly change it (**locality**); and a quantity you can predict with certainty without touching
the system must be a property it carried all along (**realism**). Conclusion: the outcomes were
determined before any measurement, and quantum mechanics' "probabilities" merely reflect hidden
determining factors we have not seen — **hidden variables**.

The claim sounds untestable: the crib sheet is hidden in the particle's pocket — who could prove it
isn't there? Bell's genius was to discover: **if the crib sheet exists, then no matter what is
written on it, it must obey an inequality**.

## What a local hidden-variable model actually promises

Write the claim as mathematics. Each particle pair sets out carrying a hidden variable $\lambda$
(the crib sheet — arbitrary content, arbitrary distribution). Alice has two possible measurement
directions $a, a'$, Bob has $b, b'$, and every outcome is recorded as $\pm1$.
Local realism promises the existence of four **predetermined** functions:

$$
A(a,\lambda),\ A(a',\lambda),\ B(b,\lambda),\ B(b',\lambda)\ \in\ \{\pm1\}
$$

Two points are crucial: $A$ does not depend on what Bob chose (locality),
and all four values **exist simultaneously** for each $\lambda$ (realism — even if Alice only
measured $a$ this round, "what would I have got measuring $a'$" has a definite answer).
The correlation function is just an average over crib sheets:

$$
E(a,b)=\int \dd\lambda\,p(\lambda)\,A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda)
$$

<Derivation title="The CHSH inequality: ∣S∣ ≤ 2" level="基础" minutes={6} open={true}>

**Step 1: for one fixed crib sheet $\lambda$, examine the combination**

$$
s(\lambda)=A(a,\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)-B(b',\lambda)\big]
+A(a',\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)+B(b',\lambda)\big]
$$

**Step 2: enumerate.** $B(b,\lambda)$ and $B(b',\lambda)$ are each $\pm1$, so there are only two cases:

- they are **equal**: the first bracket is 0, the second is $\pm2$, so $s=\pm2$;
- they are **opposite**: the second bracket is 0, the first is $\pm2$, so $s=\pm2$.

However the crib sheet is written, always $s(\lambda)=\pm2$.

**Step 3: average.** Define

$$
S\equiv E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a',b')=\int\dd\lambda\,p(\lambda)\,s(\lambda)
$$

A weighted average of $\pm2$'s can never leave $[-2,2]$:

$$
\boxed{\,|S|\le 2\,}
$$

**The derivation used exactly two assumptions**: the outcomes are predetermined (all four values
exist at once) and do not depend on the other party's choice. Not a single ingredient of quantum
mechanics was used — this is the ceiling for **every** local hidden-variable theory.

</Derivation>

## What quantum mechanics says: first compute the correlation function

<Derivation title="The singlet's correlation function E(a, b) = −cos(θa − θb)" level="进阶" minutes={8}>

Let Alice measure the spin along $\hat a$ and Bob along $\hat b$ (outcomes $\pm1$, i.e. measuring
$\hat a\cdot\vec\sigma$). The quantum correlation function is

$$
E(a,b)=\braket{\Psi^-}{(\hat a\cdot\vec\sigma)\otimes(\hat b\cdot\vec\sigma)\,|\Psi^-}
$$

**Step 1: exploit the singlet's rotational symmetry.** $\ket{\Psi^-}$ has total spin zero and is
invariant under simultaneous rotation of both particles (chapter 5). So the answer can depend only
on the angle between $\hat a$ and $\hat b$. Take both in the $x$–$z$ plane, angle $\theta_{ab}$.

**Step 2: three special cases first.** Take $\hat a=\hat b=\hat z$:

$$
(\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z)\ket{\Psi^-}
=\frac{(+1)(-1)\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}-(-1)(+1)\ket{\downarrow\uparrow}}{\sqrt2}
=-\ket{\Psi^-}
$$

So $E=-1$: parallel measurements are **perfectly anticorrelated** (total spin zero, made visible).
The same method with $\hat a=\hat z,\hat b=\hat x$ (using $\sigma_x$ to flip basis states) makes the
cross terms cancel in pairs, giving $E=0$.

**Step 3: general angle.** Decompose $\hat b\cdot\vec\sigma=\cos\theta_{ab}\,\sigma_{\parallel}+\sin\theta_{ab}\,\sigma_{\perp}$
along the directions parallel and perpendicular to $\hat a$, and assemble linearly from the two
special cases:

$$
E(a,b)=-\cos\theta_{ab}
$$

**Check the endpoints**: $\theta_{ab}=0$ gives $-1$ (perfect anticorrelation), $90^\circ$ gives $0$
(independence), $180^\circ$ gives $+1$ (perfect correlation). A smooth cosine — compare it with the
closest thing a hidden-variable model can produce, a piecewise-linear ramp: the cosine is steep in
the middle and flat at the ends, and that is precisely the geometric reason it can violate CHSH.

</Derivation>

**Now pick angles to maximise $|S|$.** Take (all in one plane, in degrees)

$$
a=0,\quad a'=90,\quad b=45,\quad b'=135
$$

Substitute each pair into $E=-\cos\theta_{ab}$:

| Pair | Angle | $E$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $a,b$ | 45° | $-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ |
| $a,b'$ | 135° | $+\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ |
| $a',b$ | 45° | $-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ |
| $a',b'$ | 45° | $-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ |

$$
S=E(a,b)-E(a,b')+E(a',b)+E(a',b')
=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\times4=-2\sqrt2\approx-2.83
$$

$$
|S|=2\sqrt2>2
$$

Quantum mechanics **crashes through the ceiling of every local hidden-variable theory**.
And $2\sqrt2$ is no lucky choice of angles: one can prove that quantum mechanics cannot produce a
larger value with any state and any measurements (the **Tsirelson bound**) —
$2\sqrt2$ is the limit of quantum correlations.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: why the cosine beats the ramp.** A hidden-variable model can also manage
"anticorrelated when parallel, uncorrelated when perpendicular" — for instance, give each pair a
random direction $\hat\lambda$ and let each side output the sign of $\hat a\cdot\hat\lambda$.
That model's correlation varies **linearly** with the angle — a straight ramp.
The four CHSH angles deliberately strike at the 45° "midsection":
at 45° the cosine's correlation strength is $0.707$, much steeper than the ramp's $0.5$,
and summing four such terms amplifies the gap into $2\sqrt2$ versus 2.
Entanglement wins by being **more strongly correlated between different measurement axes, above all at
intermediate angles, than classical physics allows**. Last section's "perfect on both axes" on its own can
still be mimicked by hidden variables; the intermediate angles are where classical models fall behind.

</div>
<div slot="right">

The hidden-variable toy model (random axis $\hat\lambda$, output by sign):

$$
E_{\text{ramp}}(\theta)=-1+\frac{2\theta}{\pi},\qquad \theta\in[0,\pi]
$$

At the CHSH angle combination:

$$
S_{\text{ramp}}=-\tfrac12\times4=-2
$$

Flat against the ceiling. Quantum:

$$
S_{\text{QM}}=-\cos45^\circ\times4=-2\sqrt2
$$

The Tsirelson bound: $|S|_{\text{QM}}\le2\sqrt2$, with equality requiring a maximally entangled
state (up to local unitaries) and measurement directions equivalent to the set above.

</div>
</DualView>

## The experimental tribunal: closing the loopholes one by one

An inequality can be violated on paper, but it must also be violated in the laboratory. From
Freedman–Clauser in 1972 to Aspect's photon experiments in 1982, the measured $S$ has always sided
with quantum mechanics. But careful sceptics pointed out two **loopholes** — assumptions of the
derivation that an experiment does not automatically enforce:

1. **The locality loophole**: if a signal carrying Alice's choice of measurement direction has time
   to reach Bob's particle (at light speed), the assumption "$B$ does not depend on $a$" is not
   enforced. The fix: put the stations far enough apart and choose the measurement directions with
   fast random number generators only while the particles are in flight, so that any "tip-off" is
   relativistically too late. Aspect first implemented fast switching in 1982; the 1998 Innsbruck
   experiment nailed it with strict spacelike separation.
2. **The detection loophole**: photon detectors are inefficient, and if only a small fraction of
   particles is recorded, the recorded sample may be biased — hidden variables could conspire to let
   only the "cooperative" particles be detected (one must assume "fair sampling" to conclude
   anything). The fix: push the total detection efficiency above roughly 83%, or switch to matter
   qubits with near-unit detection efficiency.

The trouble was that early experiments each closed only one: photon experiments had the distance but
poor detection; ion experiments detected everything but sat too close together. **In 2015, three
experiments closed both loopholes simultaneously**: Delft used diamond colour centres 1.3 km apart
(detection efficiency near 100% — extremely low event rate, but every event counts), while Vienna
and NIST used entangled photons with high-efficiency superconducting detectors (hundreds of millions
of events, with $p$-values of about $10^{-31}$ and $10^{-7}$ respectively).
All three clearly violated the Bell inequality. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded to Clauser,
Aspect and Zeilinger, put the official seal on this experimental line.

<Callout type="insight" title="What was sentenced to death is neither 'realism' nor 'locality', but their conspiracy">
What the Bell experiments rule out is the **conjunction**: "outcomes are predetermined AND
unaffected by distant choices". Quantum mechanics keeps the part of locality that truly matters —
**no signalling**: last section we computed that $\rho_A=I/2$ is independent of anything Bob does,
Alice's statistics never budge, and no information travels faster than light. The price paid is
realism: "what would I have got had I measured $a'$" has **no answer** in quantum mechanics —
an unperformed measurement has no outcome. That is not a gap in our knowledge; it is how the world
is built.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Common misreadings">
"Bell experiments prove faster-than-light action" — no: the no-signalling theorem survives intact,
and you can never use entanglement to send a message (section 9.5 will hammer on this again).
"Violating the Bell inequality proves quantum mechanics" — also no: it only **rules out** local
hidden variables; quantum mechanics is one theory consistent with the data (though predicting
exactly $2\sqrt2$ is a hard-edged success). One remaining escape route is to abandon the free choice
of measurement settings (superdeterminism) — logically irrefutable, but at the price of giving up
the methodology of experimental science altogether.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The Bell experiments tell us: the correlations inside entanglement are stronger than any classical
mechanism, and yet cannot carry a single bit. So what is this "super-strong but incommunicable
correlation" actually good for? The next section gives the first astonishing answer: transferring an
unknown quantum state from Alice's hands to Bob's — moving no particle at all, just making one
two-bit phone call.
