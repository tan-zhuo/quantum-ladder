Grover's ending pointed the way: for exponential speedup, find a problem with **structure**.
In 1994 Peter Shor found that problem — factoring large integers.
Its structure is a hidden periodicity, and "finding periods with interference"
has been quantum mechanics' signature trade since the double-slit experiment.
This section does not dive into number-theoretic proofs, but it works the full logical chain
and the quantum part all the way through.

## The problem at the door: multiplication is easy, division is deadly

$3\times5=15$ — mental arithmetic. The reverse, "15 = ? × ?", is not hard either.
But make the number 617 digits long (2048 bits): multiplying two thousand-digit primes takes a
computer milliseconds; recovering the two factors from the product, using the fastest known
classical algorithm (the number field sieve), takes time on the order of

$$
\exp\!\left[c\,(\ln N)^{1/3}(\ln\ln N)^{2/3}\right],\qquad c\approx1.9
$$

— **sub-exponential**: far worse than polynomial. For a 2048-bit $N$, all the computing power on
Earth combined would need millennia. The security of RSA public-key cryptography is staked on
exactly this one-way street: the public key displays $N$ in plain sight, and whoever can factor it
can read every ciphertext.

## First leg: factoring ⇒ period finding (classical number theory, results only)

Shor's first move is to **rewrite** the problem. Pick any $a$ coprime to $N$ and look at the
modular exponential

$$
f(x)=a^x \bmod N
$$

It is necessarily periodic: $f(x+r)=f(x)$, and the smallest positive period $r$ is called the
**order** of $a$. Number theory provides a bridge (stated without proof):

> If $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$, then
> $\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,\,N)$ and $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,\,N)$ yield nontrivial factors of $N$.

The intuition in one line: $a^r\equiv1$ means $(a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)\equiv0\pmod N$ —
$N$ divides the product yet (under the stated conditions) divides neither factor,
so $N$'s prime factors have been **split** between the two sides, and $\gcd$
(the Euclidean algorithm — blazingly fast) collects one from each.
For a random $a$ the conditions hold with probability at least $1/2$;
if they fail, pick another $a$ and try again.

**Hand-compute $N=15$ with $a=7$:**

$$
7^1=7,\quad 7^2=49\equiv4,\quad 7^3\equiv28\equiv13,\quad 7^4\equiv91\equiv1\pmod{15}
$$

The period is $r=4$ — even, and $7^2\equiv4\not\equiv-1$. Therefore

$$
\gcd(7^2-1,15)=\gcd(48,15)=3,\qquad
\gcd(7^2+1,15)=\gcd(50,15)=5
$$

$15=3\times5$ — success. **The only hard step left in the entire algorithm is finding the period
$r$.** Classically, period finding is no cheaper than factoring outright (you would have to grind
$f$ through trillions of steps before seeing a single cycle); this is precisely the step quantum
mechanics takes over.

## Second leg: loading the period into a superposition

Open with the DJ recipe: hit the first register ($t$ qubits, $Q=2^t\gg r^2$) with a row of $H$'s,
compute the modular exponential for every $x$ in parallel, and write it into the second register:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt Q}\sum_{x=0}^{Q-1}\ket{x}\ket{a^x\bmod N}
$$

Now measure the **second register** (you don't actually have to — it just makes the story easier
to tell), obtaining some value $f_0$. The first register thereupon collapses to the equal-weight
superposition of **all the $x$ that produce that value** — and these $x$ form exactly an
arithmetic progression $x_0,\ x_0+r,\ x_0+2r,\dots$:

$$
\ket{\Psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ket{x_0+jr},\qquad m\approx Q/r
$$

The period is now etched into the state, but a direct measurement would just spit out one random
$x_0+jr$ — the random starting point $x_0$ scrambles the period information (each rerun gives a
different $x_0$). The old problem: **the information went in but won't come out**. The old answer:
interference. This time the interference machine is the quantum Fourier transform.

## Third leg: the QFT turns the period into peaks

The **quantum Fourier transform** (QFT) is the deluxe version of DJ's row of $H$'s — upgrading
"bitwise phases" to "continuous phases":

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt Q}\sum_{y=0}^{Q-1}
\ee^{2\pi\ii xy/Q}\ket{y}
$$

(The phases of $H^{\otimes n}$ are only $\pm1$; the QFT's phases take $Q$ ticks around the unit
circle. It can be built from $O(t^2)$ gates — $H$'s plus controlled phase rotations; we skip the
circuit here.)

<Derivation title="Measuring after the QFT: why the peaks land on integer multiples of Q/r" level="进阶" minutes={9} open={true}>

**Step 1: apply the QFT to the periodic state.** Substitute $\ket{\Psi}$ into the definition:

$$
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{\Psi}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{mQ}}\sum_{y=0}^{Q-1}
\left[\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ee^{2\pi\ii(x_0+jr)y/Q}\right]\ket{y}
$$

**Step 2: factor out the random start.** Pull out the factor $\ee^{2\pi\ii x_0y/Q}$ —
it contributes only a phase and **vanishes in the modulus squared**: the pesky $x_0$ exits the
stage right here. The probability of measuring $y$ is

$$
P(y)=\frac{1}{mQ}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\ee^{2\pi\ii jry/Q}\right|^2
$$

**Step 3: see when the geometric series adds up.** The bracket is a geometric series with ratio
$\ee^{2\pi\ii ry/Q}$.

- If $ry/Q$ is **exactly an integer**, i.e. $y=k\,Q/r$: every term equals 1, the sum is $m$,
  and $P(y)=m/Q\approx1/r$ — a **sharp peak**;
- otherwise the phases march around the unit circle, closing on themselves and nearly cancelling:
  $P(y)\approx0$.

$r$ sharp peaks stand evenly spaced at $y=0,\ \tfrac{Q}{r},\ \tfrac{2Q}{r},\dots$, carrying total
probability about 1. (When $r$ does not divide $Q$ the peaks acquire a slight width and sit at the
nearest integers, but the conclusion stands — this is exactly why we demanded $Q\gg r^2$:
peak width much smaller than peak spacing.)

**Step 4: recover $r$ from the readout.** Measuring some $y\approx kQ/r$ means

$$
\frac{y}{Q}\approx\frac{k}{r}
$$

The left side is the known readout; the right side is the fraction we want. A **continued-fraction
expansion** finds the best rational approximation with denominator at most $N$, giving a candidate
for $r$ (if $k$ and $r$ share a factor you get a divisor of $r$; a rerun or two and a least common
multiple fixes that). Feed it back into the first leg's $\gcd$, and the factorization is done.

**Mini worked check ($N=15,a=7,r=4$, with $Q=16$)**: the peaks should sit at $y=0,4,8,12$.
Say we measure $y=12$: $\tfrac{12}{16}=\tfrac34$, the continued fraction gives $k/r=3/4$, so
$r=4$. ✓

</Derivation>

<div slot="left">

**Why this is interference's victory.** Compare the trilogy's three cases of "it went in but won't
come out": the $2^n$ parallel function values couldn't be read out (DJ used $H$ interference to
extract the parity), the target amplitude was too small to be read out (Grover used repeated
interference to snowball it), and the period was masked by a random starting point (Shor uses QFT
interference to filter out the start and amplify the period).
What the QFT does is exactly diffraction through a grating: slits arranged with period $r$,
bright fringes appearing at integer multiples of the reciprocal point $Q/r$ in the far field —
**Shor's algorithm is a diffraction experiment performed in number theory.**

</div>
<div slot="right">

The complexity ledger ($n=\log_2 N$ is the bit length):

$$
\text{Quantum (Shor)}:\ O(n^3)
$$

$$
\text{Classical (number field sieve)}:\ \exp\!\big[O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\big]
$$

$n=2048$: quantum, on the order of $10^{10}$ gate operations (at the logical level);
classical, about $10^{34}$ operations. Polynomial versus sub-exponential —
this is not a speedup; it is a different species.

<Callout type="insight" title="The timetable hanging over RSA">
The threat is real, but the accounting must be done in logical qubits. Breaking 2048-bit RSA takes
roughly **thousands of logical qubits** and trillions of gate operations; after the error-correction
overhead (Section 9.10), that converts to roughly **millions of physical qubits** on current
architectures — while machines of the mid-2020s hold only hundreds to thousands of physical qubits,
with several orders of magnitude of engineering in between.
Yet cryptographers must act **now**, because of "harvest now, decrypt later":
ciphertext intercepted and stored today can wait for the quantum computer of the future.
Hence NIST standardized quantum-resistant public-key algorithms in 2022–2024
(lattice-based Kyber/ML-KEM, Dilithium/ML-DSA, and others), and the migration is underway.
One comparison in passing: Grover only halves the strength of symmetric ciphers (AES-256 is fine),
while Shor is a break-in-principle against public-key systems (RSA, elliptic curves) —
two entirely different threat levels.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two popular misconceptions">
1. **"Shor has already factored big numbers"?** Experimental demonstrations to date are toy cases
   like 15 and 21, often with "answer known in advance" simplifications. They validate the
   principle, not the arrival of the threat.
2. **"The quantum computer tries every factor"?** No. From start to finish the algorithm never
   "trial-divides" anything: it **rewrites** factoring as a period problem, then reads the period
   out in one interference pass. Without that layer of number-theoretic structure, quantum
   parallelism would have nothing to grip — which explains once more why exponential speedup does
   not generalize to arbitrary hard problems (NP-complete problems enjoy no such treatment to this
   day).
</Callout>

## What comes next

Shor needs trillions of gate operations to run back-to-back without a slip, and real qubits are
delicate creatures: a one-percent gate error counts as excellent, and even left alone they quietly
drift. Classical computers correct errors with redundant backups — but quantum states cannot be
cloned (Section 9.5), and a measurement collapses them (Section 9.1) — **so how do you back up,
and how do you even notice an error?** The next section is this chapter's finale: quantum error
correction. Its answer is unexpectedly beautiful, and it is a grand reunion of everything this
chapter has built — entanglement, measurement, stable correlations.
