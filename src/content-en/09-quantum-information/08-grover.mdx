Deutsch–Jozsa won on an artificial problem. This section tackles a problem everyone understands —
**finding a needle in a haystack** — and this time the opponent is a genuine classical lower bound.

## The problem at the door: N drawers, one key

$N=2^n$ drawers, numbered $0$ to $N-1$, exactly one of which (call it $w$) hides the key.
You have a black box $f$: give it a number $x$ and it answers $f(x)=1$ if and only if $x=w$.
The only classical strategy is opening drawers: $N/2$ on average, $N$ in the worst case, with no
shortcut whatsoever — $f$ has no structure to exploit (this is not a sorted phone book; it is
shuffled).

The quantum oracle copies last section's phase kickback (the $\ket{-}$ ancilla is now left implicit):

$$
O\ket{x}=(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
O=I-2\ket{w}\bra{w}
$$

It does exactly one thing: **flip the sign of the target branch**.

## Why one round of interference no longer suffices

Try copying DJ's three moves: a row of $H$'s prepares the uniform superposition
$\ket{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_x\ket{x}$, call the oracle once, then interfere.
The trouble: the oracle flips the sign of only **one** of the $2^n$ amplitudes, and that
perturbation affects the overall distribution at order $O(1/\sqrt N)$ — one round of interference
cannot wash it out, and a direct measurement still hits with probability about $1/N$.
In DJ's problem, "all the function values conspiring" produced a stark global signal;
the search problem's signal is thread-thin. The way out: **stop hoping for a one-shot kill and let
a small advantage snowball**. Each round twists the amplitude pointing at the target a little
larger; twist for $\sqrt N$ rounds. This "amplitude amplifier" is built from two mirrors.

## The geometric picture: two mirrors = one rotation

All the action happens in a two-dimensional plane — the one spanned by the target state $\ket{w}$
and the uniform superposition $\ket{s}$. Take the in-plane unit vector perpendicular to $\ket{w}$,
$\ket{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}\sum_{x\ne w}\ket{x}$ (the "uniform superposition of the other
drawers"); then

$$
\ket{s}=\cos\theta\,\ket{r}+\sin\theta\,\ket{w},
\qquad \sin\theta=\braket{w}{s}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

The initial state $\ket{s}$ sits only a small angle $\theta\approx1/\sqrt N$ above the "ground"
$\ket{r}$. The Grover iteration has two steps:

1. **The oracle $O$**: flips the sign of the $\ket{w}$ component = a **reflection about $\ket{r}$** (mirror one);
2. **The diffusion operator** $D=2\ket{s}\bra{s}-I$: a **reflection about $\ket{s}$** (mirror two).

<Derivation title="Two reflections compose into one 2θ rotation" level="基础" minutes={7} open={true}>

**Step 1: confirm $O$ is a reflection.** For any in-plane state
$\ket{\psi}=\cos\phi\ket{r}+\sin\phi\ket{w}$:

$$
O\ket{\psi}=\cos\phi\ket{r}-\sin\phi\ket{w}
$$

The angle maps $\phi\mapsto-\phi$: precisely a reflection with $\ket{r}$ as the mirror.

**Step 2: confirm $D$ is a reflection.** $D=2\ket{s}\bra{s}-I$ satisfies
$D\ket{s}=\ket{s}$ (vectors on the mirror stay put) and negates the component perpendicular to
$\ket{s}$ — a reflection with $\ket{s}$ (at angle $\theta$) as the mirror: $\phi\mapsto2\theta-\phi$.

**Step 3: compose.** $O$ first, then $D$:

$$
\phi\ \xrightarrow{\ O\ }\ -\phi\ \xrightarrow{\ D\ }\ 2\theta+\phi
$$

**Two reflections = one rotation, by angle $2\theta$, toward $\ket{w}$.** This is an old theorem of
plane geometry (mirrors at angle $\theta$: two reflections make a rotation by $2\theta$), restaged
verbatim in Hilbert space.

**Step 4: iterate $k$ rounds.** Starting angle $\theta$, adding $2\theta$ per round:

$$
\phi_k=(2k+1)\theta,\qquad
P_{\text{hit}}(k)=\sin^2\big[(2k+1)\theta\big]
$$

We want $\phi_k\approx\pi/2$ (dead-on target), so

$$
k_{\text{opt}}\approx\frac{\pi/2-\theta}{2\theta}\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N
$$

(The last step uses $\theta\approx\sin\theta=1/\sqrt N$ for large $N$.)
$O(\sqrt N)$ queries, each round costing only two rows of $H$'s and a little phase work —
the quadratic speedup is in hand.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="The diffusion operator's nickname: 'inversion about the mean'">
Landed on the amplitudes, $D=2\ket{s}\bra{s}-I$ is plain speech. Let the post-query amplitudes be
$a_x$ with mean $\bar a=\frac1N\sum_x a_x$; then $D$ maps each amplitude to

$$
a_x\ \mapsto\ 2\bar a-a_x
$$

— **every amplitude is mirrored about the collective mean**. The target amplitude, flipped negative
by the oracle, sits far below the mean and rebounds to about $+3/\sqrt N$, in one stroke three times
above the crowd; the other amplitudes dip slightly to compensate. Each round the target amplitude
gains a net $\approx 2/\sqrt N$, growing to $O(1)$ after $\sqrt N$ rounds. "Reflection" and
"inversion about the mean" are the geometric and arithmetic tongues for the same thing.
</Callout>

## Hand-compute N = 4: one iteration, a perfect hit

$N=4$ (two qubits), target $w=10$. Here $\sin\theta=\tfrac12$, $\theta=30^\circ$ —
not a "small" angle, which lets us hand-compute a whole round.

**Step 0:** $\ket{s}=\tfrac12(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})$,
all four amplitudes $0.5$.

**Step 1 (oracle):** the target flips sign:

$$
\big(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ \tfrac12\big)
$$

**Step 2 (inversion about the mean):** the mean is $\bar a=\frac{0.5+0.5-0.5+0.5}{4}=\tfrac14$.
Each amplitude $\mapsto 2\times\tfrac14-a_x=\tfrac12-a_x$:

$$
\big(0,\ 0,\ 1,\ 0\big)
$$

The state has become exactly $\ket{10}$. Measure: **100% hit**, one query.
Classically it takes $2.25$ drawer-openings on average (3 in the worst case) to be sure.
Geometric check: $\phi_1=(2\times1+1)\times30^\circ=90^\circ$, dead-on target, to the degree.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Overshooting hurts.** The rotation has no automatic brake: past $90^\circ$ it keeps turning and
the hit probability **turns around and falls**. For $N=4$, a second round gives $\phi_2=150^\circ$,
$P=\sin^2150^\circ=25\%$ — back down to guessing level. Grover's algorithm must **count its laps
and stop**, the exact opposite of the classical instinct that "more computation can only make you
safer". Fortunately $P(k)=\sin^2[(2k+1)\theta]$ is a known sinusoid: at
$k=\lfloor\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N\rfloor$ the failure probability is at most $O(1/N)$, and a rerun or
two makes it negligible.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
P(k)=\sin^2\big[(2k+1)\theta\big],\qquad\sin\theta=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

$N=4$: $P(0)=0.25\to P(1)=1\to P(2)=0.25$.

$N=10^6$: $k_{\text{opt}}=\lfloor\tfrac{\pi}{4}\times10^3\rfloor=785$,
$P\approx1-10^{-6}$.

With $M$ targets, $\sin\theta=\sqrt{M/N}$ and
$k_{\text{opt}}\approx\tfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N/M}$ —
the more needles, the faster the fishing.

</div>
</DualView>

## Quadratic is the limit: the BBBV lower bound

Could a cleverer quantum algorithm manage $\log N$? **No.**
The Bennett–Bernstein–Brassard–Vazirani theorem: any quantum algorithm for unstructured search
needs at least $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ oracle queries. The idea is worth remembering:
each query perturbs the state by at most $O(1/\sqrt N)$ (it moves the sign of only one branch),
while pulling the final states for "the target is $w_1$" and "the target is $w_2$" apart to a
distinguishable distance requires a cumulative perturbation of $\Omega(1)$ — divide, and
$\Omega(\sqrt N)$ falls out. Grover flies flush against the bound; even the constant $\pi/4$ is
optimal.

<Callout type="warning" title="De-mystifying 'quantum search'">
1. **The speedup is quadratic, not exponential.** NP-hard problems do not become polynomial:
   the square root of $2^n$ is still $2^{n/2}$. For symmetric cryptography the implication is
   "double the key length" (brute-forcing AES-128 falls to around $2^{64}$, hence the post-quantum
   recommendation of AES-256), not "all ciphers are dead" — the latter is what next section's Shor
   does to **public-key** cryptography.
2. **The oracle is not free.** "One query" means one call to the reversible circuit for $f$;
   if $f$ itself is expensive, or a database must first be loaded into quantum-queryable form,
   that cost is extra. Grover's home turf is problems where "$f$ is easy to compute but solutions
   are hard to find" (satisfiability, collision finding, optimisation subroutines),
   not "searching a database" in the literal sense.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The quadratic speedup already touches the ceiling of black-box search; for exponential speedup we
must leave the black box behind and mine the problem's **internal structure**.
One problem's structure happens to be exactly what quantum mechanics does best — **periodicity**:
factoring large integers reduces to "finding the period of a function",
and finding periods is interference's signature trade. Next section: Shor's algorithm,
and the sword it dangles over RSA.
