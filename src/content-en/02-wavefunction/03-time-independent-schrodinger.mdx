The full Schrödinger equation is

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = \hat H\Psi(x,t),\qquad
\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x)
$$

a partial differential equation. The good news: when $V$ does not depend explicitly on time — which covers almost every textbook problem — **separation of variables** splits it into two ordinary differential equations, and one of them is completely trivial.

## Separating the variables

<Derivation title="From the time-dependent equation to the stationary one" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

Try a separable solution

$$
\Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,\varphi(t)
$$

and substitute:

$$
\ii\hbar\,\psi(x)\frac{\dd\varphi}{\dd t} = \varphi(t)\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}+V(x)\psi\right]
$$

Divide both sides by $\psi\varphi$:

$$
\underbrace{\ii\hbar\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{\dd\varphi}{\dd t}}_{\text{depends on }t\text{ only}}
=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\psi}\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}+V\psi\right]}_{\text{depends on }x\text{ only}}
$$

The left side is a function of $t$ alone, the right side of $x$ alone, and $x$ and $t$ are independent variables. A quantity depending only on $t$ can equal one depending only on $x$ only if **both equal the same constant**. Call that separation constant $E$ — it will turn out to be the energy.

**The time part**:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd\varphi}{\dd t}=E\varphi
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\varphi(t)=\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}
$$

(the integration constant is absorbed into $\psi$). This solution is completely universal and has nothing to do with the shape of the potential. All the physics lives in the spatial part.

**The spatial part**, which is the **time-independent Schrödinger equation**:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}+V(x)\psi = E\psi
$$

or, in operator form, $\hat H\psi = E\psi$.

</Derivation>

So a separable solution always looks like

$$
\Psi_n(x,t)=\psi_n(x)\,\ee^{-\ii E_n t/\hbar}
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="This is not an ordinary differential equation">
On the surface $\hat H\psi=E\psi$ is a second-order ODE, but it has an extra identity: it is an **eigenvalue problem**.

The difference is this. An ordinary differential equation has solutions for any value of its parameters; here we additionally demand that $\psi$ satisfy physical boundary conditions (normalisable, continuous, and so on). Those conditions can only be met simultaneously for particular values of $E$. The allowed values are the **energy levels** and the corresponding $\psi$ are the **energy eigenstates**.

**Quantisation of energy is not an extra assumption; it is a mathematical consequence of the boundary conditions.** This is one of the loveliest things in quantum mechanics: the quantisation conditions that Planck and Bohr had to insert by hand fall out of Schrödinger's framework on their own.
</Callout>

## Why E must be real

<Derivation title="Bound-state energy eigenvalues are real" level="进阶" minutes={4}>

Assume $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ with normalisable $\psi$. Take the complex conjugate (with $V$ real):

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi^{*\prime\prime}+V\psi^*=E^*\psi^*
$$

Multiply the first equation by $\psi^*$ and the second by $\psi$, then subtract; the $V$ terms cancel:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\psi^*\psi''-\psi\psi^{*\prime\prime}\right)=(E-E^*)|\psi|^2
$$

The bracket on the left is again a total derivative, $\frac{\dd}{\dd x}(\psi^*\psi'-\psi\psi^{*\prime})$. Integrating over all space turns the left side into a boundary term, which vanishes because $\psi$ is normalisable:

$$
0=(E-E^*)\int|\psi|^2\dd x = (E-E^*)\cdot 1
$$

Hence $E=E^*$, so $E$ is real.

This is no accident. Its general form is "the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real", redone in operator language in [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/) — in three lines. The long-winded integral proof here shows exactly that: **change the language and the same fact becomes much simpler.**

</Derivation>

Reality of $E$ has an immediate consequence: $|\varphi(t)|^2=|\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}|^2=1$, so the time factor has constant modulus. Therefore $|\Psi_n(x,t)|^2=|\psi_n(x)|^2$ does not change with time — which is where the name "stationary state" comes from. The next section says more.

## Reading a wavefunction from its curvature

You can see roughly what $\psi$ looks like without solving anything. Rewrite the stationary equation as

$$
\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}=\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left[V(x)-E\right]\psi
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Classically allowed region ($E>V$)**: $\psi''$ has the opposite sign to $\psi$, so the curve bends **towards the axis** — it oscillates. The larger $E-V$ (the larger the kinetic energy), the sharper the bending and the shorter the wavelength.

**Classically forbidden region ($E<V$)**: $\psi''$ has the same sign as $\psi$, so the curve bends **away from the axis** — exponential behaviour. Physically we keep only the decaying branch, or normalisation fails.

**Turning point ($E=V$)**: $\psi''=0$ and the curve changes its concavity there.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\psi''=\frac{2m(V-E)}{\hbar^2}\psi
$$

$E>V$: locally $\psi\sim\ee^{\pm\ii kx}$ with $k=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V)}}{\hbar}$

$E<V$: locally $\psi\sim\ee^{\pm\kappa x}$ with $\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V-E)}}{\hbar}$

These two approximations are the starting point of the [WKB method](/en/ch/07-approximation/04-wkb/).

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="This picture alone predicts quantisation">
Consider a potential well that traps a particle. Starting from far to the left, $\psi$ must decay exponentially towards zero. Entering the well it starts to oscillate; leaving the well it returns to exponential behaviour.

Here is the catch: the exponential solution on the right generally contains both a growing branch $\ee^{+\kappa x}$ and a decaying branch $\ee^{-\kappa x}$. For $\psi$ to go to zero far to the right as well, the coefficient of the growing branch must be tuned to **exactly** zero.

That is only possible for particular values of $E$, where the oscillation inside the well accumulates just the right phase to match both sides. This is where discrete levels come from. The larger $n$, the more half-wavelengths fit inside, the more nodes, and the higher the energy.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Why this section has no simulation">
This section is about a method (separation of variables) rather than a specific system, so the thing to visualise would be a solution procedure, not a physical quantity. The curvature intuition above can be checked directly in the simulations of sections [2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/) and [2.9](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/09-harmonic-oscillator/): change the quantum number and watch how the density of the bending inside the well tracks the energy. The genuinely general tool is the custom-potential solver in [section 4.5](/en/ch/04-1d-problems/05-custom-potential-solver/).
</Callout>

## What comes next

Separation of variables produces only a family of particular solutions. The genuinely general solution is a **superposition** of them — and superpositions behave nothing like stationary states. The next section returns to the full time-dependent equation.
