$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}V_0,&0<x<a\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad(V_0>0)
$$

A particle of energy $E<V_0$ comes in from the left. Classical mechanics says: it hits the wall, bounces back, and the transmission probability is exactly zero.

The quantum answer is not zero.

## Solving region by region

<Derivation title="Derivation: the transmission coefficient of a rectangular barrier" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**General solutions in the three regions** (for $E<V_0$):

$$
\psi_\text{I}=A\ee^{\ii kx}+B\ee^{-\ii kx}\quad(x<0),\qquad k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}
$$

$$
\psi_\text{II}=C\ee^{\kappa x}+D\ee^{-\kappa x}\quad(0<x<a),\qquad \kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}
$$

$$
\psi_\text{III}=F\ee^{\ii kx}\quad(x>a)
$$

$A$ is the incident wave, $B$ the reflected wave, $F$ the transmitted wave. Region III has no $\ee^{-\ii kx}$ term because there is no source on the right — **that is the only place where physical input enters**; everything else is mathematics.

<Callout type="warning" title="Both exponentials must be kept in region II">
When we handled bound states in [section 2.8](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/08-finite-well/) we threw away the growing exponential. Here it **must be kept**: region II has finite width, so $\ee^{\kappa x}$ is merely a finite number at $x=a$ and does nothing to normalisation.

Dropping this term is the single most common mistake in problems of this type.
</Callout>

**Matching conditions.** $V$ is finite, so $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous at both $x=0$ and $x=a$, giving four equations:

$$
A+B=C+D
$$

$$
\ii k(A-B)=\kappa(C-D)
$$

$$
C\ee^{\kappa a}+D\ee^{-\kappa a}=F\ee^{\ii ka}
$$

$$
\kappa\left(C\ee^{\kappa a}-D\ee^{-\kappa a}\right)=\ii kF\ee^{\ii ka}
$$

Four equations for four ratios ($B/A$, $C/A$, $D/A$, $F/A$) — solvable.

**Elimination.** The last two give $C$ and $D$:

$$
C\ee^{\kappa a}=\frac{F\ee^{\ii ka}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\ii k}{\kappa}\right),\qquad
D\ee^{-\kappa a}=\frac{F\ee^{\ii ka}}{2}\left(1-\frac{\ii k}{\kappa}\right)
$$

Substitute into the first two and eliminate $B$ (multiply the first by $\ii k$ and add the second):

$$
2\ii kA=(\ii k+\kappa)C+(\ii k-\kappa)D
$$

Insert $C$ and $D$ and tidy up:

$$
\frac{A}{F}=\ee^{\ii ka}\left[\cosh\kappa a+\frac{\ii}{2}\left(\frac{\kappa}{k}-\frac{k}{\kappa}\right)\sinh\kappa a\right]
$$

**Transmission coefficient** $T=|F/A|^2$:

$$
T^{-1}=\cosh^2\kappa a+\frac14\left(\frac{\kappa}{k}-\frac{k}{\kappa}\right)^2\sinh^2\kappa a
$$

Using $\cosh^2=1+\sinh^2$ and converting $k,\kappa$ back to $E,V_0$ (after some algebra):

$$
\boxed{\ T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}\ }
$$

</Derivation>

## The thick-barrier approximation

For $\kappa a\gg1$, $\sinh\kappa a\approx\frac12\ee^{\kappa a}$, so

$$
T\approx\frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\,\ee^{-2\kappa a}
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="The exponential factor decides everything">
The prefactor lies between 0 and 4 — a number of order one. **All the physics is in $\ee^{-2\kappa a}$.**

That makes $T$ **exponentially sensitive** to the barrier's width and height:

$$
\frac{\dd\ln T}{\dd a}=-2\kappa
$$

For an electron with $V_0-E=4$ eV, $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\approx1.02\ \text{Å}^{-1}$. Every extra ångström of barrier thickness costs a factor of $\ee^{-2}\approx1/7.4$ in $T$.

**This is exactly how the scanning tunnelling microscope works**: pull the tip 1 Å further from the surface and the tunnelling current drops by almost an order of magnitude. That extreme distance sensitivity is what lets an STM resolve height differences of single atoms.
</Callout>

<Derivation title="Orders of magnitude: three real examples" level="基础" minutes={6}>

**1. Scanning tunnelling microscope.** A metal work function $\phi\approx4$ eV and a tip–sample gap $a\approx5$ Å:

$$
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m\phi}}{\hbar}=\frac{\sqrt{2\times511000\times4}}{197.3}\ \text{nm}^{-1}=10.2\ \text{nm}^{-1}
$$

$$
2\kappa a=2\times10.2\times0.5=10.2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ee^{-10.2}\approx3.7\times10^{-5}
$$

A transmission probability around $10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}$. That sounds tiny, but around $10^{15}$ electrons try every second, so the tunnelling current is in the nanoamp range — comfortably measurable.

**2. Alpha decay.** An alpha particle is bound by the nuclear force inside a well of radius about 7 fm and has to get through the Coulomb barrier (peaking near 25 MeV, while the alpha's energy is only 4–9 MeV). Approximating the Coulomb barrier as a stack of rectangular barriers and summing (this is the [WKB approximation](/en/ch/07-approximation/04-wkb/)) gives

$$
\ln T\propto-\frac{Z}{\sqrt{E}}
$$

This is the **Gamow factor**, and it explains the Geiger–Nuttall law: a small change in alpha energy sweeps the half-life across **24 orders of magnitude** (14 billion years for $^{232}$Th versus 0.3 microseconds for $^{212}$Po). Exponential sensitivity taken to its extreme.

Gamow's 1928 calculation of alpha decay was one of the earliest major successes of quantum mechanics.

**3. Flash memory.** Writing to NAND flash pushes electrons by tunnelling through an oxide layer about 8 nm thick into the floating gate. Writing raises $T$ by applying a high voltage that "tilts" the barrier (Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling); once the voltage is removed, the same oxide is thick enough to keep the electrons in the floating gate for about ten years (data retention).

**Every single bit in your phone's storage chip is one controlled act of tunnelling.**

</Derivation>

## E > V₀: there is still reflection

The formula above stays valid for $E>V_0$; simply set $\kappa\to\ii k_2$ (with $k_2=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar$), which turns $\sinh$ into $\ii\sin$:

$$
T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sin^2(k_2a)}{4E(E-V_0)}\right]^{-1}
$$

Classically the particle slows down, crosses, and transmission is 100%. Quantum mechanically $T<1$: **there is reflection**.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**This should not really be a surprise.** Waves partially reflect at any abrupt change of medium: light going from air into glass reflects about 4%, and sound going from air into water reflects almost completely. An abrupt change in potential does exactly the same thing to a matter wave.

Reflection comes from **impedance mismatch**, and has nothing to do with whether the particle has enough energy to get over the top.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Resonant transmission.** When

$$
k_2a=n\pi\quad(n=1,2,\dots)
$$

we get $\sin(k_2a)=0$ and $T=1$ — the barrier becomes perfectly transparent.

The condition says the barrier width holds an exact whole number of half-wavelengths, so the reflections from the two interfaces cancel — exactly like an anti-reflection coating in optics.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="The Ramsauer–Townsend effect">
In 1921 Ramsauer and Townsend independently found that slow electrons passing through argon, krypton or xenon have a scattering cross-section that **drops nearly to zero** near a particular energy around 1 eV — the gas is almost transparent to electrons.

Classically inexplicable. The quantum answer is resonant transmission: a noble-gas atom acts on an electron like a potential **well**, and at particular energies the width of the well satisfies the resonance condition so that the reflections cancel.

This was among the earliest experimental evidence for quantum scattering theory. In the simulation below, set $V_0$ negative and switch to the transmission-curve view to see those $T=1$ spikes.
</Callout>

<Callout type="misconception" title="How long does tunnelling take?">
The question looks simple and has been argued over for seventy years without a fully settled answer.

The difficulty is that quantum mechanics has no time operator, so "how long the particle spent inside the barrier" cannot be defined directly. Different definitions (Wigner phase time, Büttiker–Landauer time, the Larmor clock, and others) give different answers, some of them apparently "superluminal" and independent of barrier width — though it can be shown that none of them can be used to send information, so causality is safe.

Around 2020 several ultracold-atom and attosecond experiments produced measured values, but the argument over what "tunnelling time" even means is not over. It is one of the few genuinely foundational questions still actively debated; see [section 13.7](/en/ch/13-frontier/07-foundations/).
</Callout>

## End of the chapter

Look back at what one complex-valued function and one equation accomplished:

- Derived energy quantisation (infinite well, harmonic oscillator) with no extra assumptions
- Explained why atoms are stable (stationary states do not radiate)
- Predicted a phenomenon classically forbidden outright (tunnelling), which now supports an entire field of technology
- Absorbed classical mechanics as a limiting case (Ehrenfest's theorem, the correspondence principle)

It also left a pile of things unexplained: why do observables correspond to operators? What actually happens in a measurement? What entitles $|c_n|^2$ to be a probability?

Answering those requires a different language. [Chapter 3](/en/ch/03-formalism/) abstracts the wavefunction into a vector in Hilbert space — after which you will find that most of the long integral derivations of this chapter take two or three lines.
