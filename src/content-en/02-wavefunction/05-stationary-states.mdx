<Term id="stationaryState">Stationary state</Term> is one of the most misread terms in the subject. The word *stationary* invites the thought "at rest". The particle is not at rest.

## What does not change in a stationary state

For $\Psi_n(x,t)=\psi_n(x)\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$:

$$
|\Psi_n(x,t)|^2=\psi_n^*(x)\psi_n(x)\underbrace{\ee^{+\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}}_{=1}=|\psi_n(x)|^2
$$

The probability density does not depend on time. More generally, for any observable $\hat A$ with no explicit time dependence, the expectation value

$$
\langle \hat A\rangle_n=\int\Psi_n^*\hat A\Psi_n\,\dd x=\int\psi_n^*\hat A\psi_n\,\dd x
$$

is time-independent too — the phase factors appear in pairs, in $\Psi^*$ and $\Psi$, and always cancel.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**What changes**: the wavefunction itself. The phase of $\Psi_n$ rotates uniformly at angular frequency $E_n/\hbar$. For the hydrogen ground state that is about $2\times10^{16}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ — extremely fast.

**What does not**: every observable. The probability density, $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle E\rangle$ — all constant.

So what is stationary is the **result of observation**, not the wavefunction, and certainly not the particle.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\Psi_n(x,t)=\psi_n(x)\,\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}
$$

$$
|\Psi_n|^2=|\psi_n|^2\quad(\text{independent of }t)
$$

$$
\frac{\dd\langle\hat A\rangle_n}{\dd t}=0\quad(\hat A\text{ without explicit }t)
$$

In a single stationary state the unobservability of the global phase is taken to an extreme: the phase spins constantly and nothing can detect it.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="misconception" title="Three common misreadings">
**"Stationary means the particle is at rest."** No. The ground state of an infinite well has kinetic energy $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2>0$ and $\langle p^2\rangle=2mE_1\ne0$. It is only $\langle p\rangle$ that vanishes, because left- and right-moving probabilities are equal.

**"Stationary means the ground state."** No. Every level $E_n$ has its stationary state $\psi_n$. The ground state is simply the stationary state of lowest energy.

**"Stationary states are the only physical states."** No. Most real states are superpositions. Stationary states matter because they form a **basis** in which any state can be expanded, not because they are somehow more real.
</Callout>

## Non-stationary states: the cross term does the work

As soon as two or more coefficients are non-zero, the probability density moves. The previous section computed the general two-state result:

$$
|\Psi|^2=|c_1|^2|\psi_1|^2+|c_2|^2|\psi_2|^2
+2|c_1||c_2|\,\psi_1\psi_2\cos\!\left(\omega_{21}t+\delta\right)
$$

(taking real $\psi_n$; $\delta$ is the initial phase difference between $c_1$ and $c_2$).

The first two terms are the individual densities, and they do not move. **All of the time dependence sits in the cross term**, oscillating at the Bohr frequency

$$
\omega_{21}=\frac{E_2-E_1}{\hbar}
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="Bigger gap, faster sloshing">
This gives a useful order-of-magnitude estimate: the characteristic evolution time of a superposition is $\tau\sim\hbar/\Delta E$.

- Electrons in an atom ($\Delta E\sim\text{eV}$): $\tau\sim10^{-15}\ \text{s}$, femtoseconds. That is why studying dynamics inside atoms requires femtosecond lasers.
- Nuclear spins in a 1 T field ($\Delta E\sim10^{-7}\ \text{eV}$): $\tau\sim10^{-8}\ \text{s}$, which is exactly why NMR works in the radio-frequency band.

One formula, eight orders of magnitude apart.
</Callout>

## Why atoms do not collapse

Classical electrodynamics makes a fatal prediction: an electron orbiting a nucleus is accelerating, must therefore radiate, and would lose all its energy and spiral into the nucleus in about $10^{-11}$ seconds. Matter should not exist.

Stationary states are the answer.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

For an electron in the ground state, the charge distribution $-e|\psi_1|^2$ **does not change with time at all**. A static charge distribution does not radiate — a point classical electrodynamics itself concedes.

The electron is not "going round the nucleus". The ground state $\psi_{100}$ is even spherically symmetric, with probability current $j$ vanishing everywhere. The orbiting picture of the Bohr model is simply wrong.

And there is no lower level to fall to, so the ground state is absolutely stable.

</div>
<div slot="right">

The deeper reason for that stability is the uncertainty principle:

$$
E\sim\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}
\gtrsim\frac{\hbar^2}{2mr^2}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}
$$

As $r\to0$ the first term ($\propto r^{-2}$) diverges faster than the second ($\propto r^{-1}$), so the total energy goes back up. The minimum sits at

$$
r\sim a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{me^2}=0.53\ \text{Å}
$$

**The Bohr radius is the balance point between the cost of confinement and the Coulomb gain.**

</div>
</DualView>
