$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}-V_0,&-a<x<a\\ 0,&\lvert x\rvert>a\end{cases}\qquad (V_0>0)
$$

We put the zero of energy outside the well, so bound states are those with $-V_0<E<0$.

<Callout type="insight" title="Guess the answer first">
Before doing any algebra, think about what will change:

1. Outside the well $E<V=0$, a classically forbidden region — but the wavefunction no longer has to vanish there; it **decays exponentially** outward. The particle has a non-zero probability of being found where it classically could never go.
2. Since the wavefunction leaks out, the effective width of confinement exceeds $2a$, and from $E\propto1/L^2$ we should expect levels **below** those of the infinite well.
3. The well is only $V_0$ deep, so it can hold only a **finite number** of bound states.
</Callout>

## Solving region by region

<Derivation title="Full solution: the even-parity bound states" level="进阶" minutes={10} open={true}>

The potential is symmetric, $V(-x)=V(x)$, so the eigenstates have definite parity (the reason is in [section 4.1](/en/ch/04-1d-problems/01-symmetry-parity/); we simply use it here). Start with even parity.

**The regions.** Write

$$
k=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}}{\hbar}\ (\text{inside}),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}\ (\text{outside})
$$

A bound state $-V_0<E<0$ guarantees that $k$ and $\kappa$ are both positive real.

- Inside ($|x|<a$): $\psi''=-k^2\psi$; the even solution is $\psi=D\cos kx$
- Right ($x>a$): $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$; normalisability keeps only $\psi=F\ee^{-\kappa x}$
- The left side is fixed automatically by even parity

**Matching conditions.** $V$ is finite, so both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ must be continuous at $x=a$:

$$
D\cos ka=F\ee^{-\kappa a}
$$

$$
-Dk\sin ka=-\kappa F\ee^{-\kappa a}
$$

**Eliminate the constants.** Divide the second by the first (a standard trick that kills both unknown coefficients at once):

$$
k\tan ka=\kappa
$$

That is the condition determining the energy levels.

**Non-dimensionalise.** Let

$$
z\equiv ka,\qquad z_0\equiv\frac{a}{\hbar}\sqrt{2mV_0}
$$

$z_0$ is the **only** dimensionless parameter; it packages depth and width into a single number. From the definitions,

$$
(ka)^2+(\kappa a)^2=\frac{2mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2}=z_0^2
\;\Longrightarrow\;\kappa a=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}
$$

Substituting gives the **transcendental equation**

$$
\tan z=\frac{\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}}{z}
$$

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="This equation has no closed-form solution">
$\tan z=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}/z$ has no solution in elementary functions. That is not for lack of cleverness — transcendental equations are usually like this.

There are two ways to proceed: **graphically** (count the solutions and estimate where they are) and **numerically** (root-finding). Most "solvable" models in quantum mechanics end up here; the infinite well and the harmonic oscillator, with their tidy closed forms, are the exceptions.
</Callout>

## Graphical method: counting the bound states

Treat each side as a function of $z$ and plot them together:

- $y_1=\tan z$: a family of branches diverging at $z=\pi/2,\ 3\pi/2,\ \dots$
- $y_2=\sqrt{z_0^2-z^2}/z$: falls monotonically from $+\infty$ as $z\to0$ and vanishes at $z=z_0$

The number of intersections is the number of even-parity bound states.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

$y_2$ only exists for $0<z<z_0$. The $n$-th rising branch of $\tan z$ starts at $z=(n-1)\pi$. So every time $z_0$ crosses an integer multiple of $\pi$, one more even-parity bound state appears.

For odd parity (redo everything with $\psi=D\sin kx$) the condition becomes $-k\cot ka=\kappa$, and a new bound state appears whenever $z_0$ crosses an odd multiple of $\pi/2$.

**Together**, the total number of bound states is

$$
N=\left\lceil \frac{2z_0}{\pi}\right\rceil
$$

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Key limits**

$z_0\to\infty$ (very deep and wide): the intersections approach the poles of $\tan z$, $z\to n\pi/2$, so $k\to n\pi/2a$ — exactly the infinite well of width $2a$. **The limit checks out.**

$z_0\to0$ (very shallow and narrow): the $y_2$ curve collapses towards the origin, but $\tan z$ also goes to 0 as $z\to0$, and $\tan z\approx z$ rises more slowly than $y_2$ falls — so **there is always at least one intersection**.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="A 1D well always has at least one bound state">
This is a peculiarity of one dimension: **an arbitrarily shallow symmetric well still binds at least one state**.

Three dimensions is completely different — there the well must be deep enough to bind anything at all. The difference has real consequences: the deuteron (proton + neutron) has exactly one bound state, and it very nearly fails to exist, with a binding energy of only 2.2 MeV. The strength of the nuclear force sits right near the critical point, and that fact matters enormously for nucleosynthesis.

The 1D-versus-3D contrast belongs to a general theme: dimensionality controls how fast probability gets diluted. [Section 6.2](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/02-radial-equation/) revisits it through the effective potential.
</Callout>

## Penetration depth

Outside the well $\psi\propto\ee^{-\kappa|x|}$, with characteristic decay length

$$
\delta=\frac{1}{\kappa}=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m\lvert E\rvert}}
$$

The more weakly bound the state (the smaller $|E|$), the further it leaks. This $\delta$ is the key quantity for understanding tunnelling: if a barrier's thickness is comparable to $\delta$, the wavefunction can emerge on the far side — [section 2.11](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/11-tunneling/) works that out quantitatively.

<Derivation title="Worked example: a concrete well" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

Take an electron, $a=0.5\ \text{nm}$ (well width 1 nm), $V_0=5\ \text{eV}$.

$$
z_0=\frac{a\sqrt{2mV_0}}{\hbar}=\frac{a}{\hbar c}\sqrt{2(mc^2)V_0}
=\frac{0.5}{197.3}\sqrt{2\times511000\times5}
$$

$$
=\frac{0.5}{197.3}\times2260=5.73
$$

Number of bound states: $N=\lceil 2\times5.73/\pi\rceil=\lceil3.65\rceil=4$.

**Ground state.** Solving $\tan z=\sqrt{5.73^2-z^2}/z$ on $(0,\pi/2)$ numerically gives $z\approx1.31$, so

$$
E+V_0=\frac{\hbar^2z^2}{2ma^2}=\frac{(\hbar c)^2z^2}{2(mc^2)a^2}
=\frac{197.3^2\times1.31^2}{2\times511000\times0.25}\ \text{eV}\approx0.26\ \text{eV}
$$

$$
E\approx-4.74\ \text{eV}
$$

**Compare with the infinite well** of width $2a=1$ nm: $E_1^{\infty}=0.376$ eV (the numeric quiz question of the previous section), **higher** than the 0.26 eV here. Just as expected: the wavefunction leaks out, the effective width grows, and the energy drops.

**Penetration depth**: with $|E|=4.74$ eV,

$$
\delta=\frac{\hbar c}{\sqrt{2(mc^2)\lvert E\rvert}}=\frac{197.3}{\sqrt{2\times511000\times4.74}}\approx0.09\ \text{nm}
$$

About 0.9 Å, close to the size of an atom. So if two such wells are placed within 2 Å of each other their wavefunctions overlap appreciably — which is exactly where chemical bonding and band formation begin.

</Derivation>
