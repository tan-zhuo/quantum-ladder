With $|\Psi|^2$ in hand we have a probability distribution. What remains is statistics: compute means, compute fluctuations.

## Expectation values

The definition of an <Term id="expectation">expectation value</Term> comes straight from probability theory:

$$
\langle x\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,\dd x
$$

<Callout type="misconception" title="An expectation value is not the most likely result">
A double-peaked distribution makes the point: a particle has a 50% chance of being near $x=-a$ and 50% near $x=+a$, so $\langle x\rangle=0$ — and $x=0$ is exactly where the probability is **lowest**.

An expectation value is the **average over many repeated measurements**, not a prediction for any single one. And it must be stressed: one measurement each on **many identically prepared copies**, not repeated measurements on the same particle — after the first measurement that particle's state has changed ([section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/)).
</Callout>

## Where the momentum operator comes from

The expectation value of position is easy to write. What about momentum? We do not know what "the particle's momentum distribution" looks like. But there is one clue: $\langle p\rangle$ ought to equal $m\,\dd\langle x\rangle/\dd t$.

<Derivation title="Deriving the momentum operator from d⟨x⟩/dt" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

$$
\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=\int x\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2\,\dd x
$$

Use the continuity equation $\partial_t|\Psi|^2=-\partial_x j$ from [section 2.2](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/02-normalization-and-current/):

$$
\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=-\int x\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}\dd x
$$

Integrate by parts; the boundary term vanishes because $\Psi$ is normalisable:

$$
=\int j\,\dd x=\int\frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}-\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)\dd x
$$

Integrate the first term by parts once more (moving the derivative from $\Psi^*$ onto $\Psi$) and combine:

$$
\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=-\frac{\ii\hbar}{m}\int\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\,\dd x
$$

Hence

$$
\langle p\rangle=m\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=\int\Psi^*\left(-\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\Psi\,\dd x
$$

and the thing in brackets is the **momentum operator**:

$$
\hat p=-\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
$$

It was not postulated; it was derived — given only the Born rule, the Schrödinger equation, and the classical correspondence "momentum = mass × velocity".

</Derivation>

In general, the operator for a classical quantity $A(x,p)$ is obtained by the substitution $x\to x$, $p\to-\ii\hbar\partial_x$, and its expectation value is

$$
\langle \hat A\rangle=\int\Psi^*\,\hat A\,\Psi\,\dd x
$$

Note that $\hat A$ must sit **between** $\Psi^*$ and $\Psi$ — the operator acts on the $\Psi$ to its right, and the order cannot be shuffled.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why there is an i in the momentum operator">
On a momentum eigenstate $\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}$, the operator $-\ii\hbar\partial_x$ returns the eigenvalue $p$:

$$
-\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}=p\,\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}
$$

That $\ii$ is exactly what cancels the $\ii$ produced by differentiating. It also guarantees that $\hat p$ is **Hermitian**, so its eigenvalues are real — drop the $\ii$ and the eigenvalues become purely imaginary and useless as momenta.

The de Broglie relation $p=\hbar k$ becomes a direct consequence of the definition.
</Callout>

## Variance and standard deviation

$$
\sigma_A^2=\langle \hat A^2\rangle-\langle \hat A\rangle^2
$$

$\sigma_A$ measures the **spread of measurement outcomes**. In particular $\sigma_A=0$ if and only if $\Psi$ is an eigenstate of $\hat A$ — in which case every measurement returns the same value.

<Derivation title="Example: σₓ and σ_p for the infinite-well ground state" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

The ground state is $\psi_1=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi x/L)$ on $[0,L]$.

**Position**: by symmetry $\langle x\rangle=L/2$.

$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x^2\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\dd x
=\frac{2}{L}\int_0^L x^2\cdot\frac{1-\cos(2\pi x/L)}{2}\dd x
$$

The first term gives $\frac{L^2}{3}$; the second, after two integrations by parts, gives $-\frac{L^2}{2\pi^2}$. Together,

$$
\langle x^2\rangle=L^2\left(\frac13-\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\right)
$$

$$
\sigma_x^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\frac{L^2}{4}=L^2\left(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\right)
\approx0.0326\,L^2
\;\Longrightarrow\;\sigma_x\approx0.181\,L
$$

**Momentum**: $\langle p\rangle=0$ (for a real wavefunction $\int\Psi\,\partial_x\Psi\,\dd x=\tfrac12\big[\Psi^2\big]$ evaluated at the boundaries, where $\Psi$ vanishes; physically, left- and right-moving components are equally likely). And

$$
\langle p^2\rangle=2m\langle T\rangle=2mE_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;\sigma_p=\frac{\pi\hbar}{L}
$$

**The product**:

$$
\sigma_x\sigma_p\approx0.181L\cdot\frac{\pi\hbar}{L}=0.568\,\hbar\approx1.14\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

Larger than $\hbar/2$, and fairly close to the bound. Note that $L$ **cancels completely**: shrink the box and $\sigma_x$ shrinks proportionally while $\sigma_p$ grows proportionally, leaving the product untouched. You can see this directly in the simulation below.

</Derivation>

## The uncertainty principle

$$
\sigma_x\,\sigma_p\ \ge\ \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

The rigorous proof needs the generalised uncertainty relation ([section 3.6](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/)). What matters here is **what it actually says**.

<Callout type="misconception" title="This is not about measurement disturbance">
The popular story runs: "to see where the electron is you must shine short-wavelength light on it, the photon kicks momentum into the electron, and so the momentum becomes uncertain."

That story — Heisenberg's microscope — has historical value, but as an explanation of the inequality it is **wrong**, for a simple reason.

$\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_p$ are two statistical properties of **the same wavefunction**. You can compute both from $\Psi$ with pen and paper without measuring anything at all — which is exactly what the derivation above did. The inequality holds before you decide what to measure.

The correct statement: a wavefunction with small $\sigma_x$ (narrow in space) **necessarily** contains a broad range of wave numbers in its Fourier expansion, hence a large $\sigma_p$. That is a theorem of Fourier analysis, equally true for sound waves and light pulses — it is why an ultrashort laser pulse necessarily has a broad spectrum. The distinctively quantum ingredient is only $p=\hbar k$, which translates "spread in wave number" into "uncertainty in momentum".
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**How to read the inequality**

It is a statement about **states**, not about **instruments**.

It says: there is no quantum state whose position and momentum distributions are both arbitrarily narrow. However good your apparatus, and whether or not you measure at all, the constraint stands.

The states that saturate it are **Gaussian wave packets**, which is why Gaussians occupy a special place in quantum mechanics ([section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/)).

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

The standard order-of-magnitude estimate:

$$
\Delta p\sim\frac{\hbar}{\Delta x},\qquad
E\sim\frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m}\sim\frac{\hbar^2}{2m(\Delta x)^2}
$$

Confine a particle to a length $L$ and the zero-point kinetic energy is $\sim\hbar^2/2mL^2$. The infinite well's $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$ differs from that estimate only by $\pi^2$.

</div>
</DualView>

Drag the well width $L$ and watch $\Delta x/L$ in the readout: it is a **constant**, about 0.181. So $\sigma_x\propto L$, while energy $\propto1/L^2$ means $\sigma_p\propto1/L$. The product is independent of $L$ — the inequality is equally tight for a box of any size.

## Ehrenfest's theorem: the return of classical mechanics

<Derivation title="⟨x⟩ and ⟨p⟩ obey Newton's equations" level="进阶" minutes={5}>

We already have the first relation:

$$
\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=\frac{\langle p\rangle}{m}
$$

A similar computation for $\langle p\rangle$ (differentiate in time, substitute the Schrödinger equation, integrate by parts) gives

$$
\frac{\dd\langle p\rangle}{\dd t}=\left\langle-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle=\langle F\rangle
$$

Together these are Newton's second law for expectation values.

**But look carefully at the right-hand side**: it is $\langle F(x)\rangle$, not $F(\langle x\rangle)$. In general these differ. Expanding $F$ about $\langle x\rangle$:

$$
\langle F(x)\rangle=F(\langle x\rangle)+\frac12 F''(\langle x\rangle)\,\sigma_x^2+\cdots
$$

Only when the force is approximately linear across the width of the packet ($F''\approx0$), or the packet is narrow enough ($\sigma_x$ small), do the two agree and the expectation value genuinely follow a classical trajectory.

This explains **when classical mechanics is a good approximation**: for macroscopic objects $\sigma_x$ is absurdly small and everyday force fields are extremely smooth on that scale. It also explains when it is not: for an electron crossing a double slit the packet width is comparable to the slit separation, and the $F''$ term is not remotely negligible.

</Derivation>
