The Born rule requires

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,\dd x = 1
$$

This is a strong constraint, and it has to hold **at every instant**. The Schrödinger equation tells us how $\Psi$ changes with time — what guarantees that this integral does not?

If it could change, quantum mechanics would be finished: particles would appear and disappear as time went on. This section proves that it cannot, and digs out a useful new quantity along the way.

## Normalisation

Given a solution $\Psi$, linearity of the equation guarantees that $A\Psi$ is also a solution for any complex constant $A$. Choose $A$ so that the integral equals 1:

$$
|A|^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi|^2\,\dd x = 1
$$

<Callout type="note" title="The global phase is free">
That equation fixes only $|A|$, not the phase of $A$. In other words $\Psi$ and $\ee^{\ii\theta}\Psi$ describe **exactly the same physical state** — identical $|\Psi|^2$, identical expectation values for everything.

This is the statement that the **global phase is unobservable**. But note carefully: *relative* phases are observable. $\psi_1+\psi_2$ and $\psi_1+\ee^{\ii\pi}\psi_2$ are completely different states, with exactly opposite interference patterns.
</Callout>

Not every function can be normalised. For $\int|\Psi|^2$ to converge, $\Psi$ must go to zero fast enough as $x\to\pm\infty$. Such functions are called **square-integrable** and form the Hilbert space $L^2$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Plane waves cannot be normalised">
For $\Psi = A\ee^{\ii kx}$ the modulus squared is the constant $|A|^2$ and the integral diverges. Strictly speaking it is not a legitimate physical state.

It is far too useful to discard, though. The trick is to use it as a **basis** in which to expand genuinely normalisable wave packets, which is exactly what [section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) does. "Non-normalisable solutions are still useful" is a pattern that recurs throughout quantum mechanics.
</Callout>

## Normalisation does not change with time

<Derivation title="Proof that d/dt ∫|Ψ|²dx = 0" level="基础" minutes={6}>

We want to show that

$$
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,\dd x = 0
$$

The limits do not depend on time, so the derivative can move inside the integral and become a partial derivative:

$$
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int|\Psi|^2\dd x = \int \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\Psi^*\Psi\right)\dd x
= \int\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi\right)\dd x
$$

Now use the Schrödinger equation (take its form on trust here; section 2.4 discusses it properly):

$$
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}V\Psi
$$

Take the complex conjugate — and note that $V$ being a *real* function is essential here:

$$
\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\ii}{\hbar}V\Psi^*
$$

Substituting back, the two terms containing $V$ have opposite signs and **cancel outright**:

$$
\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi
=\frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi\right)
$$

What is left inside the bracket happens to be a total derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)
=\Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi
$$

(the cross terms $\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}$ appear twice and cancel). Hence

$$
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int|\Psi|^2\dd x
=\frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\left[\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
$$

and normalisability requires $\Psi\to 0$ (with bounded derivative) as $|x|\to\infty$, so the boundary term vanishes. ∎

**What did the proof rely on?** Only on $V$ being **real**. Introduce a complex potential — which condensed-matter and nuclear physics really do, to describe absorption phenomenologically — and the normalisation decays with time. That decay *is* the mathematics of absorption. The absorbing boundary layer in the wave-packet simulations on this site uses exactly this trick.
</Derivation>

## The probability current

The combination that appeared in that proof is important enough to deserve a name. Define the **probability current density**

$$
j(x,t) \equiv \frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}-\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)
=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)
$$

(the two forms are equivalent; the second is easier to remember and to compute with). The proof above then amounts to

$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}=0
$$

which is the **continuity equation**, identical in form to the ones in fluid mechanics and electrodynamics.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

Think of $\rho$ as the density of an incompressible fluid and $j$ as its flux. The continuity equation says: however much "probability" a small interval loses, exactly that much must have **flowed** out through its ends. Probability is never created or destroyed, only moved.

That is also why normalisation is conserved: conservation of total probability is the global version of a local conservation law.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\int_a^b\rho\,\dd x = j(a,t)-j(b,t)
$$

The left side is the rate of change of probability inside the interval; the right side is "flux in at the left minus flux out at the right".

For a stationary state ($\Psi=\psi\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}$ with real $\psi$) we get $j\equiv 0$ — not because nothing moves, but because the leftward and rightward currents cancel exactly.

</div>
</DualView>

<Derivation title="Example: the current of a plane wave" level="基础" minutes={3}>

Take $\Psi = A\ee^{\ii(kx-\omega t)}$. Then

$$
\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}=\ii k\Psi,\qquad
\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}=\ii k|A|^2
$$

$$
j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\ii k|A|^2)=\frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2
$$

Since $\hbar k/m = p/m = v$,

$$
j = \rho\, v
$$

— density times velocity, exactly "flux = density × speed". This matches classical fluid intuition perfectly.

It also shows in passing that if $\psi$ is a **real** function (an infinite-well stationary state, say), then $\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi$ is real, its imaginary part vanishes, and $j = 0$. In a stationary state the distribution does not move and neither does the current.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="misconception" title="j = 0 does not mean the particle is at rest">
An infinite-well stationary state has $j\equiv 0$, yet the particle's kinetic energy is $E_n>0$, so it is plainly not at rest.

The correct reading: the stationary state $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ can be written as a sum of two counter-propagating plane waves, $\frac{1}{2\ii}(\ee^{\ii n\pi x/L}-\ee^{-\ii n\pi x/L})$. The rightward and leftward currents are equal and opposite, so the net current is zero. It is a standing wave on a string fixed at both ends: the string moves, but no energy is transported.
</Callout>

## What counts as a legitimate wavefunction

Not every mathematical function will do. The requirements come from two directions: the Born rule must be usable, and the Schrödinger equation must make sense.

| Requirement | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Square-integrable, $\int\lvert\Psi\rvert^2\dd x<\infty$ | Otherwise it cannot be normalised and the Born rule fails |
| $\Psi$ single-valued | $\lvert\Psi\rvert^2$ must give a unique probability |
| $\Psi$ continuous | Otherwise $\partial_x\Psi$ contains a delta function and the kinetic energy diverges |
| $\partial_x\Psi$ continuous (for finite potentials) | Follows from integrating the Schrödinger equation; it may jump where the potential is infinite or contains a delta function |

<Callout type="warning" title="The last row has exceptions, and they are common">
At the walls of an infinite well, and at the location of a delta potential, $\partial_x\Psi$ **is allowed** to jump.

This is not a loophole granted by hand; it follows from the equation itself. Integrate the Schrödinger equation across the discontinuity over a width $\varepsilon\to 0$ and the jump in $\partial_x\Psi$ is proportional to $\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon} V\Psi\,\dd x$. For finite $V$ that integral goes to zero as $\varepsilon\to0$; for $V=\alpha\,\delta(x)$ it equals $\alpha\Psi(0)$, which does not. The wall of an infinite well is best treated as the $V_0\to\infty$ limit of a finite well.

This will be needed immediately in sections [2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/) and [2.8](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/08-finite-well/).
</Callout>
