$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}0,&0<x<L\\ \infty,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

A particle trapped between two infinitely high walls. It is the simplest bound-state problem there is, and it already contains energy quantisation, zero-point energy, node structure, orthogonality and completeness — the entire grammar of quantum mechanics makes its first appearance here.

## Solving it

<Derivation title="The full solution: from equation to energy levels" level="基础" minutes={8} open={true}>

**Step 1: fix the domain.**

Outside the well $V=\infty$. The stationary equation demands $\psi''=\frac{2m(V-E)}{\hbar^2}\psi$, and as $V\to\infty$ the only finite solution is $\psi\equiv0$. The particle is absolutely never found outside the well.

Continuity of the wavefunction then gives the boundary conditions

$$
\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0
$$

(Note: $\psi'$ is **not** required to be continuous here, because $V$ is infinite — see the table at the end of [section 2.2](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/02-normalization-and-current/).)

**Step 2: solve inside the well.**

Inside, $V=0$:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}=E\psi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}=-k^2\psi,\qquad k\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}
$$

(A bound state requires $E>0$: if $E<0$ then $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$ with solutions $\ee^{\pm\kappa x}$, which cannot vanish at both ends.) The general solution is

$$
\psi(x)=A\sin kx+B\cos kx
$$

**Step 3: impose the boundary conditions.**

$\psi(0)=0$ immediately gives $B=0$. That leaves

$$
\psi(L)=A\sin kL=0
$$

Taking $A=0$ gives the trivial everywhere-zero solution (unnormalisable, and no particle). So we must have

$$
kL=n\pi,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots
$$

**Why does $n$ start at 1?** $n=0$ gives $\psi\equiv0$; negative $n$ merely flips the overall sign of $\psi$, which is the same physical state (a global phase). Only the positive integers count.

**Step 4: the energy levels.**

From $k_n=n\pi/L$ and $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$:

$$
E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots
$$

**Step 5: normalisation.**

$$
1=|A|^2\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\dd x=|A|^2\cdot\frac{L}{2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;A=\sqrt{\frac2L}
$$

(taking $A$ positive real; the phase is arbitrary). Finally

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
$$

</Derivation>

## Reading the result

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Standing waves in a box.** The boundary conditions $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$ are exactly those of a string clamped at both ends, so the eigenstates are the string's harmonics: the well must hold an integer number of half-wavelengths, $L=n\lambda/2$.

Combine that with de Broglie's $\lambda=h/p$ and you get $p_n=nh/2L$ at once; then $E=p^2/2m$ reproduces the energy formula above. **Quantisation = the standing-wave condition.**

**Nodes.** $\psi_n$ has $n-1$ zeros inside the well. Higher energy means sharper bending (curvature $\propto E$) and more nodes. This "number of nodes = n − 1" rule holds generally for one-dimensional bound states.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad
E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}
$$

$$
E_n=n^2E_1,\qquad E_{n+1}-E_n=(2n+1)E_1
$$

Orthonormality:

$$
\int_0^L\psi_m\psi_n\,\dd x=\delta_{mn}
$$

(Verify directly with a product-to-sum identity: for $m\ne n$ both terms integrate over a whole number of periods and vanish.)

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Zero-point energy: the particle cannot simply lie down">
$E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2>0$. Even the lowest state has kinetic energy; the particle can never be at rest.

The reason is the uncertainty principle: confinement to a width $L$ means $\sigma_x\sim L$, hence $\sigma_p\gtrsim\hbar/2L$ and a kinetic energy $\gtrsim\hbar^2/8mL^2$ — off the exact answer only by a numerical factor.

The scaling $E_1\propto1/L^2$ turns up everywhere in physics: the colour of light emitted by a quantum dot as its size changes, conductance quantisation in nanowires, and the degeneracy pressure that holds a neutron star up against gravity all run on the same $1/L^2$.
</Callout>

## Expanding an arbitrary initial state

Because $\{\psi_n\}$ is complete, any initial state satisfying the boundary conditions can be expanded:

$$
\Psi(x,0)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x),\qquad
c_n=\sqrt{\frac2L}\int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\Psi(x,0)\,\dd x
$$

<Derivation title="Example: a particle initially uniform over the left half" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

Take

$$
\Psi(x,0)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{2/L},&0<x<L/2\\0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

(already normalised: $\frac{2}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{2}=1$). The coefficients are

$$
c_n=\sqrt{\frac2L}\cdot\sqrt{\frac2L}\int_0^{L/2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\dd x
=\frac2L\cdot\frac{L}{n\pi}\left[1-\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}\right]
=\frac{2}{n\pi}\left[1-\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}\right]
$$

Term by term:

| $n$ | $\cos(n\pi/2)$ | $c_n$ | $\lvert c_n\rvert^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0 | $2/\pi$ | 0.405 |
| 2 | $-1$ | $2/\pi$ | 0.405 |
| 3 | 0 | $2/3\pi$ | 0.045 |
| 4 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 0 | $2/5\pi$ | 0.016 |

The first two terms already account for 81%. Check: $\sum|c_n|^2=\frac{4}{\pi^2}(1+1+\frac19+0+\frac1{25}+\cdots)=1$ (use $\sum_{k\,\text{odd}}1/k^2=\pi^2/8$).

**What does an energy measurement give?** $E_1$ with probability 40.5%, $E_2$ with 40.5%, $E_3$ with 4.5%, and so on. Note $c_4=0$: the overlap of $\psi_4$ with this initial state happens to vanish exactly.

**Evolution.** The $E_1$ and $E_2$ components carry almost half the weight each, so the probability density sloshes between the two halves at

$$
\omega_{21}=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2mL^2}
$$

Set $c_1=c_2$ in the simulation above and watch it happen.

</Derivation>

## The correspondence principle

<Callout type="insight" title="The large-n limit">
A classical particle bounces back and forth at constant speed, so it spends equal time everywhere — the classical probability density is uniform, $P_\text{cl}(x)=1/L$.

The quantum answer is $|\psi_n|^2=\frac2L\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, oscillating between 0 and $2/L$. The two look nothing alike. But note:

$$
\overline{|\psi_n|^2}=\frac2L\cdot\overline{\sin^2}=\frac2L\cdot\frac12=\frac1L=P_\text{cl}
$$

**The average of the oscillation is exactly the classical result.** And the larger $n$ is, the finer the fringes, so any measurement of finite resolution sees only the average. That is the concrete mechanism of the correspondence principle: quantum effects do not "disappear", they get averaged away.

In the simulation, push $n$ up to 12 and imagine a detector with resolution $L/10$ — you would measure a flat line.
</Callout>

The relative level spacing supports the same conclusion:

$$
\frac{E_{n+1}-E_n}{E_n}=\frac{2n+1}{n^2}\xrightarrow{\ n\to\infty\ }0
$$

At large $n$ the relative gap between neighbouring levels goes to zero and the spectrum looks continuous — precisely the classical picture.

<Callout type="warning" title="Limitations of this model">
The infinite well is an idealisation, and it departs from real systems in two places:

1. **Infinitely high walls do not exist.** Real wells have finite depth, the wavefunction leaks outside, and the number of bound states is finite. See [section 2.8](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/08-finite-well/).
2. **$\psi'$ is discontinuous at the walls.** That is an artefact of $V=\infty$ and never happens for any finite potential.

Even so, its qualitative predictions for quantum wells, quantum dots and carbon nanotubes — especially $E\propto1/L^2$ — are often accurate enough to compare directly with experiment.
</Callout>
