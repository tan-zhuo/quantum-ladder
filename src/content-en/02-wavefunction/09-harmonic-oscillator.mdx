$$
V(x)=\frac12 m\omega^2x^2
$$

## Why a parabola

Expand any potential $V(x)$ about a minimum at $x_0$:

$$
V(x)=V(x_0)+\underbrace{V'(x_0)}_{=0}(x-x_0)+\frac12V''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2+\cdots
$$

The linear term vanishes at a minimum, and the constant can be absorbed into the zero of energy. **The leading surviving term is always quadratic.** Set $m\omega^2\equiv V''(x_0)$ and you have a harmonic oscillator.

So any time you study "small vibrations" you are doing harmonic-oscillator physics: the vibrational spectra of diatomic molecules, phonons in a crystal, every mode of the electromagnetic field, LC circuits, the approximate description of a superconducting qubit… the list goes on.

<Callout type="insight" title="The oscillator's role in quantum field theory">
A free quantum field is exactly **a collection of infinitely many independent harmonic oscillators** — one per momentum mode. A photon is nothing but one quantum of excitation of one mode's oscillator.

That is why the $n$ in $E_n=(n+\frac12)\hbar\omega$ can be read either as "the $n$-th excited state" or as "$n$ particles". [Second quantisation in section 8.5](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/05-second-quantization/) makes that correspondence official.
</Callout>

## The ladder-operator method

Grinding through the differential equation (series solution plus Hermite polynomials) certainly works, but the algebraic route is faster and far more illuminating.

<Derivation title="Derivation: from a commutator to the entire spectrum" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Step 1: factorise the Hamiltonian.**

$$
\hat H=\frac{\hat p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2
$$

If $\hat p$ and $\hat x$ were ordinary numbers we could write $p^2+(m\omega x)^2=(m\omega x-\ii p)(m\omega x+\ii p)$. Operators do not commute, so the factorisation is slightly off — off by exactly the commutator. Define

$$
\hat a_\pm=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar m\omega}}\left(\mp\ii\hat p+m\omega\hat x\right)
$$

**Step 2: compute the commutator.** Using $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$:

$$
\hat a_-\hat a_+=\frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}\left(\hat p^2+(m\omega\hat x)^2-\ii m\omega[\hat x,\hat p]\right)
=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}+\frac12
$$

Similarly $\hat a_+\hat a_-=\frac{\hat H}{\hbar\omega}-\frac12$. Subtracting,

$$
[\hat a_-,\hat a_+]=1
$$

and

$$
\hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a_+\hat a_-+\frac12\right)
$$

**Step 3: show that they raise and lower the energy.** Suppose $\hat H\psi=E\psi$ and consider $\hat a_+\psi$:

$$
\hat H(\hat a_+\psi)=\hbar\omega\left(\hat a_+\hat a_-+\tfrac12\right)\hat a_+\psi
$$

Use $\hat a_-\hat a_+=\hat a_+\hat a_-+1$ to move $\hat a_+$ all the way left:

$$
=\hbar\omega\,\hat a_+\left(\hat a_-\hat a_++\tfrac12\right)\psi
=\hat a_+\left[\hbar\omega\left(\hat a_+\hat a_-+\tfrac12\right)+\hbar\omega\right]\psi
=(E+\hbar\omega)(\hat a_+\psi)
$$

So $\hat a_+\psi$ is also an eigenstate, with energy higher by $\hbar\omega$. Likewise $\hat a_-\psi$ has energy lower by $\hbar\omega$. **Hence "ladder operators".**

**Step 4: the ladder must have a bottom rung.** Applying $\hat a_-$ repeatedly would lower the energy indefinitely. But the energy is bounded below:

$$
E=\langle\hat H\rangle=\frac{\langle\hat p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\langle\hat x^2\rangle>0
$$

(both terms are expectation values of non-negative operators, and they cannot vanish together). The only escape from the contradiction is a ground state $\psi_0$ satisfying

$$
\hat a_-\psi_0=0
$$

**Step 5: solve for the ground state.** This is a first-order equation, far easier than the original second-order one:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar m\omega}}\left(\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd x}+m\omega x\right)\psi_0=0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\frac{\dd\psi_0}{\psi_0}=-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\,\dd x
$$

$$
\psi_0(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\ee^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}
$$

(the prefactor comes from normalisation). The ground state is a **Gaussian** — a fact that becomes important again in the next section.

**Step 6: the ground-state energy.** From $\hat H=\hbar\omega(\hat a_+\hat a_-+\frac12)$ with $\hat a_-\psi_0=0$:

$$
E_0=\frac12\hbar\omega
$$

**Step 7: the whole spectrum.** Climb from the bottom:

$$
\psi_n=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\left(\hat a_+\right)^n\psi_0,\qquad
E_n=\left(n+\frac12\right)\hbar\omega,\quad n=0,1,2,\dots
$$

The entire solution used only the commutation relation $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$ and **never once actually solved the second-order differential equation**.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Equally spaced levels.** This is unique to the oscillator. The infinite well's spacing grows with $n$, hydrogen's shrinks with $n$; only the oscillator keeps a constant $\hbar\omega$.

Consequence: the oscillator's spectrum is **a single line** (every adjacent transition has the same frequency). Real molecules show several vibrational lines, which is precisely the evidence that the well is not exactly parabolic — anharmonicity is a rich source of information in molecular spectroscopy.

**Zero-point energy.** $E_0=\hbar\omega/2\ne0$. Even at absolute zero the oscillator still jitters. This is no theoretical ornament: helium stays liquid at atmospheric pressure all the way down because the zero-point motion is violent enough to prevent freezing, and the Casimir force comes from the zero-point energy of electromagnetic field modes.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
E_n=\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega
$$

$$
\psi_n(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^nn!}}H_n(\xi)\,\ee^{-\xi^2/2}
$$

with $\xi=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$ and $H_n$ the Hermite polynomials:

$$
H_0=1,\quad H_1=2\xi,\quad H_2=4\xi^2-2,\quad H_3=8\xi^3-12\xi
$$

The recurrence $H_{n+1}=2\xi H_n-2nH_{n-1}$ is what this site's simulations use to evaluate them (see [appendix C](/en/ch/appendix-c-numerics/)).

</div>

<Callout type="insight" title="Zero-point energy cannot simply be defined away">
Someone will object: the zero of energy is arbitrary anyway, so why not just subtract $\hbar\omega/2$?

For a single isolated oscillator, fine. But **changes** in the zero-point energy are observable:

- The Casimir effect: changing the separation of two metal plates changes which field modes are allowed, hence the total zero-point energy, producing a measurable force (verified precisely in 1997).
- Isotope effects: $\omega\propto1/\sqrt{\mu}$, so deuterium has a lower zero-point energy than hydrogen, giving C–H and C–D bonds different dissociation energies and reaction rates differing by factors of several. This is a routine tool in chemistry.

What is physically meaningful is always an energy **difference**, and the zero-point energy varies with the parameters — so it cannot be defined away.
</Callout>

## Tails: leaking into the classically forbidden region

A classical particle with energy $E_n$ can only reach the turning point $x_n=\sqrt{2E_n}/\omega\sqrt{m}$. Beyond that the quantum wavefunction decays as $\ee^{-\xi^2/2}$, but it is not zero.

<Derivation title="Compute it: the probability of finding the ground state in the forbidden region" level="进阶" minutes={5}>

For the ground state $E_0=\hbar\omega/2$, the turning point follows from $\frac12m\omega^2x_0^2=\frac12\hbar\omega$:

$$
x_0=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \xi_0=1
$$

(in the dimensionless coordinate $\xi=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$ the turning points sit exactly at $\xi=\pm1$).

$$
P_{\text{forbidden}}=2\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\ee^{-\xi^2}\dd\xi
=1-\operatorname{erf}(1)=1-0.8427=0.157
$$

**About 15.7%.** That is not a small number — a ground-state particle has roughly a one-in-six chance of being found where it classically could never be.

What about excited states? The fraction shrinks as $n$ grows (about 6% at $n=10$), because the classically allowed region widens while the decay length of the tail stays put. Once again, the quantum effect weakens in relative terms as $n$ grows — the correspondence principle.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="misconception" title='"The particle borrows energy to get out there" is the wrong story'>
The common explanation runs: the particle "borrows" an energy $\Delta E$ from the vacuum, sneaks out for a time $\Delta t\sim\hbar/\Delta E$, and pays it back.

That invokes the energy–time uncertainty relation, but $\Delta t$ in quantum mechanics is not an observable corresponding to any operator; the meaning of that relation is quite different from $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$, and the analogy does not hold.

The cleaner statement: **finding the particle in the forbidden region cannot be distinguished from "measuring a definite negative kinetic energy"** in any operational sense. The moment you localise the particle inside the forbidden region to within $\Delta x\lesssim1/\kappa$, the uncertainty principle hands it a momentum spread $\Delta p\gtrsim\hbar\kappa$, whose kinetic energy is enough to cover the potential deficit. In other words, the contradictory scenario of "measuring negative kinetic energy" simply cannot be set up experimentally.
</Callout>
