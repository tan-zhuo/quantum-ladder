The previous section produced a family of particular solutions, $\Psi_n=\psi_n\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$. But physically you cannot expect an initial state to be exactly one of them. Real initial states are arbitrary — "the particle is confined to the left half of the well", say. What then?

The answer is hidden in one property of the equation: **linearity**.

## Superposition and the general solution

The Schrödinger equation is linear: if $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ are solutions, so is $c_1\Psi_1+c_2\Psi_2$. So we may stack infinitely many stationary states:

$$
\Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,\ee^{-\ii E_n t/\hbar}
$$

This is the general solution. **Fix the coefficients $c_n$ and you know $\Psi$ at every future time.**

<Callout type="insight" title="The causal structure of quantum mechanics">
The Schrödinger equation is **first order** in time. That means $\Psi(x,0)$ alone determines $\Psi(x,t)$ uniquely — no "initial velocity" is required.

Compare Newton's equation $m\ddot x=F$, which is second order and needs both an initial position and an initial velocity. In quantum mechanics the wavefunction carries all the information by itself.

It is also why the evolution of a quantum state is **completely deterministic**. Indeterminacy enters at **measurement**, not during evolution — two things that are constantly conflated.
</Callout>

## Finding the coefficients

The stationary states $\psi_n$ form a **complete orthonormal basis** (proved in [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/); used here on trust):

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_m^*(x)\psi_n(x)\,\dd x=\delta_{mn}
$$

<Derivation title="Extracting cₙ with orthogonality" level="基础" minutes={4}>

At $t=0$,

$$
\Psi(x,0)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)
$$

Multiply both sides by $\psi_m^*(x)$ and integrate over all space:

$$
\int\psi_m^*\Psi(x,0)\,\dd x=\sum_n c_n\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,\dd x=\sum_n c_n\delta_{mn}=c_m
$$

so that

$$
c_n=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_n^*(x)\,\Psi(x,0)\,\dd x
$$

This manoeuvre is called the **Fourier trick**. It works entirely because orthogonality annihilates every term in the sum but one. In Dirac notation it becomes $c_n=\braket{\psi_n}{\Psi(0)}$, which reads simply as "take the component" — exactly isomorphic to projecting a three-dimensional vector onto a basis vector.

</Derivation>

The coefficients also satisfy

$$
\sum_n|c_n|^2=1
$$

<Derivation title="Proof that Σ|cₙ|² = 1" level="进阶" minutes={3}>

$$
1=\int|\Psi|^2\dd x=\int\left(\sum_m c_m\psi_m\right)^{\!*}\left(\sum_n c_n\psi_n\right)\dd x
=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\,\dd x
$$

$$
=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\,\delta_{mn}=\sum_n|c_n|^2
$$

Note that the result is time-independent: including the time factors sends $c_n\to c_n\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$, which leaves the moduli untouched. So **if the initial state is normalised, it stays normalised forever** — the same conclusion reached in [section 2.2](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/02-normalization-and-current/) via the continuity equation, obtained here with far less work.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="What |cₙ|² is">
$|c_n|^2$ is the **probability of obtaining $E_n$ when you measure the energy**.

This section cannot prove that — it belongs to the measurement postulate ([section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/)). But you can already check its consistency: $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$ says exactly that "measuring the energy always returns some $E_n$".

Moreover $\langle E\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$ does not change with time (because the $|c_n|$ do not), which is the quantum version of **energy conservation**.
</Callout>

## A complete example

<Derivation title="Example: an initially localised state in an infinite well" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

Take an infinite square well of width $L$, whose stationary states are (see [section 2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/))

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}
$$

Let the initial state be an equal-weight superposition of the two lowest states:

$$
\Psi(x,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_1(x)+\psi_2(x)\right]
$$

so $c_1=c_2=1/\sqrt2$ and everything else vanishes. Evolving,

$$
\Psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_1\ee^{-\ii E_1t/\hbar}+\psi_2\ee^{-\ii E_2t/\hbar}\right]
$$

and the probability density is

$$
|\Psi|^2=\frac12\left[\psi_1^2+\psi_2^2+2\psi_1\psi_2\cos\frac{(E_2-E_1)t}{\hbar}\right]
$$

<strong>Look carefully at the cross term.</strong> The first two terms do not move; the third oscillates at angular frequency

$$
\omega_{21}=\frac{E_2-E_1}{\hbar}=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2mL^2}
$$

so the probability density **sloshes from side to side** inside the well with period $T=2\pi\hbar/(E_2-E_1)$.

Three things worth noting:

1. The frequency depends only on the **level difference**, not on where the energy zero is. Shift every $E_n$ by a constant and nothing physical changes — the choice of zero really is arbitrary.
2. If only one $c_n$ is non-zero (a pure stationary state) there is no cross term and $|\Psi|^2$ is static.
3. This $\omega_{21}$ is exactly the frequency at which the system absorbs or emits a photon ([section 7.5](/en/ch/07-approximation/05-time-dependent-perturbation/)). **Bohr's frequency condition $\Delta E=\hbar\omega$ has appeared on its own.**

</Derivation>

## Why first order in time

Schrödinger actually wrote down a relativistic second-order equation first — later called the Klein–Gordon equation — and retreated to the non-relativistic first-order form only after finding that it gave the wrong fine structure for hydrogen.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

Being first order in time buys two crucial properties:

- **Probability conservation** works. The conserved quantity of the second-order equation is not positive definite and cannot be read as a probability — this is precisely the "negative probability" difficulty of the Klein–Gordon equation.
- **The initial condition is just $\Psi(x,0)$**, which makes the notion of a state very clean.

The price is that the equation is second order in space and first order in time, so **space and time are not on the same footing** — unacceptable relativistically, and eventually fixed by the Dirac equation.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=\hat H\Psi
$$

Formal solution:

$$
\Psi(t)=\hat U(t)\Psi(0),\qquad \hat U(t)=\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}
$$

$\hat U$ is **unitary** ($\hat U^\dagger\hat U=1$), which is the operator version of probability conservation. See [section 3.9](/en/ch/03-formalism/09-time-evolution-pictures/).

The relativistic generalisation is [chapter 12](/en/ch/12-relativistic/).

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="misconception" title="The i is not optional">
You will occasionally read that the $\ii$ in the equation is a convention. It is not.

Replace the $\ii$ on the left of $\ii\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\hat H\Psi$ by $-1$ and, for a free particle, you have a **diffusion equation**, $\partial_t\Psi=\frac{\hbar}{2m}\partial_x^2\Psi$ (simply deleting the $\ii$ gives the opposite sign: the backward diffusion equation, whose initial-value problem is ill-posed). Its solutions decay exponentially; they do not oscillate and they do not interfere. That equation describes ink spreading in water, not an electron.

The $\ii$ turns exponential solutions from $\ee^{-\lambda t}$ (decay) into $\ee^{-\ii\omega t}$ (oscillation), and all wave behaviour and interference follows from that. Incidentally, substituting $t\to-\ii\tau$ really does turn the Schrödinger equation into a diffusion equation; that trick is called Wick rotation and it underpins quantum Monte Carlo and the [path integral](/en/ch/11-path-integral/).
</Callout>
