Last section, parity marshalled the bound states into a tidy even-odd-alternating queue. But think back to the scattering experiments of [section 2.11](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/11-tunneling/): a wave packet hits a barrier, part bounces back, part gets through, we computed the transmission $T$ and reflection $R$, and the story seemed complete.

Was it? Consider a loose end left hanging there — the **Ramsauer–Townsend effect**: slow electrons passing through xenon atoms find, at certain energies, $T=1$; the gas turns nearly transparent to them. $T=1$ means "everything got through". Does that mean at this energy the potential did **nothing at all** to the electron?

Not so cheap. The wave got through, yes, but it came out with its waveform **bodily displaced** — the crests are not where they would have been had there been no potential. Transmission is completely blind to this: $T$ compares only the moduli of amplitudes and throws all the phase information away. To see this invisible hand of the potential, we need a new quantity. It is called the **phase shift**.

## First, tell the two kinds of state apart

The energy eigenstates of a one-dimensional potential problem fall naturally into two classes, handled in entirely different ways:

- **Bound states**: energy below the potential's value at infinity. The wavefunction decays exponentially at both ends, can be normalised, and the energies take **discrete** values — the wells of chapter 2 and last section's parity classification are all about these.
- **Scattering states**: energy above the potential at infinity. The wavefunction oscillates as a plane wave far away, **does not decay and cannot be normalised**, and every $E$ is allowed — the spectrum is **continuous**.

<Callout type="warning" title="Scattering states are non-normalisable, but not pathological">
$\int|\ee^{\ii kx}|^2\dd x=\infty$: a single scattering state genuinely does not represent a real particle. The way out was already paved in [section 3.3](/en/ch/03-formalism/03-hilbert-space/): treat them as generalised basis vectors, normalised to a delta function; a real particle is always a superposition of them — a **wave packet**. What flies across the simulation below is exactly a wave packet.

Happily, a packet sharp enough in energy behaves as the single-$k$ solution dictates, so solving the plane-wave problem remains the fastest route to the physics.
</Callout>

The question changes accordingly. For bound states we asked "which energies are allowed". A scattering state can have any energy, so we ask instead: **a wave of given energy goes in — what has changed when it comes out?**

The full content of the answer is surprisingly small.

## The cleanest stage: the half-axis problem

First reduce the problem until only one number is left. Imagine the particle confined to the half-axis $x>0$ (an impenetrable wall at $x=0$), with a square well of width $a$ and depth $V_0$ against the wall:

$$
V(x)=\begin{cases}-V_0,&0<x<a\\0,&x>a\end{cases}
$$

This "wall plus short-range potential" stage looks special, but it is precisely the core geometry of three-dimensional scattering from [chapter 5](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/) onward — the radial equation of a 3D problem lives on a half-axis, with the origin playing the wall. Learn it now, reuse it wholesale later.

**With no potential**, the solution at energy $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ must vanish at the wall:

$$
\psi_{\text{free}}(x)\propto\sin(kx)
$$

**With the potential**, the potential acts only for $x<a$; beyond that stretch the equation is identical to the free case and the solution is still a sine wave — but there is no longer any reason for it to happen to "line up" at $x=0$. Its most general form is

$$
\psi(x)\propto\sin(kx+\delta),\qquad x>a
$$

This angle $\delta$ is the **phase shift**: the presence of the potential has translated the outside waveform bodily by a distance $\delta/k$. The potential did not change the wavelength (energy is conserved; the wave comes out with the same $k$), and in steady state it did not change the amplitude either. **The only thing it can do is slide the waveform** — everything a short-range potential does to a wave of given energy is condensed into this one number.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The sign talks.** In an attractive potential (a well) the wavenumber is larger and the wavelength shorter; the wave "runs a few extra laps" inside the well and emerges with its phase advanced: the waveform is **pulled toward** the potential, $\delta>0$.

In a repulsive potential (a barrier) the wave amplitude is suppressed, and the waveform is **pushed away** from it, $\delta<0$. A hard repulsive core is the extreme case: an impenetrable hard sphere of radius $b$ shoves the whole wave out by $b$, giving $\delta=-kb$.

In one sentence: **watch whether the waveform got pulled in or pushed out, and by how much, and you have read off the potential's character and strength.**

</div>
<div slot="right">

Split $\sin(kx+\delta)$ into incoming and outgoing travelling waves:

$$
\sin(kx+\delta)=\frac{1}{2\ii}\left[\ee^{\ii(kx+\delta)}-\ee^{-\ii(kx+\delta)}\right]
$$

Factoring out the common phase, the ratio of the outgoing wave $\ee^{\ii kx}$ to the incoming wave $\ee^{-\ii kx}$ is

$$
S(k)=\ee^{2\ii\delta(k)}
$$

This is the simplest **scattering matrix** (S-matrix). $|S|=1$ holds automatically — the wall swallows no particles, what goes in comes out, and conservation of probability current pins $S$ to the unit circle, leaving only the rotation angle $2\delta$ free to vary.

</div>
</DualView>

## The full derivation: δ(k) for the square well

<Derivation title="Derivation: phase shift of the half-axis square well" level="进阶" minutes={10} open={true}>

**Step 1: write the solutions in the two regions.**

Inside the well ($0<x<a$) the kinetic energy is larger, with wavenumber

$$
k'=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}}{\hbar}>k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}
$$

The solution must vanish at the wall, so inside it can only be

$$
\psi_{\text{in}}(x)=A\sin(k'x)
$$

Outside, by definition, we write

$$
\psi_{\text{out}}(x)=B\sin(kx+\delta)
$$

**Step 2: join them at $x=a$.** $V$ is finite, so both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous:

$$
A\sin(k'a)=B\sin(ka+\delta)
$$

$$
Ak'\cos(k'a)=Bk\cos(ka+\delta)
$$

**Step 3: divide the two equations to eliminate the amplitudes.** We do not care about $A,B$ (continuum normalisation is a separate story) — only about the shape. Dividing gives

$$
k'\cot(k'a)=k\cot(ka+\delta)
$$

The left side is all known quantities — this is "the potential passing its information outward through the logarithmic derivative": the outside waveform is required to connect at $x=a$ with a prescribed slope-to-value ratio.

**Step 4: solve for δ.** Invert the relation into a tangent:

$$
\tan(ka+\delta)=\frac{k}{k'}\tan(k'a)
$$

$$
\boxed{\ \delta(k)=-ka+\arctan\!\left[\frac{k}{k'}\tan(k'a)\right]\ }
$$

**Check 1: no potential.** With $V_0=0$ we have $k'=k$, the bracket becomes $\tan(ka)$, and $\delta=-ka+ka=0$. No potential, no phase shift. Correct.

**Check 2: shallow well, low energy.** For small $V_0$, $k'\approx k$; expanding to first order gives $\delta>0$ and proportional to $V_0$: an attractive potential gives a positive phase shift, and the stronger the potential the harder it pulls. Matches intuition.

**Plug in real numbers** (an electron, $\hbar c=197.3$ eV·nm, $mc^2=511$ keV): take $V_0=1.0$ eV, $a=0.2$ nm, $E=0.5$ eV.

$$
k=\frac{\sqrt{2\times511000\times0.5}}{197.3}=3.62\ \text{nm}^{-1},\qquad
k'=\frac{\sqrt{2\times511000\times1.5}}{197.3}=6.28\ \text{nm}^{-1}
$$

$ka=0.725$, $k'a=1.255$, $\tan(k'a)=3.06$, so

$$
\delta=-0.725+\arctan(0.577\times3.06)=-0.725+1.056=0.331\ \text{rad}\approx19°
$$

A positive shift — the well pulls the waveform in by about $\delta/k\approx0.09$ nm, roughly half the well width.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep an eye on the arctan branch">
The period of $\tan$ is $\pi$, so the $\arctan$ above can only hand back a principal value in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ at a time, and $\delta$ is born with a "mod $\pi$" ambiguity. The standard convention is to define $\delta(k)$ as a **continuous function** of $k$: start from the high-energy end with $\delta(\infty)=0$ and track downward in energy, which amounts to adding $n\pi$ to the principal value, where $n$ is the number of odd multiples of $\pi/2$ that $k'a$ has passed. In the example above, $k'a=1.255$ has not yet reached $\pi/2\approx1.571$, so the principal value happens to be right.

The $\delta(0)$ pinned down this way carries a beautiful bonus — see Levinson's theorem below.
</Callout>

## Parity returns the favour: scattering on the full line

The half-axis problem has only reflection, no transmission. Back on the full line with a symmetric potential (the square barriers and wells of section 2.11), waves can come in from the left or from the right, and the problem looks twice as complicated. Here last section's parity makes good on its promise: **it splits the scattering into two channels that never disturb each other.**

<Derivation title="Derivation: transmission T = cos²(δₑ − δₒ)" level="进阶" minutes={9}>

**Step 1: change basis.** In a symmetric potential $[\hat H,\hat P]=0$; the continuum is degenerate, but the two states at each energy can always be combined into one even and one odd (exactly what the warning box in section 4.1 said). Outside the range of the potential, the even and odd solutions are

$$
\psi_{\ee}(x)\propto\cos(k|x|+\delta_{\ee}),\qquad
\psi_{\text{o}}(x)\propto\operatorname{sgn}(x)\sin(k|x|+\delta_{\text{o}})
$$

Each channel is a half-axis problem of its own: the odd channel vanishes at the origin (the $\sin$ type — precisely the derivation above, with condition $\psi(0)=0$); the even channel is flat there (the $\cos$ type, condition $\psi'(0)=0$). Each carries its own phase shift, $\delta_{\ee}$ and $\delta_{\text{o}}$, and neither meddles with the other.

**Step 2: decompose "incident from the left" into the two channels.** A plane wave coming from the left is half even, half odd:

$$
\ee^{\ii kx}=\cos kx+\ii\sin kx
$$

**Step 3: each channel does exactly one thing.** Within a channel, scattering just means "multiply the outgoing part by $\ee^{2\ii\delta}$". Split the $\cos$ and $\sin$ into incoming and outgoing travelling waves, attach each outgoing term its own $\ee^{2\ii\delta}$, then recombine the channels: collect the coefficient of $\ee^{\ii kx}$ in the region $x>a$ (transmission) and of $\ee^{-\ii kx}$ in $x<-a$ (reflection), giving

$$
t=\frac{\ee^{2\ii\delta_{\ee}}+\ee^{2\ii\delta_{\text{o}}}}{2},\qquad
r=\frac{\ee^{2\ii\delta_{\ee}}-\ee^{2\ii\delta_{\text{o}}}}{2}
$$

**Step 4: read the result.** After factoring out the common phase, the moduli depend only on the difference of the shifts:

$$
T=|t|^2=\cos^2(\delta_{\ee}-\delta_{\text{o}}),\qquad
R=|r|^2=\sin^2(\delta_{\ee}-\delta_{\text{o}})
$$

$T+R=1$ holds **automatically** — no need for the closing algebraic miracle of section 2.11; current conservation is now guaranteed by the structure.

**Numerical check**: a symmetric square well of width $2a=0.4$ nm and depth 1.0 eV, at $E=0.5$ eV. The even-channel formula follows by the same route (swap the $\tan$ for the form matching that channel's condition): $\delta_{\ee}=0.660$, $\delta_{\text{o}}=0.331$, hence $T=\cos^2(0.329)=0.896$. The transmission formula of section 2.11, $T=[1+V_0^2\sin^2(2k'a)/4E(E+V_0)]^{-1}$, gives $0.896$ — identical to the last digit.

</Derivation>

Two numbers, $(\delta_{\ee},\delta_{\text{o}})$, hold $T$, $R$, and all the phase information they leave out — the **whole** of the scattering. $T=1$ is no longer mysterious: it only requires $\delta_{\ee}-\delta_{\text{o}}$ to be an integer multiple of $\pi$, while each channel separately may have moved the wave a long way. The "transparent" xenon atoms of the Ramsauer–Townsend effect in fact stamp a substantial phase shift onto the electron wave — in cold-atom experiments such shifts have been measured directly.

## Resonance: the sharp turn in δ

Plot $\delta(E)$ as a curve, and over most stretches it varies gently. But near certain energies it **surges by about $\pi$** within a very narrow window — this is a **resonance**. The standard local shape (the Breit–Wigner form) is

$$
\tan\delta=\frac{\Gamma/2}{E_R-E}
$$

As $E$ sweeps through the resonance energy $E_R$, $\delta$ passes through $\pi/2$ (mod $\pi$), with a width set by $\Gamma$.

<Callout type="insight" title="A resonance = a state that almost made it to bound">
Why does δ suddenly surge? Because the potential can almost hide a bound state — a "quasi-bound state": the particle gets in, is trapped for a while, and rattles around before leaking out. During the rattling the wave winds up nearly a full half-turn of extra phase inside the potential, so δ jumps by π.

"How long it rattles" can be quantified: the **Wigner time delay** τ = 2ħ dδ/dE. The steeper δ becomes, the longer the particle lingers; at the peak of a resonance τ = 4ħ/Γ, the same order as the quasi-bound lifetime ħ/Γ — the narrower the line, the longer the lifetime. This is the scattering version of the alpha-decay picture from section 2.11: an alpha particle is a particle stuck in an extremely narrow resonance, with Γ so small the lifetime can reach tens of billions of years.

And there is a theorem that stitches bound states and scattering together for good — **Levinson's theorem**: with the continuity convention, the zero-energy phase shift satisfies δ(0) = n_b π, where n_b is the number of bound states in that channel (this is the form for the odd channel and the half-line problem; the one-dimensional even channel generically loses half a turn: δₑ(0) = (n_b − ½)π). In other words, **count how many turns the scattering waveform has been pulled in at infinity, and you have counted the bound states hiding in the potential**. Last section and this one were two halves of a single story all along.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Phase shifts and transmission — we can now compute both, as long as the potential is **one** square well or barrier. Real potentials are rarely so obliging: a resonant-tunneling diode is a three-layer barrier-well-barrier stack, and semiconductor superlattices run to hundreds of layers. With the method of section 2.11, every added layer means one more interface and two more matching equations — a hundred layers means solving several hundred simultaneously. Doing that by hand is suicide.

The next section brings in an assembly-line tool: package each slab of potential as a $2\times2$ matrix, and the whole multilayer structure becomes a **product of matrices**. The razor-sharp resonance in the double barrier — that $T$ rocketing from 0.04 to 1 — will be its first trophy.
