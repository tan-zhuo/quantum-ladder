Last section ended by saying it plainly: real potentials are smooth, and a staircase of piecewise-constant slabs is no long-term plan. And looking back at chapter 2, the potentials we can solve **exactly** can be counted on one hand — infinite well, finite well, harmonic oscillator, square barrier — each cracked by its own bespoke trick (a standing-wave condition, a transcendental equation, ladder operators). Change the potential even slightly — two wells of unequal depth, say, or a tilted floor from an applied field — and every trick fails at once.

But [chapter 3](/en/ch/03-formalism/) wrote down the way out long ago, even if it sounded like philosophy at the time: **wavefunctions are components of a vector, operators are matrices** ([section 3.1](/en/ch/03-formalism/01-from-wavefunction-to-vector/), [section 3.4](/en/ch/03-formalism/04-linear-operators/)). The "matrices" there were infinite-dimensional and would not fit in a computer. This section does something impolite: it **chops** the infinite dimensions down to finite — the $x$ axis becomes a row of grid points, $\psi(x)$ becomes a column of numbers, and $\hat H$ genuinely becomes a matrix you can print out and look at. Energy levels? The matrix's eigenvalues. Wavefunctions? Its eigenvectors. The rest goes to the linear algebra library.

## Step one: turn the function into an array

Choose an interval $[-L/2,\,L/2]$ large enough that the bound-state wavefunctions have long since decayed to nothing at the endpoints, and scatter $N$ equally spaced grid points across it:

$$
x_n=-\frac{L}{2}+nh,\qquad h=\frac{L}{N+1},\qquad n=1,2,\dots,N
$$

The wavefunction is demoted from a continuous curve to a column of samples $\psi_n\equiv\psi(x_n)$. The potential likewise becomes a column of numbers $V_n\equiv V(x_n)$ — **and this is why no potential is special any more**: however exotic the potential, it is just a different column of numbers in an array.

The kinetic term of the stationary Schrödinger equation contains $\psi''$. Here comes the trouble: with only a row of discrete points left, what do we do about derivatives?

## Step two: compute the second derivative from the neighbours

<Derivation title="Derivation: the three-point difference formula and its error" level="基础" minutes={7} open={true}>

**The idea**: the second derivative at a point measures how much the curve bends there, and bending can be probed by asking "how much higher is the average of the two neighbours than the point itself". The tool for turning that sentence into a formula is the Taylor expansion.

**Step 1: expand to both sides.** Assuming $\psi$ is smooth enough, expand about $x_n$ to fourth order:

$$
\psi_{n\pm1}=\psi(x_n\pm h)=\psi_n\pm h\psi_n'+\frac{h^2}{2}\psi_n''\pm\frac{h^3}{6}\psi_n'''+\frac{h^4}{24}\psi_n''''+\cdots
$$

**Step 2: add.** When the two expansions are added, every **odd-order** term ($\pm h\psi'$, $\pm h^3\psi'''/6$) comes with opposite signs and cancels in pairs — which is the entire motivation for taking neighbours placed symmetrically left and right:

$$
\psi_{n+1}+\psi_{n-1}=2\psi_n+h^2\psi_n''+\frac{h^4}{12}\psi_n''''+\cdots
$$

**Step 3: solve for $\psi''$.** Rearrange and divide by $h^2$:

$$
\psi_n''=\frac{\psi_{n+1}-2\psi_n+\psi_{n-1}}{h^2}-\frac{h^2}{12}\psi_n''''+\cdots
$$

Dropping the correction term gives the **three-point difference formula**:

$$
\boxed{\ \psi''(x_n)\approx\frac{\psi_{n+1}-2\psi_n+\psi_{n-1}}{h^2}\ }
$$

**The error is $O(h^2)$**: the leading error term $-\frac{h^2}{12}\psi''''$ shrinks with the **square** of the grid spacing. Halve $h$ and the error drops to a quarter — this "halve and check for four" fingerprint is one we will verify with our own eyes in the numerical experiment shortly.

**Read the formula's face**: the numerator is exactly "sum of the two neighbours minus twice the point itself", i.e. twice the gap between the neighbours' average and the point — dovetailing perfectly with the opening intuition. Where $\psi$ is locally a straight line the numerator vanishes: straight lines do not bend, the second derivative is zero, correct.

</Derivation>

## Step three: the Hamiltonian shows its matrix form

Insert the difference formula into the stationary equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+V\psi=E\psi$; at grid point $n$:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}\psi_{n+1}+\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{mh^2}+V_n\right)\psi_n-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}\psi_{n-1}=E\,\psi_n
$$

The left side is a linear operation on the column vector $(\psi_1,\dots,\psi_N)$ — which is to say a matrix multiplication, $H\boldsymbol\psi=E\boldsymbol\psi$, with

$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\hbar^2}{mh^2}+V_1&-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}&&\\[2pt]
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}&\frac{\hbar^2}{mh^2}+V_2&-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mh^2}&\\[2pt]
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots
\end{pmatrix}
$$

A **tridiagonal** matrix: the diagonal holds "kinetic baseline + local potential", the entries hugging the diagonal hold the coupling between neighbouring grid points, and everything else is zero — because the three-point formula only talks to its immediate neighbours.

Two details to settle properly:

- **Boundary conditions**: the equation at $n=1$ involves $\psi_0$, and at $n=N$ involves $\psi_{N+1}$ — the values at the interval's endpoints. We simply set $\psi_0=\psi_{N+1}=0$, which amounts to locking the system into an infinite box of width $L$. As long as $L$ is big enough that the bound states have decayed exponentially to zero well before the walls, the two fake walls are harmless (how big is "big enough" — see the warning below).
- **The matrix is real and symmetric** — the discrete incarnation of chapter 3's Hermitian operator. The theorem of [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/) is honoured verbatim: all eigenvalues real, all eigenvectors mutually orthogonal. Theory's guarantee and numerics' verification settle their accounts on the spot, in the code below.

## Step four: run it

Practise on the harmonic oscillator (in dimensionless units $\hbar=m=\omega=1$, the levels should be $n+\frac12$). Tridiagonal matrices have a dedicated fast routine, `eigh_tridiagonal`, so there is no need to store $N^2$ zeros:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eigh_tridiagonal

def solve(V, L, N):
    """Solve -psi''/2 + V(x) psi = E psi on [-L/2, L/2] with N interior grid points."""
    x = np.linspace(-L/2, L/2, N + 2)[1:-1]     # drop the two boundary points
    h = x[1] - x[0]
    diag = 1.0 / h**2 + V(x)                    # diagonal: kinetic baseline + potential
    off  = -0.5 / h**2 * np.ones(N - 1)         # off-diagonal: neighbour coupling
    E, psi = eigh_tridiagonal(diag, off, select='i', select_range=(0, 5))
    return x, E, psi / np.sqrt(h)               # divide by √h so that Σ|ψ|²h = 1

x, E, psi = solve(lambda x: 0.5 * x**2, L=16, N=1000)
print(E)        # [0.499992  1.499960  2.499896  3.499800  4.499673  5.499513]
```

All six levels deviate from the exact values $0.5,1.5,\dots,5.5$ only in the fourth or fifth decimal place. And verify chapter 3's promise while we are here: `psi[:,0] @ psi[:,1] * h` prints $0.0$ — distinct eigenstates strictly orthogonal, to machine precision.

## Step five: doubt your results

The most important discipline in numerical work: **a result must prove that it has converged**. The numbers above carry two independent error sources, each with its own check.

**Error one: finite grid spacing $h$.** Refine the grid and watch what moves:

| $N$ | 50 | 100 | 200 | 400 | 800 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| error in $E_0$ | $3.1\times10^{-3}$ | $7.9\times10^{-4}$ | $2.0\times10^{-4}$ | $5.0\times10^{-5}$ | $1.2\times10^{-5}$ |

Each doubling of $N$ (halving of $h$) shrinks the error almost exactly to $1/4$ — the $O(h^2)$ fingerprint the derivation predicted, plain as day. The practical version of this discipline: **double $N$, recompute, and only quote the digits that did not move.**

**Error two: finite box $L$.** Fake walls placed too close pinch the wavefunction. The check is the same in spirit: enlarge $L$, recompute, and trust the result only if it stays put.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two knobs, two ways to fail">
- **$L$ too small**: the wavefunction's exponential tails get clipped by the walls and the levels are pushed **up** (a smaller box means more zero-point energy — remember section 2.7's $1/L^2$). Symptom: enlarging $L$ still visibly moves the low levels.
- **$h$ too coarse**: too few samples per wavelength, so the bending cannot be measured. The high excited states suffer first — the faster $\psi_n$ oscillates, the worse the damage, which is why the errors in the table above creep up with level number. Rule of thumb: **at least a dozen or so grid points in the shortest oscillation period**.

The two knobs pull against each other: with $N$ fixed, enlarging $L$ simultaneously coarsens $h$. The correct posture is to check each one separately and converge each on its own.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="The discrete world has exact solutions of its own">
The error of the difference method need not be probed by numerical experiment alone — the **discrete** infinite-well problem can be solved exactly. Substitute $\psi_n=\sin(n\pi j h/L)$ into the difference equation (with $V=0$), and a trigonometric identity gives the eigenvalues

$$
E_j^{(h)}=\frac{\hbar^2}{mh^2}\left[1-\cos\frac{j\pi h}{L}\right]
$$

As $h\to0$, $1-\cos x\approx x^2/2-x^4/24$, and expanding yields $E_j^{(h)}\approx E_j\left[1-\frac{1}{12}\left(\frac{j\pi h}{L}\right)^2\right]$: the error is indeed $O(h^2)$ and grows as the square of $j$ — higher levels are hurt more. Everything checks out. One more subtle message: the bracket carries a minus sign — **the difference method computes the levels low** (the discrete Laplacian underestimates the bending of fast oscillations).

This idea of putting a continuum problem on a grid is far more than a numerical trick: lattice field theory (lattice QCD) is today's only systematic way to compute the strong interaction, and the tight-binding models of solid-state physics simply declare that crystals were grids all along. That $1-\cos$ spectrum above is exactly the standard shape of a tight-binding band.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The method is in place and the code exists, but every change of potential still means fiddling afresh with the grid, the units, and the checks — like cooking every meal starting from lighting the fire. The next section packages all of it into a **general-purpose solver**: feed it any potential curve and it returns levels and wavefunctions, quality checklist included. Then we use it to do something analytic methods cannot — solve the **double well** — and watch a pair of levels squeeze to within a percent of each other, reading off tunneling, the ammonia maser, and the embryo of the chemical bond.
