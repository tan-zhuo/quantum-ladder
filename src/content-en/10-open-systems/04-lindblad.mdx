The quantum channel of the last section answered "what a single evolution can do to $\hat\rho$". But it is a snapshot: a state goes in, a state comes out, and time is flattened into the map.

The questions in the lab, however, are continuous.

## The phenomenon first: two time constants

To interrogate a superconducting qubit, the standard procedure is two experiments.

**Experiment one**: excite it to $\ket{1}$, wait a time $t$, and measure whether it is still in $\ket{1}$. The result is a clean exponential decay, $P_1(t)=\ee^{-t/T_1}$. This $T_1$ is called the **energy relaxation time**.

**Experiment two**: prepare it in the superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})$, wait a time $t$, and then measure its interference fringes (a Ramsey experiment). The fringe contrast also decays exponentially, with a time constant written $T_2$, called the **coherence time**.

Two experiments, two constants — and experiment finds $T_2\le2T_1$ **every single time**, with no exception on record. The von Neumann equation of chapter 3, $\ii\hbar\,\dd\hat\rho/\dd t=[\hat H,\hat\rho]$, explains neither: it is unitary, purity is conserved, nothing decays. Last section's channels can describe decay, but only as a discrete one-shot deal — they cannot say "what happens each microsecond".

What we need is: **a differential equation, every small step of which is a legitimate quantum channel.**

## From channel to equation: one extra assumption required

Slice the evolution into slabs of time: $\hat\rho(t+\dd t)=\mathcal{E}_{\dd t}\big(\hat\rho(t)\big)$. For this sentence to become a differential equation, a non-trivial assumption is hiding inside — $\mathcal{E}_{\dd t}$ must depend **only on the current $\hat\rho(t)$**, not on the history.

This is the **Markov approximation**. In plain words: **the environment forgets**. The information the system leaks into the environment (that escaped photon, that carried-away phonon) is gone for good; the environment does not hand it back, and does not hold a grudge to settle later. The condition for this to hold is an environment that is "big and fast": its own correlation time is far shorter than the system's relaxation time, so at every instant the system faces a freshly "laundered" environment. Add the requirement that the system-environment coupling is weak (treatable as a perturbation — the Born approximation), and together they are the **Born–Markov approximation** — the only approximation in this section, and the only place things could go wrong.

Under these two assumptions, ask "what is the most general Markovian equation preserving the three properties of a density matrix", and the answer is a theorem (Gorini–Kossakowski–Sudarshan–Lindblad, 1976; we state it without a rigorous derivation):

$$
\boxed{\;
\frac{\dd\hat\rho}{\dd t}
=-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[\hat H,\hat\rho]
+\sum_k\gamma_k\Big(\hat L_k\hat\rho\hat L_k^\dagger
-\tfrac12\big\{\hat L_k^\dagger\hat L_k,\hat\rho\big\}\Big)
\;}
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Term by term**

- First term: an old friend, the von Neumann equation. Switch the environment off ($\gamma_k=0$) and we are back to an isolated system.
- $\hat L_k$: the **jump operators** — the $k$-th thing the environment does to the system (sucking away a photon, kicking the phase…), at rate $\gamma_k\ge0$.
- $\hat L_k\hat\rho\hat L_k^\dagger$: the branch "the jump really happened" — recognise it? It is last section's Kraus sandwich.
- The curly braces are the anticommutator $\{\hat A,\hat B\}=\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A$: the correction to the state from the branch "the jump did **not** happen". No news is also news (last section's amplitude-damping $\hat E_0$), and this term balances the total probability.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Structural checks**

Trace preservation: take the trace of the dissipator and use cyclic invariance,

$$
\operatorname{Tr}\big(\hat L\hat\rho\hat L^\dagger\big)
=\operatorname{Tr}\big(\hat L^\dagger\hat L\hat\rho\big)
$$

which exactly cancels the trace of the anticommutator term, $\operatorname{Tr}(\hat L^\dagger\hat L\hat\rho)$ — the coefficient $\tfrac12$ and the shape of the anticommutator are **designed for precisely this**.

Hermiticity: verify term by term that conjugation leaves everything unchanged. Positivity: each slab $\hat\rho\to\hat\rho+\dd t\,(\cdots)$ can be written in Kraus form ($\hat E_0\approx\hat 1-\ii\hat H\dd t/\hbar-\tfrac12\sum_k\gamma_k\hat L_k^\dagger\hat L_k\dd t$, $\hat E_k=\sqrt{\gamma_k\dd t}\,\hat L_k$), so it is a legitimate channel.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="The Lindblad equation in one sentence">
**Lindblad equation = von Neumann equation + a continuous weak measurement by the environment whose results are never read out.**

The two halves of each dissipator are exactly the two branches of a measurement: "jumped" ($\hat L\hat\rho\hat L^\dagger$) and "didn't jump" (the anticommutator term). You don't know which branch the environment recorded, so you mix them by probability — the differential version of last section's "Kraus = the environment secretly measures and doesn't tell you".
</Callout>

## A full worked example: the damped two-level atom

A two-level atom ($\ket{0}$ ground, $\ket{1}$ excited, gap $\hbar\omega_0$) sits in vacuum and emits spontaneously. The environment does exactly one thing: pull the atom from $\ket{1}$ down to $\ket{0}$. The jump operator is just the lowering operator

$$
\hat L=\hat\sigma_-=\ket{0}\bra{1},\qquad \text{rate}\ \gamma
$$

<Derivation title="Solving the damped atom: from equation to T₁ and T₂" level="基础" minutes={9}>

**Step 1: write the matrix-element equations.** Take $\hat H=\hbar\omega_0\ket{1}\bra{1}$ and compute term by term.

The jump term: $\hat\sigma_-\hat\rho\,\hat\sigma_+=\ket{0}\bra{1}\hat\rho\ket{1}\bra{0}=\rho_{11}\ket{0}\bra{0}$ — it moves the excited-state population into the ground state.

The anticommutator term: $\hat\sigma_+\hat\sigma_-=\ket{1}\bra{1}$, so

$$
\tfrac12\{\ket{1}\bra{1},\hat\rho\}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12\rho_{01}\\ \tfrac12\rho_{10}&\rho_{11}\end{pmatrix}
$$

The Hamiltonian term merely gives the off-diagonal a rotating phase $+\ii\omega_0\rho_{01}$. Assembling, the equations for the four matrix elements:

$$
\dot\rho_{11}=-\gamma\rho_{11},\qquad
\dot\rho_{00}=+\gamma\rho_{11},\qquad
\dot\rho_{01}=\Big(\ii\omega_0-\frac{\gamma}{2}\Big)\rho_{01}
$$

($\rho_{10}=\rho_{01}^*$ needs no separate solution.) Notice the equations have already decoupled — integrate directly.

**Step 2: read the populations.**

$$
\rho_{11}(t)=\rho_{11}(0)\,\ee^{-\gamma t}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
T_1=\frac{1}{\gamma}
$$

The excited state decays exponentially — exactly the curve of "experiment one". $\rho_{00}$ grows to match; the total trace is conserved.

**Step 3: read the coherences.**

$$
\rho_{01}(t)=\rho_{01}(0)\,\ee^{\ii\omega_0t}\,\ee^{-\gamma t/2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
T_2=\frac{2}{\gamma}=2T_1
$$

The coherence rotates at $\omega_0$ (the Ramsey fringes themselves) while decaying at $\gamma/2$ (the falling fringe contrast).

**Why exactly half the decay rate?** $\rho_{11}\propto|c_1|^2$ is the square of an amplitude, while $\rho_{01}\propto c_0c_1^*$ contains the amplitude only to the first power — the amplitude decays at $\gamma/2$, so its square naturally decays at $\gamma$. The relation between $1-\gamma$ and $\sqrt{1-\gamma}$ in last section's amplitude damping reappears here in differential form.

**Step 4: add pure dephasing, and get the general relation.** A real environment does more than absorb energy — it also **kicks the phase alone**: low-frequency noise jitters $\omega_0$ at random, no energy is lost, and the phase cannot be kept. This corresponds to a second jump operator $\hat L'=\hat\sigma_z$ with rate $\gamma_\varphi/2$. Since $\hat\sigma_z^2=\hat 1$, this dissipator simplifies to $\tfrac{\gamma_\varphi}{2}(\hat\sigma_z\hat\rho\hat\sigma_z-\hat\rho)$: the diagonal does not move at all, while the off-diagonal decays by an extra $\gamma_\varphi$. Hence

$$
\frac{1}{T_2}=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\gamma_\varphi=\frac{1}{2T_1}+\frac{1}{T_\varphi}
$$

$T_\varphi\equiv1/\gamma_\varphi$ is called the pure dephasing time. From $\gamma_\varphi\ge0$ it follows at once that

$$
T_2\le 2T_1
$$

**Losing energy necessarily costs coherence (even if that is the only thing the environment does, $T_2$ is at most $2T_1$), while losing coherence need not cost any energy at all.** Phase is the more delicate commodity — that sentence is the trailer for the entire decoherence story.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="When Born-Markov fails">
The Lindblad equation is not a new axiom of quantum mechanics; it is an **approximation**, and its two assumptions fail in different ways:

- **The environment has memory** (non-Markovian): when the environment's correlation time is not short compared with the system's relaxation time, leaked information partly flows back, and coherence can "revive". The classic example: an atom inside a high-quality optical cavity — the photon bounces off the mirrors and gets reabsorbed, so the populations oscillate instead of decaying monotonically.
- **The coupling is too strong** (Born fails): system and environment hybridise strongly, and the very notion of "the system's own $\hat\rho$" starts to blur.

The rule of thumb: if the environment is "big, fast, and forgetful", Lindblad is reliable; if it is "small, slow, and grudge-holding", non-Markovian methods are needed. Superconducting qubits and atomic spontaneous emission mostly belong to the former, and this book stops there.
</Callout>
