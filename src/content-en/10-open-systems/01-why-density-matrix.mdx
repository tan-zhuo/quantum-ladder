By the end of chapter 9 we held a complete quantum-information toolkit: qubits, entanglement, Bell inequalities, teleportation. But that chapter ran on an assumption that was never said out loud — **every system is isolated**, evolution is always unitary, states are always pure.

Open the door of any real laboratory and that assumption shatters on the spot.

## The phenomenon first: why a qubit in the lab "goes bad"

A superconducting qubit is fabricated, initialised into the superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})$, and then left alone. Measure it again a few tens of microseconds later and its ability to interfere has **vanished** — it behaves exactly like a classical coin that is "50% $\ket{0}$, 50% $\ket{1}$".

Nobody measured it, and the Hamiltonian did not evolve it anywhere else. By the rules of chapter 3, unitary evolution preserves inner products and preserves superpositions; $\ket{\psi}$ should forever remain some definite $\ket{\psi(t)}$. And yet it went bad.

The reason is not hard to guess: the qubit is not isolated. It soaks in an **environment** of electromagnetic noise, substrate phonons, and stray two-level defects. To describe a system that "is entangled with its environment while we can only see the system itself", the language of state vectors is congenitally inadequate — which is exactly why [section 3.10](/en/ch/03-formalism/10-density-operator/) introduced the density operator.

What this chapter does is turn that section's static tool into **dynamics**: how open systems evolve (10.3, 10.4), how coherence disappears (10.5), and how the classical world emerges from all of it (10.6). This section first re-inspects the foundations, then pushes three steps forward.

## Re-inspecting the foundations: ρ at a glance

The essentials of [section 3.10](/en/ch/03-formalism/10-density-operator/), in four sentences:

$$
\hat\rho=\sum_i p_i\ket{\psi_i}\bra{\psi_i},\qquad
\langle A\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\rho\hat A)
$$

1. **Definition**: an ensemble that is in the pure state $\ket{\psi_i}$ with classical probability $p_i$ is described by the formula above; a pure state is the special case with a single term.
2. **Three properties**: Hermitian, $\operatorname{Tr}\hat\rho=1$, positive semi-definite — and conversely, any operator satisfying these three is a legitimate quantum state.
3. **Purity**: $\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\rho^2)=1$ if and only if the state is pure; the maximally mixed state in $d$ dimensions gives the lowest value, $1/d$.
4. **Reduction**: a part of an entangled state is described by $\hat\rho_A=\operatorname{Tr}_B\hat\rho_{AB}$, which is necessarily mixed.

If any one of these four sentences makes you hesitate, go back and work through section 3.10 first — every page of this chapter is built on it.

## Two kinds of randomness, one table

Quantum mechanics contains two completely different kinds of "uncertain", and the density matrix files them into different places within one and the same object.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Classical uncertainty: I don't know**

Silver atoms flying out of an oven: 70% are prepared in $\ket{\uparrow}$, 30% in $\ket{\downarrow}$. Each atom has a definite state — it is just **we** who don't know which. This ignorance is classical, no different from drawing balls out of a lottery box.

It sits on the **diagonal** of $\hat\rho$:

$$
\hat\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\0&0.3\end{pmatrix}
$$

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Quantum superposition: it has no definite value**

For the state $\sqrt{0.7}\ket{\uparrow}+\sqrt{0.3}\ket{\downarrow}$, the $z$ component of the spin is **not** "there really is a value, we just haven't measured it" — chapter 9's Bell experiments have ruled out the local version of that reading (local hidden variables). The randomness is created only at measurement.

Its signature is the **off-diagonal elements** (the coherences):

$$
\hat\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&\sqrt{0.21}\\\sqrt{0.21}&0.3\end{pmatrix}
$$

</div>
</DualView>

The two matrices have the same diagonal, so a measurement along $z$ gives identical statistics; the entire difference lives in the off-diagonal elements, and measuring along any other direction separates them at once (section 3.10 worked the 50/50 version). **A real state generally carries both kinds of randomness**: the diagonal gives "the probability of what you measure", the off-diagonal records "how much interference capability is left". The main plot of this chapter is how the environment devours specifically the latter.

## Step one forward: the ensemble interpretation and "infinitely many recipes"

Reading $\hat\rho=0.7\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\uparrow}+0.3\ket{\downarrow}\bra{\downarrow}$ as "an ensemble, 70% up and 30% down" comes naturally. But the warning at the end of section 3.10 must now be promoted to an official position: **the recipe is not unique, and the recipes are indistinguishable in principle.**

<Derivation title="Two completely different recipes, one and the same ρ" level="基础" minutes={5}>

Recipe one: mix $\ket{\uparrow},\ket{\downarrow}$ in proportions $0.7/0.3$.

Recipe two: prepare, with probability $\tfrac12$ each, the two pure states

$$
\ket{a}=\sqrt{0.7}\,\ket{\uparrow}+\sqrt{0.3}\,\ket{\downarrow},\qquad
\ket{b}=\sqrt{0.7}\,\ket{\uparrow}-\sqrt{0.3}\,\ket{\downarrow}
$$

Compute recipe two's density matrix. The two projectors are

$$
\ket{a}\bra{a}=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&\sqrt{0.21}\\\sqrt{0.21}&0.3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\ket{b}\bra{b}=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&-\sqrt{0.21}\\-\sqrt{0.21}&0.3\end{pmatrix}
$$

Multiply each by $\tfrac12$ and add; the off-diagonal elements cancel in pairs:

$$
\hat\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\0&0.3\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Element for element identical** to recipe one. Since every measurable prediction enters only through $\operatorname{Tr}(\hat\rho\hat A)$, the two recipes give the same results for any measurement and any subsequent evolution — not "hard to tell apart", but **no fact of the matter, in principle**.

The general result (the Hughston–Jozsa–Wootters theorem, stated here without proof): two ensembles give the same $\hat\rho$ if and only if their lists of states are related by a unitary matrix. One and the same $\hat\rho$ has infinitely many legitimate recipes.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't read ρ as an 'objective list of ingredients'">
Beginners tend to picture $\hat\rho$ as "the system is objectively in some $\ket{\psi_i}$, and the $p_i$ are the mixing proportions". The derivation above shows this picture cannot stand: the ingredient list simply is not unique.

The correct reading is operational: $\hat\rho$ **is** the complete ledger of every prediction you can make about this system — no more, no less. Two identical ledgers are, physically, one and the same state. The security of quantum key distribution in chapter 9 rests exactly here — the eavesdropper's reduced state is independent of the legitimate recipe, and reading the recipe out of it is impossible in principle.
</Callout>

## Step two forward: where mixed states come from — purification

There is a deeper question still: are mixed states "fundamental"? Or does every mixed state hide a larger pure state behind it?

The answer is the latter, and the construction is surprisingly simple.

<Derivation title="Purification: finding an entangled 'upstream' for any mixed state" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

Take any $\hat\rho_A$ and first diagonalise it (section 3.10: it is Hermitian, so this can always be done):

$$
\hat\rho_A=\sum_k\lambda_k\ket{k}\bra{k},\qquad \lambda_k\ge0,\ \sum_k\lambda_k=1
$$

Bring in an auxiliary system B whose dimension is at least the number of non-zero $\lambda_k$, and build the **pure state** of the composite system

$$
\ket{\Psi}_{AB}=\sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}\,\ket{k}_A\ket{k}_B
$$

(You should recognise this: it is exactly the Schmidt form from chapter 9.) Verify that it really is the upstream of $\hat\rho_A$ — trace out B:

$$
\operatorname{Tr}_B\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}
=\sum_j\bra{j}_B\Big(\sum_{k,l}\sqrt{\lambda_k\lambda_l}\,
\ket{k}_A\bra{l}_A\otimes\ket{k}_B\bra{l}_B\Big)\ket{j}_B
$$

The inner products $\braket{j}{k}_B=\delta_{jk}$ and $\braket{l}{j}_B=\delta_{lj}$ collapse the double sum into a single one:

$$
=\sum_k\lambda_k\ket{k}_A\bra{k}_A=\hat\rho_A\qquad\blacksquare
$$

Example: one purification of $\hat\rho_A=0.7\ket{\uparrow}\bra{\uparrow}+0.3\ket{\downarrow}\bra{\downarrow}$ is

$$
\ket{\Psi}_{AB}=\sqrt{0.7}\,\ket{\uparrow}_A\ket{\uparrow}_B+\sqrt{0.3}\,\ket{\downarrow}_A\ket{\downarrow}_B
$$

— an entangled state. The more mixed the state, the more entanglement it takes; the purification of the maximally mixed state is precisely a Bell state.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="Mixedness = a local view of entanglement">
The purification theorem welds this chapter to chapter 9: **any mixed state can be seen as the reduction of some larger pure state**, and "the larger part" is the environment.

Read in reverse it is even more powerful. When a qubit in the lab "goes mixed", there is no need to imagine a hand throwing dice — it has simply **become entangled with the environment**, and we cannot see the environment's half. What is lost is not the information itself; the information has moved from "the system's own person" into "the correlations between system and environment" (the lesson of the singlet state in section 3.10: globally pure, locally maximally mixed).

This one viewpoint runs through the whole chapter: the quantum channels of section 10.3 = "evolve unitarily together with the environment, then trace it out"; the decoherence of 10.5 = the environment continually "keeping records" of the system; and 10.6 goes one further — the classical world = those records copied countless times over.
</Callout>

## What comes next

A density matrix is a $2\times2$ complex matrix, which sounds abstract. But for a qubit it has an exact **map** — the Bloch sphere. Pure states live on the surface, mixed states live in the interior, and every "going bad" process in this chapter appears on the map as a visible **contraction**. The next section draws that map.
