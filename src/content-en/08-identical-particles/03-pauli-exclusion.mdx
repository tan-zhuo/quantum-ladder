At the end of the last section, the Slater determinant handed us a gift: two equal columns make the determinant vanish. Translated into physics —

> **The Pauli exclusion principle**: two identical fermions cannot occupy the same single-particle quantum state.

The derivation is one line. Put both electrons in the spin-orbital $\phi_a$; the antisymmetric combination gives

$$
\psi_A=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi_a(x_1)\phi_a(x_2)-\phi_a(x_1)\phi_a(x_2)\bigr]\equiv0
$$

This state **does not exist** — not "very high in energy", not "forbidden to transition into", but simply absent from the state space altogether. Strictly speaking, the deeper principle is the stronger one from last section: fermion wavefunctions must be antisymmetric; "exclusion" is merely its most famous corollary.

The principle is short. The bill it pays is long. This section tours what it buys.

## Exhibit one: why does the periodic table have periods?

Start with a question chemistry teachers usually leave unanswered: **why don't all of an atom's electrons fall into the 1s orbital?**

Chapter 6 solved the hydrogen atom: levels $E_n=-13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$, with $1s$ lowest. The principle of minimum energy would seem to say that the ground state of a many-electron atom has every electron crammed into $1s$ — in which case lithium and uranium would both have a 1s "outer shell", chemical properties would drift monotonically, there would be no sudden inertness of the noble gases or sudden violence of the alkali metals, and the periodic table would have no periods at all.

The exclusion principle rewrites the rules. Count how many electrons each shell can hold: the shell with principal quantum number $n$ has $n^2$ spatial orbitals ($\ell=0,\dots,n-1$, each with $2\ell+1$ values of $m$), and each spatial orbital takes spin up and spin down, giving a capacity of

$$
2n^2:\qquad n=1\to2,\quad n=2\to8,\quad n=3\to18,\quad n=4\to32
$$

Electrons can only **fill shell by shell**: hydrogen's single electron takes $1s$; helium's two fill up $n=1$; lithium's third electron is forced to move into $2s$ — so lithium's outermost electron sits alone and loosely bound, chemically eager, remarkably like hydrogen. Periodicity has arrived: **each time a shell fills, the next electron is forced to open a new one, and the chemistry starts its cycle again.**

The lengths of the first few periods — 2, 8, 8, 18 — do not match $2n^2$ term by term (the third period is 8, not 18), and the reason was prepared in chapters 6 and 7: in a many-electron atom the inner electrons **screen** the nuclear charge, so the levels depend on $\ell$ as well as $n$ (and $4s$ fills before $3d$). But the big question, "why are there periods at all?", has a two-part answer: hydrogen-like level structure + Pauli exclusion.

<Callout type="warning" title="Exclusion is not a force">
A common misconception: "Pauli repulsion" is some new repulsive force between electrons. It is not. The Hamiltonian contains only the Coulomb interaction, with no extra term; the exclusion principle restricts the **allowed states**, not the forces. The "exchange repulsion" or "hard-sphere repulsion" of chemistry is how this state-space restriction **shows up** in the energy: try to press two closed-shell electron clouds together and the electrons are forced up into higher orbitals, the energy climbs steeply, and it "looks like" a strong repulsion. Hunt for the source of this force and you will find nothing — its source is a minus sign.
</Callout>

## Exhibit two: pouring a great many electrons into a box

The periodic table is filling "inside one atom". Now the opposite extreme: a metal holds about $10^{23}$ free electrons per cubic centimetre — a colossal number of fermions poured into one big box. What do they do?

A classical gas at $T\to0$ has every particle at rest with zero energy. Fermions cannot: each state seats only two (spin up and down), so latecomers must occupy ever higher levels — like filling a stadium, where once the front rows are taken you can only climb. The highest energy reached is the **Fermi energy** $E_F$.

<Derivation title="The Fermi energy of a free electron gas" level="进阶" minutes={9}>

**Step 1: count the states in the box.** In a cubic box of side $L$, the generalisation of the infinite well gives standing-wave solutions with quantised wavevectors $\vec{k}=\frac{\pi}{L}(n_x,n_y,n_z)$, the $n_i$ positive integers. Each triple $(n_x,n_y,n_z)$ is one spatial orbital, seating 2 electrons.

**Step 2: the ground state = a filled sphere.** At $T=0$, fill from low energy up according to $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$, stopping at some maximum wavenumber $k_F$ — an eighth of a sphere in $k$-space (all $n_i$ positive). The number of states:

$$
N=2\times\frac{1}{8}\times\frac{\frac{4}{3}\pi k_F^3}{(\pi/L)^3}
=\frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2}L^3
$$

(The denominator $(\pi/L)^3$ is the $k$-space volume per state; the factor 2 is spin.) Writing the electron number density as $n=N/L^3$ and solving,

$$
k_F=(3\pi^2 n)^{1/3}
$$

**Step 3: the Fermi energy.**

$$
E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}
$$

**Step 4: plug in real numbers (copper).** Copper contributes 1 free electron per atom, $n=8.5\times10^{28}\,\text{m}^{-3}$:

$$
k_F=(3\pi^2\times8.5\times10^{28})^{1/3}=1.36\times10^{10}\,\text{m}^{-1}
$$

$$
E_F=\frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})^2\times(1.36\times10^{10})^2}{2\times9.11\times10^{-31}}
\approx1.13\times10^{-18}\,\text{J}\approx7.0\,\text{eV}
$$

**Reading the number.** 7 eV corresponds to a "temperature" $T_F=E_F/k_B\approx8\times10^4$ K — nearly 300 times room temperature (0.025 eV). Even at absolute zero, the fastest electrons in a metal fly at about $1.6\times10^6$ m/s (0.5% of the speed of light). This speed is almost independent of temperature; it is squeezed out purely by Pauli exclusion. The classical intuition "cold = slow" fails in the world of fermions.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Intuition: degeneracy pressure.** Want to compress this box of electron gas? Shrink the box and the level spacing grows (recall the infinite well's $E\propto1/L^2$); every occupied level is lifted, and the total energy rises — compression costs work, so the gas is effectively **pushing back**, even at zero temperature. This "purely quantum incompressibility" is called **degeneracy pressure**.

It is everywhere: metals are hard to compress, atoms have definite volumes, your hand pressed on the table does not sink in — microscopically it is always "the electrons have nowhere to go" doing the pushing.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Formula: the pressure.** Differentiate the ground-state energy with respect to volume to get the pressure. The mean kinetic energy of a filled Fermi sphere is $\frac{3}{5}E_F$, so the total energy is $U=\frac{3}{5}NE_F\propto V^{-2/3}$, hence

$$
P=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}=\frac{2}{5}nE_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{5m}(3\pi^2)^{2/3}n^{5/3}
$$

The key scalings: $P\propto n^{5/3}$, and $P\propto1/m$ — **the lighter the particle, the stronger the degeneracy pressure**. In copper this pressure is about $3.8\times10^{10}$ Pa, roughly 380,000 atmospheres, balanced by the Coulomb attraction.

</div>
</DualView>

## Exhibit three: why doesn't a white dwarf collapse?

A star spends its whole life in a tug of war: gravity pulls inward, thermal pressure pushes outward. When the fuel runs out the thermal pressure retreats, and gravity looks certain to win — the star should collapse without limit.

Yet stars like the Sun end up as **white dwarfs**: a solar mass squeezed into an Earth-sized sphere, density about $10^9\,\text{kg/m}^3$ (a teaspoonful weighs some five tonnes), and no matter how far it cools it shrinks no further. What holds it up?

That $P\propto n^{5/3}/m$ from above. Collapse sends the electron density soaring, degeneracy pressure climbs as $n^{5/3}$ — faster than gravity (whose effective scaling is $n^{4/3}$) — so balance is always reached at some radius. **A white dwarf is a star held up by the Pauli exclusion principle** — no burning needed, no temperature needed, only electrons with "nowhere to go".

<Callout type="insight" title="The Chandrasekhar limit: even degeneracy pressure has a ceiling">
The heavier the white dwarf, the harder its electrons are squeezed. As electron speeds approach the speed of light, the energy-momentum relation changes from $E\propto p^2$ to $E\propto p$, and the degeneracy-pressure scaling **softens** from $n^{5/3}$ to $n^{4/3}$ — exactly the same scaling as gravity, and the balance is no longer stable. The critical mass is the **Chandrasekhar limit**, about $1.4M_\odot$.

Beyond it, electron degeneracy pressure concedes and the star collapses on: electrons are pressed into protons to make neutrons, and **neutron** degeneracy pressure takes over (a neutron star, density $\sim10^{17}\,\text{kg/m}^3$); when even neutron degeneracy pressure fails (above roughly $2$–$3M_\odot$), the result is a black hole. Type Ia supernovae explode at masses close to this limit, so their peak brightnesses are similar; calibrated further by the empirical relation between peak brightness and light-curve width (the Phillips relation), they serve as "standard candles" for surveying the universe — a law of quantum statistics, calibrating cosmology's ruler.
</Callout>

## The view on the boson side

Glance across at the other family. Bosons face no exclusion, and as $T\to0$ they can drop **en masse** into the same lowest state — Bose–Einstein condensation (achieved in rubidium-87 in 1995, at 170 nK). Last section's $^4$He superfluid, and the $10^{16}$ photons sharing one mode in a laser cavity, are the same "legal bunching". One minus sign apart: fermions build the periodic table and white dwarfs; bosons light lasers and superfluids.

## What comes next

So far the role of antisymmetry has been to **forbid**: delete certain states. But it does one subtler thing as well — **it reprices the states that remain**.

The Hamiltonian contains no spin, yet the helium atom's levels split into two ladders by total spin, nearly 1 eV apart; the spins in a lump of iron prefer to line up as if a powerful aligning force acted between them. The next section settles that account: the exchange interaction — a "force" that does not exist, and its very real energy.
