Nineteenth-century spectroscopists saw something odd in the helium spectrum: the lines sorted themselves neatly into **two sets**, with transitions between the sets almost never occurring — as if the bottle held two different gases. For a while people genuinely believed it did, naming them "orthohelium" and "parahelium" and treating them as two substances.

Only later did it become clear: the bottle holds one kind of helium. The two sets of lines correspond to the two values of the two electrons' **total spin** — singlet ($S=0$, parahelium) and triplet ($S=1$, orthohelium). And with that came something odder still:

> For the same configuration (say one electron in $1s$ and one in $2s$), the singlet and triplet differ in energy by about **0.80 eV**.

What is odd about that? Helium's Hamiltonian

$$
\hat{H}=\frac{\hat p_1^2}{2m}+\frac{\hat p_2^2}{2m}
-\frac{2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_1}-\frac{2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_2}
+\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}
$$

**contains no spin from start to finish** (spin-field couplings and spin-orbit coupling are small enough to neglect for now; chapter 6's fine structure is only of order $10^{-4}$ eV). Spin appears nowhere in the energy, yet the energies sort themselves by spin, differing by nearly 1 eV — four orders of magnitude too much. Where does this energy come from?

The perturbation tools of chapter 7 are ready to hand, and the last missing puzzle piece was fitted in the previous section: **the total spin, via the antisymmetry requirement, dictates the symmetry of the spatial wavefunction.** And the shape of the spatial wavefunction is something the Coulomb energy can see.

## Settling the account: helium's 1s2s configuration

<Derivation title="The singlet-triplet energy splitting" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Step 0: set up the zeroth-order states.** Neglecting the electron-electron repulsion $e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}$, each electron lives in a hydrogen-like orbital (nuclear charge $Z=2$). Consider the excited configuration: one electron in $\phi_{1s}$, one in $\phi_{2s}$. By [section 8.2](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/02-bosons-fermions/), the spatial part has only two legal combinations:

$$
\psi_\pm(\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi_{1s}(\vec{r}_1)\phi_{2s}(\vec{r}_2)\pm\phi_{2s}(\vec{r}_1)\phi_{1s}(\vec{r}_2)\bigr]
$$

$\psi_+$ (symmetric) must pair with the spin singlet; $\psi_-$ (antisymmetric) with the spin triplet. The zeroth-order energies are equal: $E^{(0)}=E_{1s}+E_{2s}$.

**Step 1: treat the electron repulsion as a perturbation and find the first-order correction.** Write $\hat V=\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}$. The first-order energy correction is the expectation value (the spin part is normalised and contracts away):

$$
E^{(1)}_\pm=\braket{\psi_\pm}{\hat V\,\psi_\pm}
=\frac12\Bigl[\braket{ab}{\hat V\,ab}+\braket{ba}{\hat V\,ba}
\pm\braket{ab}{\hat V\,ba}\pm\braket{ba}{\hat V\,ab}\Bigr]
$$

Here $\ket{ab}$ abbreviates $\phi_{1s}(\vec{r}_1)\phi_{2s}(\vec{r}_2)$. Since $\hat V$ is symmetric under $1\leftrightarrow2$, the four terms are equal in pairs, giving

$$
E^{(1)}_\pm=J\pm K
$$

**Step 2: meet the two integrals.**

The **direct integral** $J$:

$$
J=\int|\phi_{1s}(\vec{r}_1)|^2\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}\,|\phi_{2s}(\vec{r}_2)|^2\,\dd^3r_1\dd^3r_2
$$

This is just the classical Coulomb repulsion energy of the two charge clouds $|\phi_{1s}|^2$ and $|\phi_{2s}|^2$ — no quantum strangeness whatsoever.

The **exchange integral** $K$:

$$
K=\int\phi_{1s}^*(\vec{r}_1)\phi_{2s}^*(\vec{r}_2)\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{12}}\,\phi_{2s}(\vec{r}_1)\phi_{1s}(\vec{r}_2)\,\dd^3r_1\dd^3r_2
$$

Notice that the orbitals on the two sides of the kernel have **swapped places** — this has no classical counterpart. It is an interference term: the interference between the two histories "particle 1 in $1s$, particle 2 in $2s$" and "the other way round". For nodeless overlapping real orbitals like $1s,2s$, $K>0$ (one can prove it is strictly positive here).

**Step 3: read off the physics.**

$$
E_{\text{singlet}}=E^{(0)}+J+K,\qquad
E_{\text{triplet}}=E^{(0)}+J-K,\qquad
\Delta E=2K
$$

**The triplet is lower.** For helium 1s2s, experiment gives: triplet $2^3S_1$ at excitation energy 19.82 eV, singlet $2^1S_0$ at 20.62 eV — a splitting of 0.80 eV, i.e. $K\approx0.40$ eV. Evaluating the integral directly with hydrogen-like orbitals gives the same order of magnitude (somewhat too large, because screening is neglected).

</Derivation>

## Why is the triplet lower? — the Fermi hole

The formula has answered; intuition is still owed. Look at $\psi_-$ at $\vec{r}_1=\vec{r}_2$:

$$
\psi_-(\vec{r},\vec{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi_{1s}(\vec{r})\phi_{2s}(\vec{r})-\phi_{2s}(\vec{r})\phi_{1s}(\vec{r})\bigr]=0
$$

In the triplet, the probability density for the two electrons **to meet at the same point is identically zero**, and it is suppressed throughout each other's neighbourhood — every electron is surrounded by a "moat" its companion refuses to enter, called the **Fermi hole**. The electrons stay far apart on average, so the Coulomb repulsion $\propto1/r_{12}$ costs less. The singlet is the opposite: $\psi_+$ is enhanced at coincidence (a "Fermi heap"), and the repulsion bill runs high.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The one-sentence version.** Spin itself never appears in the energy, but spin **remote-controls the electrons' relative positions through the symmetry**, and position sets the Coulomb energy. Every joule of the energy difference is Coulomb energy — spin is merely the switch that decides "how the bill is paid".

The chain: total spin → (antisymmetry requirement) → spatial symmetry → electrons approach/avoid → Coulomb energy high/low.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The expectation-value version.** For two general orthogonal orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$, the mean-square separation splits off a cross term:

$$
\bigl\langle r_{12}^2\bigr\rangle_\pm
=\bigl\langle r_{12}^2\bigr\rangle_{\text{unsymmetrized}}
\mp2\,\bigl|\braket{\phi_a}{\,\vec{r}\,\phi_b}\bigr|^2
$$

In the antisymmetric case (lower sign, i.e. $+2|\cdots|^2$) the mean-square separation is larger: the avoidance is computable, not a figure of speech. (For same-parity s orbitals like $1s,2s$ this particular matrix element vanishes by parity and the avoidance shows up in higher-order correlations, but $\psi_-(\vec{r},\vec{r})=0$ remains exact throughout.)

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The exchange interaction is not a new force">
Textbook shorthand like "exchange force" or "exchange interaction" invites confusion, so let us nail this down:

The only interaction in the system **is the Coulomb interaction**; not one term has been added to the Hamiltonian. "Exchange interaction" means this: because the wavefunction must be (anti)symmetrized, the expectation value of the Coulomb energy acquires an extra piece, the exchange integral $\pm K$ — **a contribution classical physics does not have**. It is the product of "old force × new symmetry", not a new force.

Two touchstones. First, if the two particles were distinguishable despite different spins (replace one electron by a muon, say), the $K$ term disappears — since when does a "force" care about particle identity? Second, the size of $K$ is set by **wavefunction overlap**: it is exactly zero for non-overlapping orbitals and decays exponentially with distance — which is also why exchange effects are always short-ranged.
</Callout>

## From helium to iron: the quantum origin of ferromagnetism

Abstract the conclusion one step. Since the energy depends only on whether the total spin is singlet or triplet, we can **rewrite it as a function of spin operators**. Using the fact that $\hat{\vec S}_1\cdot\hat{\vec S}_2$ takes the value $-\frac{3}{4}\hbar^2$ in the singlet and $+\frac{1}{4}\hbar^2$ in the triplet (the addition results of chapter 5), the two levels can be packaged into a single **effective Hamiltonian**:

$$
\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}=\text{const}-\frac{2K}{\hbar^2}\,\hat{\vec S}_1\cdot\hat{\vec S}_2
$$

Check by substitution: the triplet gets a relative shift of $-K/2$, the singlet $+3K/2$, and the gap is exactly $2K$. ✔

The significance of this form goes far beyond the helium atom: it says that **pure Coulomb + antisymmetry is equivalent to a spin-spin coupling**. Generalised to the countless atoms of a crystal lattice, it is the starting point of the theory of magnetism — the Heisenberg model $\hat H=-\sum_{ij}J_{ij}\,\hat{\vec S}_i\cdot\hat{\vec S}_j/\hbar^2$. For $J_{ij}>0$ the spins prefer to align in parallel: **ferromagnetism**.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why ferromagnetism has to be a quantum effect: an order-of-magnitude audit">
The most obvious candidate for a "spin-aligning mechanism" is the magnetic dipole interaction — each electron is a tiny compass needle, and needles orient one another. Check whether it is enough. For two electron magnetic moments ($\mu_B=9.27\times10^{-24}$ J/T each) a lattice spacing $r\approx0.25$ nm apart, the dipole energy is

$$
U\sim\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\mu_B^2}{r^3}
=\frac{10^{-7}\times(9.27\times10^{-24})^2}{(2.5\times10^{-10})^3}
\approx5.5\times10^{-25}\,\text{J}\approx3.4\ \mu\text{eV}
$$

Converted to temperature, that is only **0.04 K**: thermal motion above a few hundredths of a kelvin would scramble such an alignment completely. Yet iron's Curie temperature is **1043 K** — an energy scale of about 0.09 eV, more than four orders of magnitude beyond the dipole interaction.

What bridges the chasm is precisely the exchange interaction: $K$ is a Coulomb-scale quantity (a good fraction of an eV), and in helium it is already 0.4 eV. The "aligning force" of ferromagnetism is not magnetic at all — it is Coulomb repulsion acting through the hands of antisymmetry. A magnet is quantum mechanics you can hold. (Incidentally, the sign of the exchange integral depends on the orbital details; for $J_{ij}<0$ the spins prefer antiparallel alignment — that is an antiferromagnet.)
</Callout>

## The spectrum puzzle, revisited

Finally, close the case we opened with. The two sets of lines rarely cross-transition because the operator for optical transitions (electric dipole) contains no spin and cannot change the total spin: the singlet-to-triplet matrix element is approximately zero (the "spin selection rule" $\Delta S=0$). So parahelium and orthohelium each transition internally, forming two independent line systems; $2^3S_1$ cannot drop back to the $1^1S_0$ ground state and becomes a metastable state living over two hours — one of the longest-lived metastable states in atomic physics. The "two heliums" are one helium's two spin families, driven apart by exchange symmetry.

## What comes next

Helium has just two electrons, and we have already filled pages with symmetrized combinations. Iron has 26, whose antisymmetrized expansion runs to $26!\approx4\times10^{26}$ terms; a mole of metal does not bear thinking about. Clearly, the road of "writing out every term" has come to its end.

The next section switches bookkeeping systems: stop asking "which particle is in which state" (a question that should never have been asked — particles carry no labels) and ask instead "**how many particles are in each state**". A new language — second quantization — will shrink the $N!$-term determinant to a single line.
