The last section ended with a hopeless tally: the antisymmetric wavefunction of 26 electrons, expanded in product states, has $26!\approx4\times10^{26}$ terms. Never mind solving it — merely **writing it down** exceeds the storage capacity of the universe.

But before despairing, ask how much information those terms actually carry.

## The bookkeeping is exhausting because the ledger records what it shouldn't

Take 3 fermions occupying orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b,\phi_c$; the Slater determinant expands into 6 terms. How are these 6 terms related? **They are 6 ways of saying the same sentence.** Each one says "orbitals $a,b,c$ each house one particle", merely permuting "which particle lives in which room" — and the very first lesson of [section 8.1](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/01-identity-principle/) was that particles have no labels, so "which particle" is not a legitimate question at all.

The $N!$ explosion is the translation tax we pay for insisting on describing a **label-free world** in a **labelled language**. The actual information content of the whole state is one sentence:

> Orbital $a$: 1 particle; orbital $b$: 1 particle; orbital $c$: 1 particle; all others: 0.

So let the notation record only that sentence. Fix a set of single-particle orbitals $\{\phi_1,\phi_2,\dots\}$ and define the **occupation-number representation**:

$$
\ket{n_1,n_2,n_3,\dots}
$$

where $n_i$ is the number of particles in orbital $\phi_i$. The 6-term determinant above becomes $\ket{1,1,1,0,0,\dots}$; a ten-particle state of $10!=3\,628\,800$ terms is just $\ket{1,1,\dots,1,0,\dots}$. Every detail of symmetrization and antisymmetrization is **built into** the definition of the symbol (by convention it stands for that normalised determinant or symmetric sum) and never has to be written by hand again.

The space these states span is called **Fock space** — it merges the state spaces of "0 particles" (the vacuum $\ket{0}$), "1 particle", "2 particles", and so on into one. For the first time, particle number is a **quantum number** rather than a premise of the theory.

## Moving particles between orbitals: creation and annihilation operators

New notation needs matching operators. Define the **creation operator** $\hat a_i^\dagger$: add one particle to orbital $\phi_i$; and the **annihilation operator** $\hat a_i$: remove one particle from orbital $\phi_i$ (returning zero if there is none to remove).

This algebra should feel familiar — in the harmonic oscillator of [section 2.9](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/09-harmonic-oscillator/), $\hat a^\dagger,\hat a$ climb up and down the ladder of levels. What read as "up one level" there reads here as "one more quantum in this mode". The oscillator's ladder is exactly **the occupation number of a single mode**; all we do now is give every orbital a ladder of its own.

For **bosons**, copy the oscillator's commutation relations, one set per orbital, different orbitals independent:

$$
[\hat a_i,\hat a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij},\qquad
[\hat a_i,\hat a_j]=[\hat a_i^\dagger,\hat a_j^\dagger]=0
$$

The matrix elements copy over too: $\hat a_i^\dagger\ket{\dots,n_i,\dots}=\sqrt{n_i+1}\,\ket{\dots,n_i+1,\dots}$, with $n_i$ unbounded — bosons love to bunch, and the $\sqrt{n+1}$ factor even **rewards** bunching (it is the root of stimulated emission beating spontaneous emission).

For **fermions**, the antisymmetry must be written into the algebra. The correct move is to replace commutators by **anticommutators** (writing $\{\hat A,\hat B\}=\hat A\hat B+\hat B\hat A$):

$$
\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij},\qquad
\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j\}=\{\hat c_i^\dagger,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=0
$$

Why anticommutators? Look at $i\ne j$: $\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_j^\dagger=-\hat c_j^\dagger\hat c_i^\dagger$ — **filling $j$ then $i$ differs from filling $i$ then $j$ by a minus sign**. That is exactly the sign flip of exchanging two particles, now promoted to a grammatical rule of the operators: you could not violate it if you tried.

<Derivation title="Pauli exclusion in two lines of algebra" level="基础" minutes={6} open={true}>

**Step 1: put two fermions in the same orbital.** Set $i=j$ in the anticommutation relation:

$$
\{\hat c_i^\dagger,\hat c_i^\dagger\}=2(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0
$$

Creating two particles in the same orbital gives the zero vector — not some high-energy state, but **no state at all**. What section 8.3 said with determinants is one line of algebra here.

**Step 2: occupation numbers can only be 0 or 1.** Define the number operator $\hat n_i=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i$ and square it:

$$
\hat n_i^2=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i
=\hat c_i^\dagger\bigl(1-\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\bigr)\hat c_i
=\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i-\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i\hat c_i
$$

The first step used $\hat c_i\hat c_i^\dagger=1-\hat c_i^\dagger\hat c_i$ (the $i=j$ component of the anticommutation relation). The last term contains $(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0$, so

$$
\hat n_i^2=\hat n_i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_i(n_i-1)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_i\in\{0,1\}
$$

The eigenvalue equation $n^2=n$ has only two roots. **Pauli exclusion = the anticommutation relations of fermion operators**, no more and no less. Compare bosons: $[\hat a_i,\hat a_i^\dagger]=1$ gives $n_i=0,1,2,\dots$ without bound.

**Step 3 (a bookkeeping detail): the sign convention.** Fermion operators act with a tracking sign:

$$
\hat c_i^\dagger\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i,\dots}=(-1)^{\sum_{j<i}n_j}\,(1-n_i)\ket{n_1,\dots,n_i+1,\dots}
$$

The factor $(-1)^{\sum_{j<i}n_j}$ counts the occupied orbitals listed before $i$ — it guarantees that repeated operations agree term by term with the determinant expansion. The good news: in practice one almost never writes it by hand; the anticommutation relations take care of everything automatically.

</Derivation>

## Translating the Hamiltonian

For the new language to earn its keep, it must express Hamiltonians. The rules are stated without proof (to verify: sandwich both sides between occupation-number states and compare matrix elements).

**One-body terms** (kinetic energy, external potentials — operators that "touch" one particle at a time):

$$
\hat H_1=\sum_{ij}h_{ij}\,\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j,
\qquad h_{ij}=\int\phi_i^*(\vec{r})\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\vec{r})\Bigr]\phi_j(\vec{r})\,\dd^3r
$$

**Two-body terms** (interparticle interactions):

$$
\hat H_2=\frac12\sum_{ijkl}V_{ijkl}\,\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_l\hat a_k,
\qquad V_{ijkl}=\int\phi_i^*(\vec{r})\phi_j^*(\vec{r}\,')\,U(\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,')\,\phi_k(\vec{r})\phi_l(\vec{r}\,')\,\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'
$$

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Every term is a little cartoon.** $\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j$: take a particle out of orbital $j$ and put it into orbital $i$ — one "hop", with amplitude $h_{ij}$. The diagonal term $h_{ii}\hat n_i$ just counts the particles in $i$ and charges each one an energy $h_{ii}$.

$\hat a_i^\dagger\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_l\hat a_k$: two particles start in $k,l$, collide once, and scatter into $i,j$ — amplitude $V_{ijkl}$.

The whole Hamiltonian becomes a "menu of processes": which hops and collisions are allowed, and with what amplitudes. The pictorial language of many-body physics (Feynman diagrams being its ultimate form) starts here.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Sanity check: a non-interacting system.** Choose the $\phi_i$ as eigen-orbitals of the single-particle Hamiltonian, so $h_{ij}=\varepsilon_i\delta_{ij}$, and

$$
\hat H=\sum_i\varepsilon_i\,\hat n_i
$$

Total energy = orbital energy × occupation, at a glance. The Fermi-gas ground state of section 8.3 reads, in this language,

$$
\ket{\text{FS}}=\prod_{|\vec k|\le k_F}\hat c_{\vec k}^\dagger\ket{0}
$$

(fill the modes inside the Fermi sphere one by one). The same state, in determinant language, is a determinant of order $\sim10^{23}$.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The name is wrong: nothing gets 'quantized twice'">
"Second quantization" is a bad name history has left us. No second quantization happens here — from start to finish there is one quantum theory, and all we have done is **change basis** (from the coordinate representation to the occupation-number representation) and change algebraic notation. The particles are the same particles, the Hamiltonian the same Hamiltonian.

The name's origin: historically, people took the particle's wavefunction $\psi(\vec{r})$ and treated it "again" as an operator field $\hat\psi(\vec{r})=\sum_i\phi_i(\vec{r})\hat a_i$, which looked like quantizing the wavefunction a second time. In the modern view it is simply "many-body quantum mechanics in occupation-number notation". The name has stuck, mistake and all; do not let the content follow the mistake.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="The notation's real superpower: particle number can change">
So far, second quantization looks like an efficient compression format. But it has quietly unlocked something the old language **fundamentally cannot do**: describing processes where **particle number changes**.

A wavefunction $\psi(\vec{r}_1,\dots,\vec{r}_N)$ has $N$ locked in from its very definition; in Fock space, $\hat a^\dagger$ is born connecting $N$-particle states to $(N+1)$-particle states. The Hamiltonian for an atom emitting light — "the electron drops a level **and a photon is created**" — can only be written in this language (roughly $\hat H_{\text{int}}\sim g\,\hat c_1^\dagger\hat c_2\,\hat b^\dagger+\text{h.c.}$: the electron falls from 2 to 1 while a photon is created by $\hat b^\dagger$). When chapter 7's Fermi golden rule computed transition rates, it dodged the question "where does the photon come from"; quantum field theory's official answer begins here — every mode of the field is a harmonic oscillator, and particles are its quanta of excitation. See chapter 12.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The language is ready; back to the unfinished practical question: how does one actually compute a many-electron atom? Helium already pushed perturbation theory and the variational method (chapter 7) to their limits; carbon has 6 electrons, iron 26.

The next section introduces an idea that has ruled computational physics for nearly a century: **instead of making each electron face all the others, let it face one averaged "electron cloud"** — then let the cloud and the orbitals feed each other, iterating to self-consistency. The Hartree–Fock method: the many-body problem's first genuinely computable answer.
