The variational method of chapter 7 can pin down the helium ground state to decent accuracy — that was 2 electrons and 6 spatial dimensions. Now face the depth of the periodic table honestly: an iron atom has 26 electrons, and its wavefunction is a function of 78 coordinates

$$
\Psi(\vec{r}_1,\vec{r}_2,\dots,\vec{r}_{26})
$$

## First, size up the enemy

Try to store this function in a computer: even a mere 10 grid points per coordinate axis (absurdly coarse) requires $10^{78}$ numbers — closing in on the number of atoms in the observable universe (of order $10^{80}$), a hundred numbers to be stored on every atom. This is not "insufficient computing power"; **the object itself does not fit inside the physical universe**. Cutting to 5 grid points does not help: $5^{78}\sim3\times10^{54}$. The exponential wall does not negotiate.

There is only one way out: stop solving for the whole $\Psi$ and ask a far cheaper question. Twenty-six single-electron orbitals $\phi_i(\vec{r})$, at $10^3$ grid points each, cost only $2.6\times10^4$ numbers in total. **If** the many-electron state can be approximately assembled from single-electron orbitals — and we happen to have a ready-made assembly: the Slater determinant of [section 8.2](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/02-bosons-fermions/).

So the strategy takes shape:

> **Within the subset "single Slater determinants", find the one of lowest energy.**

This is exactly the variational routine of chapter 7 — except the trial wavefunction is no longer a function with one or two parameters, but the enormous parameter space of "any choice of N orthogonal orbitals".

## The mean field: what does each electron see?

Before any formulas, set up the physical picture. Single out electron $i$. It feels the nuclear attraction, plus the Coulomb repulsion of the other $N-1$ electrons. The trouble is that the latter depends on where the other electrons are, and where they are depends on the fields they each feel... everyone's motion is tangled with everyone else's.

The **mean-field approximation** cuts this knot with one stroke: replace "the other electrons" with their **average charge cloud**

$$
\rho_{\text{rest}}(\vec{r}\,')=\sum_{j\ne i}|\phi_j(\vec{r}\,')|^2
$$

Electron $i$ then moves in an ordinary single-particle effective potential — solving one three-dimensional Schrödinger equation suffices. $N$ electrons mean $N$ three-dimensional equations: the exponential wall collapses into a linear staircase.

There is a price, of course: real electrons **dodge each other as they move** (when you approach, I step aside — this is called correlation), while the mean field smears the other electron into a fixed haze that cannot be dodged. That bill is settled later.

## The self-consistent-field loop

There is also a chicken-and-egg problem: writing electron $i$'s effective potential requires knowing the other orbitals $\phi_j$, and the $\phi_j$ are precisely the unknowns. The answer is iteration — guess a set of orbitals and compute the field; solve for new orbitals in that field, compute the new field; loop until input and output agree. The procedure is called the **self-consistent field** (SCF):

```text
Input: nuclear charge Z, electron number N
1  Guess an initial set of orbitals {φ_1, ..., φ_N}   # e.g. hydrogen-like orbitals
2  repeat:
3      Compute the mean field from the current orbitals:
4          Coulomb potential  J[φ](r)   ← the charge clouds of all orbitals
5          Exchange potential K[φ](r)   ← the antisymmetry correction (see below)
6      For each i, solve the single-particle equation:
7          [ -ħ²∇²/2m - Ze²/4πε₀r + J - K ] φ_i' = ε_i φ_i'
8      Keep the N lowest-energy new orbitals {φ_i'}
9      if max|φ_i' - φ_i| < tolerance: break   # input = output, self-consistent
10     {φ_i} ← {φ_i'}                          # (in practice, mixing helps convergence)
11 Output: orbitals, orbital energies ε_i, total energy E_HF
```

"Guess → compute field → solve → compare → repeat", typically converging in a dozen to a few dozen rounds. In the 1930s, Hartree and his father iterated atomic orbitals on a hand-cranked calculator, months per atom; today your laptop does the same for an iron atom in under a second.

## The Hartree-Fock equations

Now carry out "determinant + variation" rigorously, and watch the mean field grow out of the mathematics by itself — with one more term than the naive picture.

<Derivation title="The energy of a determinant and the HF equations" level="进阶" minutes={12}>

**Step 1: write the energy functional.** Let the trial state be a Slater determinant $\ket{\Phi}$ of $N$ orthonormal spin-orbitals. Using the determinant's orthogonality properties (or, fastest, the second quantization of section 8.5), one finds

$$
E[\Phi]=\braket{\Phi}{\hat H\,\Phi}
=\sum_{i=1}^N h_{ii}+\frac12\sum_{i,j=1}^N\bigl(J_{ij}-K_{ij}\bigr)
$$

where $h_{ii}$ is orbital $i$'s kinetic plus nuclear-attraction energy, and

$$
J_{ij}=\int|\phi_i(\vec{r})|^2\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,'|}\,|\phi_j(\vec{r}\,')|^2\,\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'
$$

$$
K_{ij}=\int\phi_i^*(\vec{r})\phi_j^*(\vec{r}\,')\,\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,'|}\,\phi_j(\vec{r})\phi_i(\vec{r}\,')\,\dd^3r\,\dd^3r'
$$

These are exactly the **direct** and **exchange integrals** of [section 8.4](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/04-exchange-interaction/), now summed over all orbital pairs. ($K_{ij}$ is non-zero only when $i,j$ have the same spin; for $i=j$, $J_{ii}=K_{ii}$ cancel automatically, so an electron does not repel itself — the bookkeeping is clean.)

**Step 2: vary.** Minimising $E[\Phi]$ under the constraint that the orbitals stay orthonormal (handled with Lagrange multipliers, chapter 7's standard trick) gives, for each $\phi_i$, the condition

$$
\Bigl[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2-\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}
+\hat J-\hat K\Bigr]\phi_i(\vec{r})=\varepsilon_i\,\phi_i(\vec{r})
$$

These are the **Hartree-Fock equations**. Two new operators:

The **Coulomb operator** (a multiplicative operator — the very charge cloud of the mean-field picture):

$$
\hat J\,\phi_i(\vec{r})=\Bigl[\sum_j\int\frac{e^2\,|\phi_j(\vec{r}\,')|^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,'|}\,\dd^3r'\Bigr]\phi_i(\vec{r})
$$

The **exchange operator** (a non-local operator — its output depends on the values of $\phi_i$ **elsewhere**):

$$
\hat K\,\phi_i(\vec{r})=\sum_j\phi_j(\vec{r})\int\frac{e^2\,\phi_j^*(\vec{r}\,')\,\phi_i(\vec{r}\,')}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,'|}\,\dd^3r'
$$

**Step 3: read the structure.** $\hat J$ is Hartree's naive mean field of 1928; $\hat K$ is the antisymmetry correction added by Fock and Slater in 1930 — the naive picture **could not have guessed it**; the determinant variation delivers it free of charge. $\hat K$ lowers the repulsion energy of same-spin electron pairs (the Fermi hole: same-spin electrons already avoid each other and pay a smaller Coulomb bill) — the whole-system version of the exchange effect of section 8.4.

The operator on the left-hand side (the **Fock operator** $\hat F$) depends on the full set of solutions $\{\phi_j\}$, so this is a system of non-linear equations — it must be solved with the SCF loop above.

</Derivation>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**What the orbital energies mean.** The eigenvalues $\varepsilon_i$ are not arbitrary bookkeeping: **Koopmans' theorem** says $-\varepsilon_i$ is approximately the energy needed to ionise an electron out of orbital $i$ (assuming the other orbitals do not relax). The "HOMO level" and "ionisation energy" on every chemist's lips have their theoretical home right here.

A related pitfall: $E_{\text{HF}}\ne\sum_i\varepsilon_i$. Each $\varepsilon_i$ already includes the repulsion with all other electrons, so a straight sum counts every pair twice; one must subtract $\frac12\sum(J_{ij}-K_{ij})$.

HF captures 98.6% of the total energy. The remaining $1.14$ eV cannot be recovered — no matter how perfectly the orbitals are optimised, the single determinant's ceiling sits right there.

## The part that cannot be recovered: correlation energy

Define the **correlation energy** as the gap between the exact value and the HF limit:

$$
E_{\text{corr}}=E_{\text{exact}}-E_{\text{HF}}
$$

For helium, $E_{\text{corr}}\approx-1.14$ eV. Its source is exactly the bill foreshadowed at the start: real electrons dodge each other instant by instant, so configurations like "you left, me right" carry extra weight; in a single determinant each electron sees only the other's **average** cloud, and this pas de deux cannot be described. In the language of chapter 3: the true ground state is a superposition of **many** determinants, and we kept only one.

<Callout type="warning" title="Why a 1.4% error cannot be ignored">
"Capturing 98.6%" sounds nearly perfect, but chemistry does not run on total energies — it runs on **energy differences**. A chemical bond is typically worth only 2-5 eV, the same order as the correlation energy; reaction barriers are smaller still. HF gives a qualitatively wrong dissociation limit for H₂ (forcing in ionic components), and the van der Waals attraction between molecules (the dispersion force) is **exactly zero** in HF — it is a correlation effect through and through.

Modern computation therefore works as "HF for the base coat, correlation billed separately": configuration interaction, coupled cluster, and perturbation expansions (MP2) follow the "add more determinants" route; density functional theory (DFT) takes a different road, packaging exchange and correlation into a functional of the density. The 1998 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Kohn and Pople) honoured precisely this road of computational chemistry that sets out from HF.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Mean field: an idea that runs through physics">
The territory of "replace complicated interactions with a self-consistent mean field" reaches far beyond atoms: the Curie–Weiss theory of magnetism (each spin feels its neighbours' average magnetisation), the BCS theory of superconductivity (electron pairs feel an average pairing field), the nuclear shell model (nucleons move in an average nuclear potential), even galactic dynamics — all the same move. The universal price is also the same: fluctuations and correlations get erased. Knowing where mean field fails (low dimensions, strong correlation, near phase transitions) is one of the central concerns of modern condensed-matter physics.
</Callout>
