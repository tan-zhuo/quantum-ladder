## What this site is

A course in quantum mechanics that runs from no background to the research frontier, with one distinguishing habit: **wherever something can be dragged, you get to drag it**. Wavefunctions, energy levels and transmission probabilities are computed in your browser on the spot, not pasted in as images.

The whole site is static. There is no account and no server; your progress lives only in this device's browser. Pages you have already opened stay readable with the network off.

## Three routes

Same content, different order. Which one fits depends on what you are currently missing.

<Callout type="insight" title="How to use a simulation so that it counts">
Every simulation has a "try this" list under it. The right way to use it is:

1. **Read the question and commit to a prediction** — in your head or on paper
2. Then drag the slider and check
3. If the prediction was wrong, go back to the text and find the step where your reasoning went off

Drag first and you will feel that everything is "intuitive and obvious", and then discover in an exam or a derivation that nothing stuck. **Learning happens at the failed prediction.**
</Callout>

## Notation and units

<DualView left="Notation" right="Units">
<div slot="left">

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $\Psi(x,t)$ | The full time-dependent wavefunction |
| $\psi(x)$ | The spatial part (solution of the stationary equation) |
| $\hat A$ | An operator (hatted); $A$ is the corresponding classical quantity or eigenvalue |
| $\langle \hat A\rangle$ | Expectation value |
| $\sigma_A$ | Standard deviation (some books write $\Delta A$) |
| $\ket{\psi}$, $\bra{\psi}$ | Dirac notation, used from chapter 3 onwards |
| $\ee$, $\ii$, $\dd$ | Euler's number, the imaginary unit and the differential (upright) |

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The prose** always keeps $\hbar$ and full dimensions, so that any formula can be checked by substituting SI or eV units directly.

**The simulations** work internally in natural units, $\hbar=m=1$, because then energies, lengths and times are all O(1): slider ranges are easy to choose and floating-point precision is comfortable. Converting back to SI is covered in [appendix C](/en/ch/appendix-c-numerics/).

**Handy conversions** (very useful for estimates):

$$
\hbar c=197.3\ \text{eV·nm},\qquad m_ec^2=511\ \text{keV}
$$

$$
k_BT\big|_{300\text{K}}=25.9\ \text{meV}
$$

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="When comparing with other textbooks">
Conventions clash between books. Three places in particular:

1. **Where the $2\pi$ goes in the Fourier transform.** This site uses the symmetric convention ($1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ on each side). Griffiths does the same; Sakurai does not.
2. **Ladder-operator notation.** This site writes $\hat a_\pm$ (Griffiths style); most of the literature writes $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$. They are the same thing: $\hat a_-=\hat a$, $\hat a_+=\hat a^\dagger$.
3. **The symbol for uncertainty.** This site writes $\sigma_x$ to emphasise that it is a standard deviation. Books writing $\Delta x$ usually mean the same quantity, but a few mean full width at half maximum, which differs by a factor of about 2.35.
</Callout>
