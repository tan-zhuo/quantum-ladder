The previous section ended at a boundary line: Planck held that "one unit at a time" was a property of the **oscillators in the cavity wall**, while light travelling through space remained a continuous electromagnetic wave.

This section is about someone crossing that line.

## Simple experiment, awkward results

The apparatus has three parts: a metal plate, a collector and an ammeter. Shine light on the plate; if electrons are knocked out and reach the collector, the ammeter reads.

<Callout type="history" title="It was found by accident">
In 1887, while doing the experiments that confirmed electromagnetic waves exist, Hertz noticed that shining ultraviolet light on his spark gap made the spark jump more easily.

There is some irony here: **the experiment that gave Maxwell's wave theory its decisive confirmation also turned up the phenomenon that would undermine it.**
</Callout>

By 1902 Lenard had the measurements nailed down. Four facts:

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Fact 1: there is a threshold frequency.** Each metal has a threshold $\nu_0$. Below it, **no matter how bright the light or how long you wait**, not a single electron comes out.

**Fact 2: intensity does not affect electron energy.** Turn the light up and the **number** of electrons increases, but the **maximum kinetic energy** of each one does not budge.

**Fact 3: kinetic energy depends only on frequency.** Higher frequency gives higher maximum kinetic energy, and the relation is linear.

**Fact 4: the response is instantaneous.** The current appears essentially the moment the light arrives (later measured at under $10^{-9}$ s), even for light almost too faint to detect.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**What classical wave theory expects, point by point:**

For fact 1: energy is carried by amplitude, not frequency. Bright enough light for long enough ought to accumulate enough energy. **Does not match.**

For fact 2: more intensity means stronger fields, stronger forces on the electron, so it should come out faster. **Does not match.**

For fact 3: classically the frequency only sets how fast the electron is shaken, not its energy ceiling. **Does not match.**

For fact 4: energy flows in continuously from the whole wavefront, so it takes time to accumulate. **Does not match.**

</div>
</DualView>

<Derivation title="Estimate: how long classical theory says you must wait" level="基础" minutes={5}>

Putting a number on "accumulating energy" shows how fatal fact 4 is.

Take a faint beam with power density $I=10^{-6}\ \text{W}/\text{m}^2$ (roughly a candle a few metres away). An atom has a cross-section of about $\sigma\approx10^{-20}\ \text{m}^2$.

The power intercepted by one atom is then

$$
P=I\sigma=10^{-26}\ \text{W}
$$

Ejecting an electron requires overcoming the work function, typically $W\approx2\ \text{eV}=3.2\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. The time to accumulate that is

$$
t=\frac{W}{P}=\frac{3.2\times10^{-19}}{10^{-26}}\approx3\times10^{7}\ \text{s}
$$

**About a year.**

The measured delay is under a nanosecond. Sixteen orders of magnitude apart — this is not a theory that lacks precision, it is a picture that is simply wrong.

</Derivation>

## Einstein changed one sentence

In 1905 the 26-year-old Einstein, working in a patent office, wrote a paper titled "On a heuristic point of view concerning the production and transformation of light". He said:

<Callout type="insight" title="The light-quantum hypothesis">
Light is not merely emitted and absorbed in units — **it comes in units**. A beam of frequency $\nu$ consists of many particles of energy $h\nu$, later called **photons**.

An electron can absorb **one** photon at a time: all of it, or nothing.
</Callout>

That is the whole change. Now go through the four facts again:

$$
h\nu = W + E_{k,\text{max}}
$$

$W$ is the **work function**, the minimum cost of pulling an electron out of the metal. $E_{k,\text{max}}$ is what the electron has left over.

- **Threshold frequency**: if $h\nu<W$, a single photon cannot pay for the ticket and no electron emerges. More photons do not help, because pooling is not allowed — an electron absorbs one at a time. Hence $\nu_0=W/h$.
- **What intensity does**: intensity equals the number of photons. More photons knock out more electrons (larger current), but each still receives $h\nu$, so the energy ceiling is unchanged.
- **Linear in frequency**: $E_{k,\text{max}}=h\nu-W$, a straight line of slope exactly $h$ and intercept $-W$.
- **Instant response**: nothing needs accumulating. A photon arrives, hits an electron, and the transaction is done.

<Callout type="check" title="Note where the real work is done">
The sentence doing the work is not "light has energy hν"; it is **"an electron can only absorb one photon at a time"**.

That is what forbids pooling, and pooling is what would destroy the sharp threshold. If an electron could absorb two photons in succession, low-frequency light would eject electrons provided it were bright enough.

And today that is exactly what can be done: crank the intensity up with a femtosecond laser and two-photon photoemission is a routine technique. **Low-frequency light really does eject electrons when it is intense enough** — which does not refute Einstein at all; it confirms that the restriction comes from a very improbable process rather than an absolute ban.
</Callout>

## Millikan came to refute it and nailed it down instead

Einstein's paper was not popular. Even Planck did not accept it — putting $h$ inside light in flight struck him as going too far.

<Callout type="history" title="Ten years of precision measurement">
Millikan spent nearly a decade on precision photoelectric measurements, stating openly that his goal was to **disprove** Einstein's equation. He cut metal surfaces fresh in vacuum (to avoid oxide layers) and pushed the voltage–current curves to the limits of the day.

In 1916 he published: the plot of $E_k$ against $\nu$ **is a perfect straight line**, and the $h$ from its slope agrees to within 0.5% with the value Planck had fitted from black-body radiation.

He wrote the much-quoted line that Einstein's equation "appears in every case to predict exactly the observed results", while maintaining that the physical hypothesis behind it was untenable.

The upshot: Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize for the photoelectric effect (**not** for relativity), and Millikan received his in 1923 partly for this work.
</Callout>

<Derivation title="Work it out: the threshold wavelength of sodium" level="基础" minutes={4}>

Sodium has a work function $W=2.28\ \text{eV}$. The corresponding threshold wavelength is

$$
\lambda_0=\frac{hc}{W}=\frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{2.28\ \text{eV}}=544\ \text{nm}
$$

(Remember $hc=1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}$ — one of the most useful constants there is.)

544 nm is green. So green, blue and violet light eject electrons from sodium; yellow and red never will, however long you shine them.

One more: with 400 nm violet light the maximum kinetic energy is

$$
E_k=\frac{1240}{400}-2.28=3.10-2.28=0.82\ \text{eV}
$$

Stopping those electrons requires 0.82 V of reverse bias on the collector — and this **stopping voltage** is precisely what the experiment measures directly.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="misconception" title='"Light is a particle, so wave theory is wrong" — no'>
The photoelectric effect shows that light behaves granularly **when it exchanges energy with matter**. It does not abolish interference and diffraction.

The same beam passing through two slits makes interference fringes (wave behaviour) and, landing on a metal, knocks out electrons one at a time (particle behaviour). Both are true.

The correct statement is not "light is a particle" but: **light is neither a classical wave nor a classical particle. Both concepts are borrowed from the macroscopic world, and each fits only half of what light does.**

[Section 1.6](/en/ch/01-history/06-double-slit/) pushes this to its limit with the double slit: photons arriving one at a time still build up interference fringes.
</Callout>

## What comes next

By now two of the three dead ends from [section 1.1](/en/ch/01-history/01-classical-crisis/) have been undone by the same sentence: **energy is exchanged in whole units.**

The third remains: why atoms do not collapse, and why spectra are sets of sharp lines.

In 1913 a young Dane working under Rutherford in Manchester decided to try attaching "one unit at a time" directly to the electron's orbit, and see what came out.
