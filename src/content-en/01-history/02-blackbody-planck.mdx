The first dead end from the previous section: classical theory computes black-body radiation and returns infinity.

This section is about how Planck strangled it — and about the fact that he did not reason his way to the formula, but the other way round.

## First, what the measured curve looks like

Through the 1890s the Imperial Physical-Technical Institute in Berlin produced the most accurate black-body measurements then available. The curve has a distinctive shape:

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Low-frequency end**: energy density rises with frequency, and the measurements agree well with Rayleigh–Jeans.

**Peak**: a maximum at some frequency. The hotter the body, the higher that frequency — which is why iron goes from dull red to orange-white.

**High-frequency end**: falls away fast, faster than any power law. The total area (the total energy) is finite.

In 1896 Wien produced a purely empirical formula that fits the high end well but runs visibly low at the bottom. Rayleigh–Jeans fits the low end and diverges at the top. **Each formula gets half the curve.**

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Wien's displacement law** (empirical, 1893):

$$
\lambda_\text{max}T=2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}
$$

**Stefan–Boltzmann law** (empirical, 1879):

$$
\text{total radiated power}\propto T^4
$$

Any correct theory has to reproduce both of these and reduce to Wien and Rayleigh–Jeans at the two ends. The constraints are tight.

</div>
</DualView>

## What Planck did

In October 1900 Planck used interpolation to stitch Wien's formula and Rayleigh–Jeans together — a purely mathematical move, aimed at producing an expression correct at both ends:

$$
u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\cdot\frac{1}{\ee^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}
$$

That same evening someone tested it against the data. The agreement was perfect.

**But why should the formula hold?** For the next two months Planck was, in his own words, working "desperately" to find a reason. What he found was this:

<Callout type="insight" title="The quantum hypothesis">
An oscillator of frequency $\nu$ in the cavity wall cannot have arbitrary energy; it is restricted to

$$
E_n=nh\nu,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots
$$

Energy is exchanged in **whole units**, each of size $h\nu$. Here $h$ is a new constant of nature, which Planck fixed at about $6.55\times10^{-34}\ \text{J}\cdot\text{s}$ by fitting the data (modern value $6.626\times10^{-34}$).
</Callout>

## Why "one unit at a time" defeats the catastrophe

This is the step in the section most worth understanding, and it takes no calculation at all.

<Callout type="check" title="Work it out first">
The classical failure was: there are very many high-frequency modes, and each gets $k_BT$.

Now impose that energy comes only in units of $h\nu$. The higher the frequency, the more expensive a single unit. Meanwhile the thermal bath supplies energies of order $k_BT$.

So what happens when $h\nu \gg k_BT$?
</Callout>

The answer: **that mode cannot even afford its first unit, so it stays at $n=0$ and its average energy is essentially zero.**

There are still just as many high-frequency modes, but they are all empty. The divergence is cut off at its source.

<Derivation title="Derivation: from E = nhν to Planck's law" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

Restrict the oscillator energies to $E_n=nh\nu$ and compute the average with the Boltzmann distribution. Write $x\equiv h\nu/k_BT$:

$$
\langle E\rangle=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}nh\nu\,\ee^{-nx}}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ee^{-nx}}
$$

The denominator is a geometric series:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ee^{-nx}=\frac{1}{1-\ee^{-x}}
$$

For the numerator use a standard trick — differentiate the denominator:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,\ee^{-nx}=-\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\frac{1}{1-\ee^{-x}}=\frac{\ee^{-x}}{(1-\ee^{-x})^2}
$$

Divide:

$$
\langle E\rangle=h\nu\cdot\frac{\ee^{-x}}{1-\ee^{-x}}=\frac{h\nu}{\ee^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}
$$

**That is the key result.** Now substitute it for $k_BT$ in the classical derivation; the mode density $8\pi\nu^2/c^3$ needs no change at all:

$$
u(\nu,T)=\frac{8\pi\nu^2}{c^3}\cdot\frac{h\nu}{\ee^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}
$$

**Check the two limits.**

Low frequency ($h\nu\ll k_BT$, i.e. $x\ll1$): using $\ee^x\approx1+x$,

$$
\langle E\rangle\approx\frac{h\nu}{x}=k_BT
$$

Equipartition returns intact, so Rayleigh–Jeans is the low-frequency limit of Planck's law.

High frequency ($h\nu\gg k_BT$): the $-1$ in the denominator is negligible, so

$$
\langle E\rangle\approx h\nu\,\ee^{-h\nu/k_BT}
$$

**Exponential suppression.** This is Wien's formula, and this is where the ultraviolet catastrophe is cured: exponential decay beats the $\nu^2$ growth easily, and the integral converges.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="history" title="Planck did not believe it">
Planck was 42 when he wrote this down, and a classical physicist to the core. He later called the quantum hypothesis "an act of desperation" (*ein Akt der Verzweiflung*).

For over a decade he kept trying to reinterpret $h$ as the product of some classical effect. All the attempts failed. As late as 1913 he was writing to the Prussian Academy in support of Einstein, arguing that Einstein should not be judged for having "occasionally gone too far in his light-quantum hypothesis" — and the light quantum is a direct consequence of Planck's own assumption.

**The originator being the last to believe it** is not rare in the history of physics.
</Callout>

## The second dead end falls out for free

The low-temperature specific heat problem is solved here too, using the very same sentence.

Atomic vibrations in a solid are oscillators as well. To excite a mode of frequency $\nu$ costs at least $h\nu$. When the temperature is low enough that $k_BT\ll h\nu$, the mode is frozen and contributes nothing to the heat capacity.

Raise the temperature and more modes thaw out, so the heat capacity climbs towards the classical $3R$. Einstein built the first quantum model of specific heat on this in 1907, and Debye refined it in 1912 to the point of quantitative agreement.

<Callout type="insight" title="Why diamond misbehaves already at room temperature">
Vibrational frequency goes as $\nu\propto\sqrt{k/m}$. Diamond's carbon–carbon bonds are extremely stiff (large $k$) and carbon atoms are light (small $m$), so its vibrational frequencies are unusually high.

How high? Diamond's Debye temperature is about 2200 K, far above room temperature. So as far as diamond is concerned, 300 K *is* the cold, and most of its vibrational modes are still frozen.

Lead is the opposite: soft bonds, heavy atoms, Debye temperature only about 105 K. It obeys Dulong–Petit obediently at room temperature.
</Callout>

## An exercise in orders of magnitude

<Derivation title="Estimate: what a body, a filament and the Sun emit" level="基础" minutes={4}>

Using Wien's displacement law $\lambda_\text{max}T=2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}$:

- **A human body** (310 K): $\lambda_\text{max}=9.3\ \mu\text{m}$, far infrared. That is the band thermal cameras work in, and why you cannot see a person in the dark.
- **An incandescent filament** (2800 K): $\lambda_\text{max}=1.0\ \mu\text{m}$, near infrared. The peak is not in the visible at all — a light bulb turns most of its energy into heat, which is exactly why it is so inefficient.
- **The Sun's surface** (5800 K): $\lambda_\text{max}=0.50\ \mu\text{m}$, green light, right in the middle of the visible spectrum.

The last one is no coincidence: **the sensitivity band of the human eye was selected by the Sun's radiation peak.**

</Derivation>

## What comes next

Planck held that "one unit at a time" was a property of the oscillators in the cavity wall; light itself remained a continuous wave.

Five years later a patent clerk pushed that sentence somewhere Planck did not want it to go: **what if light itself comes in units?**
