The previous section left the third dead end standing: a classical atom lives under $10^{-11}$ s and ought to emit a continuous spectrum.

Start by looking at what experiment actually offered.

## Spectral lines are not random — they have a formula

Nineteenth-century spectroscopy accumulated a great deal of data: each element, heated or discharged, emits only at certain wavelengths, fixed precisely enough to identify the element.

Hydrogen is the simplest, with only a handful of visible lines. In 1885 Balmer, a Swiss schoolteacher of mathematics, stared at those four numbers and found a formula. Rydberg later generalised it:

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda}=R_\infty\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right),\qquad n_2>n_1
$$

with $R_\infty=1.097\times10^7\ \text{m}^{-1}$ fitted purely from experiment.

<Callout type="check" title="Notice the shape of this formula">
The right-hand side is **the difference of two terms, each depending only on an integer.**

That is close to saying outright: each spectral line corresponds to a jump from one state to another, and the states are labelled by integers.

The trouble is that in 1885 nobody knew what those integers were. For almost thirty years the formula was an unsolved numerical puzzle.
</Callout>

## Bohr's two postulates

In 1913 Bohr proposed two postulates in direct conflict with classical physics. He knew perfectly well they were unjustified; the plan was to accept them and see what followed.

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate one: stationary states">
Electrons can occupy only certain orbits, and while in them they **do not radiate**. These are called **stationary states**.

The criterion is quantisation of angular momentum:

$$
L=m v r=n\hbar,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots
$$
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate two: transitions">
When an electron jumps from a state of energy $E_{n_2}$ to one of energy $E_{n_1}$, the difference leaves as a single photon:

$$
h\nu=E_{n_2}-E_{n_1}
$$

Radiation happens only during the jump, and has nothing to do with how the electron moves while in an orbit.
</Callout>

The first postulate cuts off the collapse directly: **an electron in a stationary state does not radiate, so it cannot spiral in**; and $n=1$ is the lowest rung, with no step below it, so the atom is stable.

The second turns the "difference of two terms" in the Rydberg formula into "difference of two energy levels".

<Derivation title="Derivation: from two postulates to 13.6 eV" level="基础" minutes={8} open={true}>

**Step 1: classical mechanics gives the orbit condition.** The Coulomb force supplies the centripetal force:

$$
\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}=\frac{mv^2}{r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
mv^2=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}
$$

Note this step is entirely classical — Bohr invoked quantum assumptions only where he had to, and used Newton everywhere else.

**Step 2: impose the quantisation condition.** From $mvr=n\hbar$ we get $v=n\hbar/mr$; substituting,

$$
m\cdot\frac{n^2\hbar^2}{m^2r^2}=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
r_n=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{me^2}\,n^2
$$

**The orbital radii are discrete and go as $n^2$.** The smallest ($n=1$) is the **Bohr radius**:

$$
a_0=\frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar^2}{me^2}=0.529\ \text{Å}=5.29\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}
$$

This matched the atomic size then estimated from gas viscosity and similar methods. **A length assembled purely from fundamental constants landing at the right order of magnitude** — this was the model's first genuinely persuasive moment.

**Step 3: compute the energy.** Total energy is kinetic plus potential:

$$
E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}
=\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0r}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}
=-\frac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0r}
$$

(using step 1's $mv^2=e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$; note in passing that **the kinetic energy is exactly half the magnitude of the potential energy**, a consequence of the virial theorem for a Coulomb force).

Substituting $r_n$:

$$
E_n=-\frac{me^4}{32\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}=-\frac{13.606\ \text{eV}}{n^2}
$$

**Step 4: match the Rydberg formula.** By postulate two,

$$
h\nu=E_{n_2}-E_{n_1}=13.606\ \text{eV}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

In terms of wavelength,

$$
\frac1\lambda=\frac{13.606\ \text{eV}}{hc}\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2}-\frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)
$$

That leading coefficient works out to $1.097\times10^7\ \text{m}^{-1}$ — **agreeing with the measured Rydberg constant to four significant figures.**

Thirty years of numerical puzzle, solved.

</Derivation>

<div slot="left">

**What the level diagram looks like**

$E_n=-13.6/n^2$ eV: $-13.6,\ -3.40,\ -1.51,\ -0.85,\dots$

The gaps shrink as you climb, crowding towards $E=0$ (where the electron just escapes). So:

- Transitions down to $n_1=1$ (the **Lyman series**) carry the most energy and lie in the ultraviolet.
- Down to $n_1=2$ (the **Balmer series**) happen to land in the visible — these are the four lines Balmer could see with his eyes.
- Down to $n_1=3$ (the **Paschen series**) lie in the infrared.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
E_n=-\frac{13.606\ \text{eV}}{n^2},\qquad r_n=a_0n^2
$$

The **ionisation energy** — taking the electron from $n=1$ to $n=\infty$ — is 13.6 eV, in exact agreement with experiment.

Using the fine-structure constant $\alpha=e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c\approx1/137$ it can be written very cleanly:

$$
E_n=-\frac12\alpha^2mc^2\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}
$$

that is, $\frac12\times(1/137)^2\times511000\ \text{eV}=13.6\ \text{eV}$.

</div>

## The correspondence principle: how Bohr found that condition

In the derivation above, $L=n\hbar$ was written down out of nowhere. Bohr did not guess it — he had a methodological principle.

<Callout type="insight" title="The correspondence principle">
**At large quantum numbers, quantum predictions must reduce to the classical result.**

Classical electrodynamics says an orbiting electron radiates at its **orbital frequency** $f_\text{orb}$. Quantum theory says the radiated frequency is set by the gap between adjacent levels, $\nu=(E_{n+1}-E_n)/h$.

In the limit of large $n$ these two must agree. Bohr found that they agree only if the angular momentum is quantised as $n\hbar$ — so he used the correspondence **backwards**, as the basis for deriving the quantisation condition.
</Callout>

<Derivation title="Check: the two frequencies really do agree at large n" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

**Quantum side.** The gap between adjacent levels:

$$
\Delta E=E_{n+1}-E_n=13.6\ \text{eV}\left(\frac{1}{n^2}-\frac{1}{(n+1)^2}\right)
$$

Expanding for large $n$: $\frac{1}{n^2}-\frac{1}{(n+1)^2}\approx\frac{2}{n^3}$, so

$$
\nu_\text{quantum}=\frac{\Delta E}{h}\approx\frac{2\times13.6\ \text{eV}}{hn^3}
$$

**Classical side.** The orbital frequency is $f=v/2\pi r$. With $v=n\hbar/mr$ and $r=a_0n^2$:

$$
f_\text{orb}=\frac{n\hbar}{2\pi mr^2}=\frac{n\hbar}{2\pi ma_0^2n^4}=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi ma_0^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^3}
$$

**Both go as $1/n^3$**, and putting the constants in shows the coefficients match too.

**For a feel for the numbers**: at $n=2\to1$ the two differ by more than a factor of two; at $n=100\to99$ by under 2%; at $n=1000$ they are indistinguishable.

Quantum effects do not "vanish" at large $n$; they simply become impossible to tell apart from the classical answer. The same idea reappears in another guise in [section 2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/).

</Derivation>

## Where it goes wrong

The Bohr model is absurdly accurate for hydrogen, but it is a **semi-classical patchwork**: half Newtonian orbit, half quantum rule, and the two are fundamentally incompatible.

<Callout type="warning" title="Three things it cannot do">
**1. It cannot handle helium.** Add a second electron and the model fails immediately, with errors too large to be useful. Helium is the second simplest atom there is.

**2. It cannot give line intensities.** It tells you where the lines are but not which are bright and which are faint, nor why some transitions never happen at all (selection rules).

**3. The very concept of an "orbit" is wrong.** This is the serious one. Bohr has the electron moving on a circle of radius $a_0$ carrying a definite angular momentum $\hbar$.

Correct quantum mechanics says the hydrogen ground state has angular momentum $L=0$ (not $\hbar$), the electron has no definite trajectory at all, and $a_0$ is the characteristic scale of a probability distribution rather than the radius of a circle. See [section 6.3](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/03-hydrogen-solution/).

**It got the levels right and the picture wrong.**
</Callout>

<Callout type="misconception" title="That textbook picture of an electron circling a nucleus">
The familiar logo-like image of electrons on elliptical paths around a nucleus is the Bohr model — and it has been superseded for a century.

A real atom is closer to a structured cloud of probability: spherical s orbitals, dumbbell-shaped p orbitals, four-lobed d orbitals. By [chapter 6](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/) you can rotate them yourself in a 3D scene.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Bohr's quantisation condition $L=n\hbar$ is still a rule with no reason behind it. **Why integer multiples of $\hbar$ in particular?**

Ten years later a doctoral student gave an unexpected answer: because the electron is a wave, and the orbit has to hold a whole number of wavelengths.
