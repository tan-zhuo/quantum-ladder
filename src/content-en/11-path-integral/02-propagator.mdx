The last section laid out the manifesto: the amplitude from $a$ to $b$ is the sum of $\ee^{\ii S/\hbar}$ over all paths. But "sum over all paths" is still just a sentence — there are uncountably many paths, so how do you add them? In this section we actually compute the sum, for the simplest system there is: the free particle ($V=0$).

First, let us give this "amplitude from $a$ to $b$" its official name.

## What the propagator is

The **propagator** $K(x_b,t_b;x_a,t_a)$ is defined as the probability amplitude to find the particle at $x_b$ at time $t_b$, given that it was definitely at $x_a$ at time $t_a$. In the language of chapter 3 it is a matrix element of the evolution operator:

$$
K(x_b,t_b;x_a,t_a)=\bra{x_b}\ee^{-\ii\hat H(t_b-t_a)/\hbar}\ket{x_a}
$$

Why does it deserve a name of its own? Because **knowing $K$ means knowing all of the dynamics**. Any initial state $\Psi(x_a,t_a)$ is a superposition of position eigenstates, each component propagating independently, so

$$
\Psi(x_b,t_b)=\int K(x_b,t_b;x_a,t_a)\,\Psi(x_a,t_a)\,\dd x_a
$$

The propagator is the Schrödinger equation's "universal solution": solve for $K$ once, and the solution for any initial condition is a single integral away. (Mathematically this is exactly the role of a Green's function: $K$ satisfies the Schrödinger equation with the initial condition $K\to\delta(x_b-x_a)$ as $t_b\to t_a$ — at the start the particle is at $x_a$, with zero amplitude anywhere else.)

$K$ also has a **composition** property, inherited directly from the fact that paths can be spliced: going from $a$ to an intermediate point $c$ and then from $c$ to $b$, summed over all intermediate points, equals going from $a$ to $b$ directly:

$$
K(b,a)=\int K(b,c)\,K(c,a)\,\dd x_c
$$

This property is about to earn its keep — it lets us chop a long propagation into many short segments.

## Defining the path integral properly: time slicing

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**How do you index "all paths"?** The same old trick as the definite integral: discretise first, take the limit after.

Slice the interval $t_a\to t_b$ into $N$ steps of length $\varepsilon=(t_b-t_a)/N$. A path is specified by its positions at the slice times, $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{N-1}$ (the endpoints $x_0=x_a$, $x_N=x_b$ are fixed), joined by straight segments.

"Sum over all paths" then means: let each $x_j$ range independently over the whole real line — an ordinary $(N-1)$-fold integral — and finally take $N\to\infty$. Last section's picture of "inserting infinitely many boards that are all slit" becomes a rigorous definition here.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
K(b,a)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1A\int\frac{\dd x_1}{A}\cdots\frac{\dd x_{N-1}}{A}\;\ee^{\ii S(x_0,\dots,x_N)/\hbar}
$$

where the action of the zigzag path accumulates segment by segment:

$$
S=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\varepsilon\left[\frac{m}{2}\Bigl(\frac{x_{j+1}-x_j}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^2-V(x_j)\right]
$$

$A$ is a per-step normalisation constant (needed for the limit to exist); the derivation below fixes it along the way:

$$
A=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon}{m}}
$$

</div>
</DualView>

## The free particle: computing the integral to the end

<Derivation title="The full derivation of the free-particle propagator" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

With $V=0$ we face a chain of nested Gaussian-type integrals. Only one tool is needed throughout — the Gaussian integral formula (valid for complex $\alpha$ too, understood by analytic continuation):

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ee^{\ii\alpha u^2}\dd u=\sqrt{\frac{\pi\ii}{\alpha}}
$$

**Step 1: the single-step propagator.** Over a short interval $\varepsilon$ there is effectively only the straight-line "path", with amplitude

$$
K_\varepsilon(x_{j+1},x_j)=\frac1A\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x_{j+1}-x_j)^2}{2\hbar\varepsilon}\right]
$$

**Step 2: compose two steps.** Use the composition formula to merge two $\varepsilon$ steps into one of $2\varepsilon$, integrating over the intermediate point $x_1$:

$$
K_{2\varepsilon}(x_2,x_0)=\frac1{A^2}\int\dd x_1\,
\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m}{2\hbar\varepsilon}\bigl[(x_2-x_1)^2+(x_1-x_0)^2\bigr]\right]
$$

Complete the square in $x_1$ inside the exponent:

$$
(x_2-x_1)^2+(x_1-x_0)^2=2\Bigl(x_1-\frac{x_0+x_2}{2}\Bigr)^2+\frac{(x_2-x_0)^2}{2}
$$

The second term is independent of $x_1$ and comes outside; the first is killed by the Gaussian formula (set $u=x_1-\frac{x_0+x_2}2$, $\alpha=\frac{m}{\hbar\varepsilon}$):

$$
K_{2\varepsilon}(x_2,x_0)=\frac1{A^2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon}{m}}\,
\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x_2-x_0)^2}{2\hbar(2\varepsilon)}\right]
$$

**Key observation**: the result has exactly the same shape as the single-step propagator, just with $\varepsilon\to2\varepsilon$. Choosing $A=\sqrt{2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon/m}$ makes the prefactor tidy up to exactly $\sqrt{m/2\pi\ii\hbar(2\varepsilon)}$ — this is how the normalisation constant gets fixed "self-consistently".

**Step 3: induction to N steps.** Each intermediate point integrated away leaves the propagator with the same shape and one more time step. After $N$ steps (with $N\varepsilon=t$, writing $t=t_b-t_a$, $x=x_b-x_a$):

$$
\boxed{\;K(x_b,t_b;x_a,t_a)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\ii\hbar t}}\,
\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x_b-x_a)^2}{2\hbar t}\right]\;}
$$

The limit $N\to\infty$ is trivial — the shape never depended on $N$ in the first place.

**Step 4: checks.**
- As $t\to0$ the exponent oscillates infinitely fast and the prefactor diverges — precisely one representation of $\delta(x_b-x_a)$. The initial condition checks out.
- Substitute back into the Schrödinger equation $\ii\hbar\partial_t K=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_{x_b}^2K$ and the two sides match term by term (verify it yourself — five lines).

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="The phase is exactly the classical action">
The classical action for a free particle travelling from $x_a$ to $x_b$ at constant speed (in time $t$) is

$$
S_{\text{cl}}=\int_0^t\frac{m}{2}\Bigl(\frac{x_b-x_a}{t}\Bigr)^2\dd t'=\frac{m(x_b-x_a)^2}{2t}
$$

Compare with the result of the derivation: $K\propto\ee^{\ii S_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}$ — after summing infinitely many paths, the phase comes out **exactly** equal to the action of the one classical path, with every quantum correction absorbed into the prefactor $\sqrt{m/2\pi\ii\hbar t}$.

This is not luck reserved for the free particle: whenever the Lagrangian is quadratic in coordinates and velocities (free particle, harmonic oscillator, uniform field), the path integral equals $F(t)\,\ee^{\ii S_{\text{cl}}/\hbar}$ exactly, with the fluctuation part independent of the endpoints. In quadratic systems the last section's stationary-phase argument is promoted from "approximation" to "exact".
</Callout>

## Squaring the books with wave-packet spreading

If the propagator is right, it has to reproduce physics we already know. [Section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) computed, by superposing momenta, that a free Gaussian wave packet of initial width $\sigma_0$ spreads with time. Let us redo the calculation with $K$.

Take the initial state to be a Gaussian packet centred at the origin, $\Psi(x_a,0)\propto\ee^{-x_a^2/4\sigma_0^2}$, and insert it into the evolution formula:

$$
\Psi(x,t)=\int K(x,t;x_a,0)\,\Psi(x_a,0)\,\dd x_a
\;\propto\;\int\dd x_a\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x-x_a)^2}{2\hbar t}-\frac{x_a^2}{4\sigma_0^2}\right]
$$

The exponent is again quadratic in $x_a$ — yet another Gaussian integral. Complete the square, integrate, and tidy up:

$$
|\Psi(x,t)|^2\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma(t)^2}\right],\qquad
\sigma(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\Bigl(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\Bigr)^2}
$$

Word for word the result of section 2.10: spreading time $\tau=2m\sigma_0^2/\hbar$, and the narrower the packet, the faster it spreads. Two languages, one physics — the first concrete audit of their equivalence.

<Callout type="warning" title="The propagator is not a probability — don't rush to square it">
For the free particle, $|K(b,a)|^2$ equals $m/2\pi\hbar t$, independent of $x_b$ — and this is not some sensible "the particle is equally likely everywhere" distribution (it is not even normalisable). The reason is that the initial state $\delta(x-x_a)$ is not a physically realisable state (infinitely narrow, infinite momentum uncertainty). The correct use of $K$ is as an **integral kernel**: sandwiched between genuine, normalisable wavefunctions.
</Callout>

## What comes next

We have now computed a concrete result from the path-integral side and verified that it satisfies the Schrödinger equation. But "verifying one example" is not "proving the two formulations equivalent". The next section does something more thorough: starting from a short time slice, it **derives** the Schrödinger equation itself — turning chapter 2's starting point into a theorem of the path integral.
