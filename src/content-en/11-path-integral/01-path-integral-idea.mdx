For ten chapters we have spoken quantum mechanics in only one language: the wavefunction obeys the Schrödinger equation, $\ii\hbar\partial_t\Psi=\hat H\Psi$, and given the initial state you solve for the future. That language has served us well, but it hides the most naive question of all — "which way did the particle go?" The wavefunction spreads over all of space, and you simply cannot say which route the particle took.

In 1948 Feynman gave us a second language. Its starting point is exactly the glaring fact we left hanging in [section 1.6](/en/ch/01-history/06-double-slit/): in the double-slit experiment, the arrival pattern of a single electron somehow "knows" about both slits. Back then we said "the electron's wave passes through both slits at once." Feynman pushed that sentence to its logical extreme.

## From two slits to infinitely many

Recall the double-slit rule: the total amplitude to arrive at a point on the screen is the **sum** of the amplitudes of the two routes,

$$
A_{\text{tot}}=A_{\text{slit}\,1}+A_{\text{slit}\,2}
$$

The probability is $|A_{\text{tot}}|^2$, and the cross term is the interference fringes.

Now run a thought experiment, upgrading it in three steps:

**Step 1: more slits.** Cut three slits in the barrier, then four… then $N$. The rule does not change: the total amplitude is the sum over $N$ routes.

**Step 2: more screens.** Insert a second barrier between source and screen, also riddled with slits. A "path" must now specify which slit of the first barrier the particle threads, then which slit of the second — the number of paths multiplies. The total amplitude is still the sum over **all** of these zigzag routes.

**Step 3: remove the boards.** Cut ever more slits in each barrier until the whole board is slit — that is, remove the board; then pack the barriers ever more densely, until there is a "board" at every point of space. The zigzag "which slits in which order" now becomes an **arbitrary continuous path** from source to screen.

What is left once every board is gone? Vacuum. But the summation rule has no reason to fail at the instant the last board is removed. We are forced to a startling conclusion:

<Callout type="insight" title="The central statement of the path integral">
The total amplitude for a particle to go from the spacetime point $(x_a,t_a)$ to $(x_b,t_b)$ is the sum of the amplitudes of **all** paths connecting the two points — not just the straight line, but every detour, every backtrack, every path that jitters beyond recognition:

$$
K(b,a)=\sum_{\text{all paths}\,x(t)}A[x(t)]
$$

The double slit is not a special case so much as this rule degenerating to "only two routes left open". Free propagation through vacuum is the same rule with "every route open".
</Callout>

## What does each path contribute?

The first half of the rule (sum over paths) was forced on us by the double slit. The second half must answer: what is the amplitude $A[x(t)]$ of a single path?

Feynman's answer is startlingly short: **every path has the same magnitude of amplitude; only the phase differs**, and the phase is set by the path's **action**:

$$
A[x(t)]=\text{(const)}\times\ee^{\ii S[x(t)]/\hbar}
$$

The action is an old friend from classical mechanics: along a given path, accumulate "kinetic minus potential energy" (the Lagrangian $L$) over time,

$$
S[x(t)]=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}L\,\dd t=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\left[\frac{m\dot x^2}{2}-V(x)\right]\dd t
$$

In plain words: $S$ is a "score" assigned to each path — moving fast earns kinetic-energy points, loitering where the potential is high loses points. Classical mechanics' **principle of least action** says the true path is the one that makes $S$ stationary (usually a minimum) — Newton's equation is precisely the mathematical statement of $\delta S=0$.

$S$ has dimensions of energy × time, the same as $\hbar$, so $S/\hbar$ is a pure number and can serve as a phase angle. That is no coincidence — we are about to see that the ratio of $S$ to $\hbar$ is exactly what decides "how quantum" a system is.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The little-arrow picture.** Think of each path as a small arrow of fixed length, pointing at the angle $S/\hbar$. Computing the total amplitude means chaining infinitely many little arrows tip to tail.

If two neighbouring paths have nearly the same action, their arrows point nearly the same way and reinforce each other when added; if their actions differ by something of order $\pi\hbar$, the arrows point opposite ways and cancel.

So the question becomes: **which band of paths has an action insensitive to deformations of the path?** In that band the arrows all point the same way — they dominate the sum.

</div>
<div slot="right">

Writing the sum over paths in formal notation (a weighted infinite-dimensional integral):

$$
K(b,a)=\int\mathcal{D}x(t)\;\ee^{\ii S[x(t)]/\hbar}
$$

$\int\mathcal{D}x(t)$ reads "integrate over all paths"; its rigorous definition (a limit of time slices) waits until [section 11.2](/en/ch/11-path-integral/02-propagator/).

The first-order variation of the action with respect to the path:

$$
S[x+\eta]=S[x]+\delta S[\eta]+O(\eta^2)
$$

The path with $\delta S=0$ is the classical path $x_{\text{cl}}(t)$ — the one obeying Newton's equation.

</div>
</DualView>

## Where did the classical world go? Stationary phase

If an electron really takes every route, why does a baseball appear to fly along a single parabola? The answer hides in the **cancellation** of phases.

<Derivation title="The stationary-phase argument: why only the Newtonian path survives as ħ→0" level="基础" minutes={7}>

Examine the contributions of two classes of paths.

**Class 1: paths far from the classical one.** Take a path $x(t)$ with $\delta S\neq0$, meaning any slight deformation changes the action at first order. Call the deformation $\eta$; the phase changes by roughly $\delta S[\eta]/\hbar$.

The key is the $\hbar$ in the denominator. For a macroscopic system the typical $S$ is enormously larger than $\hbar$ (we estimate it below), so even a deformation $\eta$ too small to measure produces a huge phase change: the little arrows of neighbouring paths spin wildly, pointing at every possible angle. Add them up — **near-total cancellation**.

This is the same phenomenon as the integral $\int\ee^{\ii\lambda f(u)}\dd u$ tending to zero as $\lambda\to\infty$ (the rapidly oscillating positive and negative parts wipe each other out).

**Class 2: paths near the classical one.** At $x_{\text{cl}}$ we have $\delta S=0$, so the action responds to deformations only at **second** order:

$$
S[x_{\text{cl}}+\eta]=S[x_{\text{cl}}]+O(\eta^2)
$$

There is therefore a whole swath of paths with nearly identical phases; their arrows align and reinforce. The width of this "coherence band" is set by the $O(\eta^2)$ term reaching a phase of order $\pi$: for a free particle one can estimate $\eta\sim\sqrt{\hbar T/m}$ ($T$ being the time of flight).

**Conclusion.** The total amplitude comes almost entirely from a bundle of paths of width about $\sqrt{\hbar T/m}$ around the classical one. As $\hbar\to0$ (or $S\gg\hbar$) this bundle shrinks to a single line — the particle "takes the Newtonian path".

**Order-of-magnitude check.** A baseball ($m=0.145$ kg) flying at 40 m/s for 0.5 s has $S\sim E_k T\approx116\times0.5\approx58$ J·s, so

$$
\frac{S}{\hbar}\approx\frac{58}{1.05\times10^{-34}}\approx5\times10^{35}
$$

Stray from the classical path by even $10^{-17}$ m and the phase winds through many full turns. The baseball has no choice. The electron in the double slit, by contrast, has an action difference between its two routes of order $\hbar$ — which is why it "takes both".

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="The mystery of least action, solved">
Classical mechanics carried an odd question no one ever answered: how does a particle "know" which path has the least action? Did it try all of them first?

The path integral's answer: **yes, it literally tried them all.** Every path is taken; every path contributes an amplitude. It is just that the non-stationary-phase contributions cancel one another, and what survives is precisely the $\delta S=0$ path. The principle of least action is not a mysterious preference of nature — it is interference's survivorship bias.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two easy misconceptions">
1. **"All paths" is wilder than you think.** The overwhelming majority of paths in the sum are everywhere continuous but nowhere differentiable — jagged like Brownian trajectories. Smooth paths form a set of measure zero. "The particle followed some smooth trajectory" is only an approximate statement valid in the macroscopic limit.
2. **The path integral is not a new theory.** It makes exactly the same predictions as the Schrödinger equation ([section 11.3](/en/ch/11-path-integral/03-to-schrodinger/) proves the equivalence) — it is the same quantum mechanics in different notation. Some problems are easier with wavefunctions (bound states, energy levels), others with path integrals (tunnelling, field theory, the link to statistical mechanics) — one more well-made tool in the box.
</Callout>

## What comes next

"Sum over all paths" is still only a manifesto. To make it compute actual numbers, we must first define $\int\mathcal{D}x(t)$ properly and then carry the infinite-dimensional integral through completely for the simplest system — the free particle. The result is called the **propagator**, and it will lock onto the wave-packet spreading of chapter 2 with a satisfying click.
