Last section we computed the free-particle propagator and checked that it satisfies the Schrödinger equation — but that was only one example. Now we settle a much larger debt: in chapter 2 we accepted the Schrödinger equation as a **postulate**; this section proves that once you accept the single rule "sum over all paths, each contributing $\ee^{\ii S/\hbar}$", the Schrödinger equation is no longer a postulate but a **consequence**. This is the central calculation of Feynman's 1948 paper, and every step uses nothing beyond first-year calculus.

## Strategy: look at just one short interval

Tackling the finite-time path integral head-on is hard (for a general potential it cannot be done in closed form). But a differential equation only ever talks about "what happens in the next instant" — so all we need is the short-time behaviour.

Take a short interval $\varepsilon$. In the slicing definition, this interval contains just a single step: the particle goes in a straight line from $x'$ to $x$. By the zigzag rule the action of this step is

$$
S_\varepsilon\approx\frac{m(x-x')^2}{2\varepsilon}-\varepsilon V\!\Bigl(\frac{x+x'}{2}\Bigr)
$$

The kinetic term uses the average velocity $(x-x')/\varepsilon$; the potential term uses the midpoint value times the duration (to first order in $\varepsilon$, midpoint, endpoint or average all give the same result — see the warning box at the end). The **short-time propagator** is therefore

$$
K_\varepsilon(x,x')=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon}}\,
\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x-x')^2}{2\hbar\varepsilon}-\frac{\ii\varepsilon}{\hbar}V\!\Bigl(\frac{x+x'}{2}\Bigr)\right]
$$

The evolution formula for this one small step becomes an integral equation:

$$
\Psi(x,t+\varepsilon)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_\varepsilon(x,x')\,\Psi(x',t)\,\dd x'
$$

From here the whole game is: expand both sides to first order in $\varepsilon$ and see what equation gets forced out.

## Which x′ actually contribute?

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Read the structure of the integral before computing.** The fiercest factor in the integrand is

$$
\exp\!\left[\frac{\ii m(x-x')^2}{2\hbar\varepsilon}\right]
$$

When $x'$ is far from $x$, $(x-x')^2/\varepsilon$ is large and this phase oscillates wildly with $x'$, so the contributions cancel — the stationary-phase mechanism of two sections ago, replayed within a single step.

What survives is the range over which the phase varies by no more than $\sim1$:

$$
\frac{m(x-x')^2}{2\hbar\varepsilon}\lesssim1
\;\Longrightarrow\;
|x-x'|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar\varepsilon}{m}}
$$

In one step $\varepsilon$ the particle only "probes" a neighbourhood of size $\sqrt{\hbar\varepsilon/m}$. That is why we can safely Taylor-expand in $x'-x$ below.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**A remarkable property, read off in passing.** The typical displacement per step is $\delta x\sim\sqrt{\hbar\varepsilon/m}$, so the typical "velocity" is

$$
\frac{\delta x}{\varepsilon}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\varepsilon}}
\xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon\to0\ }\infty
$$

Displacement scales as $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, not $\varepsilon$ — exactly the scaling of Brownian motion (a diffusion process). Typical paths in the path integral are everywhere continuous, nowhere differentiable, and an instantaneous velocity simply does not exist. This meshes perfectly with the uncertainty principle: slicing time more finely means pinning the position more tightly, so the momentum runs ever wilder.

</div>
</DualView>

## The derivation

<Derivation title="From the short-time propagator to the Schrödinger equation" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Step 1: change variables.** Let $\eta=x'-x$ (the displacement of this step); the integral equation becomes

$$
\Psi(x,t+\varepsilon)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon}}
\int\dd\eta\;\ee^{\ii m\eta^2/2\hbar\varepsilon}\;
\ee^{-\ii\varepsilon V(x+\eta/2)/\hbar}\;\Psi(x+\eta,t)
$$

**Step 2: count the orders of each factor.** The effective $\eta\sim\sqrt{\hbar\varepsilon/m}$, so $\eta^2\sim\varepsilon$: to keep first order in $\varepsilon$, $\Psi$ must be expanded to **second** order in $\eta$ — the single most important judgement in the whole derivation (and the origin of the second spatial derivative):

$$
\Psi(x+\eta,t)=\Psi+\eta\,\partial_x\Psi+\frac{\eta^2}2\,\partial_x^2\Psi+O(\eta^3)
$$

In the potential factor, $\varepsilon V$ is already first-order small, so $V(x+\eta/2)\to V(x)$ suffices:

$$
\ee^{-\ii\varepsilon V/\hbar}=1-\frac{\ii\varepsilon}{\hbar}V(x)+O(\varepsilon^2)
$$

**Step 3: do the Gaussian integrals term by term.** Three moments are needed (all standard Gaussian integrals, with $\alpha\equiv m/2\hbar\varepsilon$):

$$
\int\ee^{\ii\alpha\eta^2}\dd\eta=\sqrt{\frac{\pi\ii}{\alpha}},\qquad
\int\eta\,\ee^{\ii\alpha\eta^2}\dd\eta=0,\qquad
\int\eta^2\,\ee^{\ii\alpha\eta^2}\dd\eta=\frac{\ii}{2\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi\ii}{\alpha}}
$$

(The second vanishes because the integrand is odd; the third follows from the first by differentiating with respect to $\alpha$.) Insert $\alpha=m/2\hbar\varepsilon$: the zeroth moment exactly cancels the normalisation prefactor $\sqrt{m/2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon}$ — total coefficient 1 — and the second moment brings an extra factor $\ii\hbar\varepsilon/m$.

**Step 4: collect to first order in ε.** Multiply the pieces together and keep only $\varepsilon^1$:

$$
\Psi(x,t+\varepsilon)=\Psi(x,t)
+\frac{\ii\hbar\varepsilon}{2m}\,\partial_x^2\Psi
-\frac{\ii\varepsilon}{\hbar}V(x)\,\Psi
+O(\varepsilon^2)
$$

(Cross terms such as $\varepsilon V\times\eta^2\partial_x^2\Psi$ are of order $\varepsilon^2$ and get discarded.)

**Step 5: take the limit.** Write the left side as $\Psi(x,t)+\varepsilon\,\partial_t\Psi+O(\varepsilon^2)$, cancel $\Psi(x,t)$ from both sides, divide by $\varepsilon$, let $\varepsilon\to0$, and multiply through by $\ii\hbar$:

$$
\boxed{\;\ii\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\,\Psi\;}
$$

The Schrödinger equation. Chapter 2's postulate has just become a theorem.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="Every term has a birthplace">
It pays to look back at how the parts of this machine mesh:

- **$\ii\hbar\partial_t$**: from expanding one step of evolution to first order in $\varepsilon$ — the first time derivative reflects the "one step" structure.
- **The kinetic term $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2$**: from the second moment of the kinetic phase $\ee^{\ii m\eta^2/2\hbar\varepsilon}$. The more violently the path jitters (the larger $\partial_x^2\Psi$), the larger this term — the kinetic-energy operator measures exactly the "bending" of the wavefunction, in full accord with the reading in [section 2.3](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/03-time-independent-schrodinger/).
- **The potential term $V\Psi$**: from the first-order expansion of $\ee^{-\ii\varepsilon V/\hbar}$ — every instant spent in the potential accumulates phase.

The coefficients match exactly, neither more nor less, and this depends on the normalisation constant $A=\sqrt{2\pi\ii\hbar\varepsilon/m}$ taking the value fixed by composition self-consistency last section. Three sources, one equation, a perfect fit.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two technical traps">
1. **Where do you evaluate the potential?** We used the midpoint, $V\bigl((x+x')/2\bigr)$. To first order in $\varepsilon$ the endpoint works just as well — because $V(x+\eta/2)-V(x)\sim\eta\sim\sqrt\varepsilon$, and multiplied by the existing $\varepsilon$ it is a higher-order correction. But the moment the potential depends on velocity (for instance the $\vec A\cdot\dot{\vec x}$ term in a magnetic field), the midpoint rule is **no longer optional** — different choices differ by exactly the operator-ordering ambiguity. This is the most famous fine print in the correspondence between path integrals and operator language.
2. **Count the expansion orders correctly.** The classic beginner's mistake is expanding $\Psi(x+\eta)$ only to first order in $\eta$ — the kinetic term simply vanishes. The criterion is always "$\eta^2\sim\varepsilon$": wherever you intend to keep $\varepsilon^1$, you must keep $\eta$ to second order.
</Callout>

At this point the three formulations formally join forces: Heisenberg's matrices (1925), Schrödinger's wave equation (1926), Feynman's sum over paths (1948) — three formal languages, one quantum mechanics. Chapter 3 proved the first two equivalent; this section supplies the third piece of the puzzle.

## What comes next

With the equivalence proven, the path integral might seem to have merely "re-derived what we already knew". The next section lets it show off a trick the wavefunction language finds very hard: rotate time onto the imaginary axis, and tunnelling becomes a **classical** mechanics problem — hiding beneath the barrier is a classical path called the "instanton".
