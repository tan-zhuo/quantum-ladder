The previous section noted that linear operators come in infinitely many kinds. This section asks: **which kind earns the title of "observable"?**

## Working backwards from the physics

Set the mathematics aside for a moment and list the three things physics cannot do without:

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Requirement one: measurement results are real numbers.**

Measure a length, an energy, a spin — the readout is always real. So the eigenvalues of $\hat A$ must be real.

**Requirement two: distinct results can be told apart.**

If the result is $a_1$, the state should land among "the states belonging to $a_1$", with no overlap with "the states belonging to $a_2$". Mathematically: eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

**Requirement three: every state can be resolved by this measurement.**

For $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$ to hold, the eigenvectors must form a complete basis, so that any state can be expanded.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The answer: Hermitian operators**

Taken together, the three requirements single out exactly one class of operator. Define the **Hermitian conjugate** $\hat A^\dagger$ of $\hat A$ as the operator satisfying

$$
\bra{\phi}\hat A^\dagger\ket{\psi}=\bra{\psi}\hat A\ket{\phi}^*
$$

If

$$
\hat A^\dagger=\hat A
$$

then $\hat A$ is **Hermitian** (self-adjoint).

In matrix language: $A^\dagger=(A^{\mathsf T})^*$, and Hermitian means $A_{mn}=A_{nm}^*$ — **transpose-and-conjugate equals itself**. The diagonal elements must therefore be real.

</div>
</DualView>

## Cashing in the three requirements, one by one

<Derivation title="Proof one: Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues" level="基础" minutes={3} open={true}>

Let $\hat A\ket{a}=a\ket{a}$ with $\braket{a}{a}=1$. Multiply on the left by $\bra{a}$:

$$
\bra{a}\hat A\ket{a}=a
$$

Take the complex conjugate and use Hermiticity, $\hat A^\dagger=\hat A$:

$$
a^*=\bra{a}\hat A\ket{a}^*=\bra{a}\hat A^\dagger\ket{a}=\bra{a}\hat A\ket{a}=a
$$

So $a=a^*$: the eigenvalue is real. $\blacksquare$

**Compare**: proving the same thing took [section 2.3](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/03-time-independent-schrodinger/) half a page — conjugate, subtract, massage into a total derivative, integrate by parts, kill the boundary term via normalisation.

That half page was not wasted: it was **verifying, in the concrete**, the Hermiticity of $\hat H$ (integration by parts shifts the derivative from one side to the other with vanishing boundary term — exactly the content of $\bra{\phi}\hat H\ket{\psi}=\bra{\hat H\phi}\ket{\psi}$).

**Switching languages is not a magic trick: it abstracts that half page once and for all, so it never has to be repeated.**

</Derivation>

<Derivation title="Proof two: eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal" level="基础" minutes={4}>

Let $\hat A\ket{a_1}=a_1\ket{a_1}$ and $\hat A\ket{a_2}=a_2\ket{a_2}$ with $a_1\ne a_2$.

Evaluate $\bra{a_1}\hat A\ket{a_2}$ two ways:

**Acting to the right**: $\bra{a_1}\hat A\ket{a_2}=a_2\braket{a_1}{a_2}$

**Acting to the left**: by Hermiticity, $\hat A$ acting on the bra gives $a_1^*=a_1$ (already proved real), so $=a_1\braket{a_1}{a_2}$

Subtract:

$$
(a_1-a_2)\braket{a_1}{a_2}=0
$$

Since $a_1\ne a_2$, we get $\braket{a_1}{a_2}=0$. $\blacksquare$

**The degenerate case**: if $a_1=a_2$ the equation holds trivially and orthogonality does not follow. But inside the eigenspace you can manufacture an orthogonal basis by Gram-Schmidt. So **an orthonormal set of eigenvectors can always be chosen**.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="The spectral theorem: requirement three">
The third requirement (completeness) runs deeper than the first two — it is the content of the **spectral theorem**:

> In a finite-dimensional space, every Hermitian operator can be diagonalised, and its eigenvectors form an orthonormal complete basis.

The infinite-dimensional version needs more conditions (boundedness, self-adjointness), but it holds for the observables that actually occur in physics.

In Dirac notation, the spectral theorem states the **spectral decomposition**:

$$
\hat A=\sum_n a_n\ket{a_n}\bra{a_n}
$$

together with

$$
\sum_n\ket{a_n}\bra{a_n}=\hat 1
$$

**The second equation is the completeness relation of [section 3.2](/en/ch/03-formalism/02-dirac-notation/).** Back then we took it as given; now we know where it comes from: the eigenvectors of any observable automatically form a basis.

Chapter 2's claim that "$\{\psi_n\}$ is complete, so any state can be expanded" — here, at last, is the reason.
</Callout>

## Some examples

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Hermitian**

- $\hat x$ (position): in the position basis, multiplication by the real number $x$
- $\hat p=-\ii\hbar\partial_x$: the $\ii$ and the derivative each contribute one minus sign, which cancel
- $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V(\hat x)$ (with $V$ real)
- the projection operator $\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}$
- the Pauli matrices $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z$

**Not Hermitian**

- $\hat a_\pm$ (ladder operators): $\hat a_+^\dagger=\hat a_-$ — conjugates of each other, not of themselves
- $\hat x\hat p$: see the right-hand column
- $\partial_x$ by itself (missing that $\ii$)

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Why $\hat p$ must carry the $\ii$**

$$
\bra{\phi}\partial_x\ket{\psi}=\int\phi^*\psi'\,\dd x
=-\int\phi'^*\psi\,\dd x=-\bra{\partial_x\phi}\ket{\psi}
$$

(integration by parts; the boundary term vanishes by normalisation). So $\partial_x^\dagger=-\partial_x$ — **anti-Hermitian**.

Multiply by $-\ii\hbar$ and conjugation sends $\ii\to-\ii$, contributing a second minus sign; the two cancel:

$$
\hat p^\dagger=(-\ii\hbar\partial_x)^\dagger=(+\ii\hbar)(-\partial_x)=-\ii\hbar\partial_x=\hat p
$$

[Section 2.6](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/06-expectation-and-uncertainty/) said "that $\ii$ makes $\hat p$ Hermitian" — this is the full verification.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The product of two Hermitian operators is generally not Hermitian">
Let $\hat A^\dagger=\hat A$ and $\hat B^\dagger=\hat B$. Conjugation reverses order:

$$
(\hat A\hat B)^\dagger=\hat B^\dagger\hat A^\dagger=\hat B\hat A
$$

which equals $\hat A\hat B$ only when $\hat A\hat B=\hat B\hat A$ (they commute).

**So $\hat x\hat p$ is not an observable**, because $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ do not commute. To build the corresponding observable you take the symmetrised combination

$$
\frac{\hat x\hat p+\hat p\hat x}{2}
$$

This step is compulsory whenever the classical quantity contains $xp$, and it is called the **ordering problem** — classically $xp=px$, yet quantisation forces you to pick an order by hand. That ambiguity is intrinsic to the very process of "quantisation", not a technical inconvenience.
</Callout>

## What comes next

There can be many Hermitian operators: $\hat x$, $\hat p$, $\hat H$, $\hat L_z$...

A natural question: **can two observables be measured sharply at the same time?** The answer hinges on whether their product cares about the order. The next section upgrades the uncertainty principle from "a corollary of Fourier analysis" to a theorem that holds for any pair of observables.
