The toolkit is complete. This section settles the accounts: **translate every result of chapter 2 into the new language**, then tally up what was saved.

This is not revision. Some proofs will shrink from half a page to three lines; others will turn from "solve a partial differential equation" into "do some algebra" — and **the second kind is the real prize**.

## The infinite square well, written as matrices

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Chapter 2's version**

$$
\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}
$$

$$
E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}
$$

$$
\int_0^L\psi_m^*\psi_n\dd x=\delta_{mn}
$$

$$
\Psi(x,0)=\sum_nc_n\psi_n(x)
$$

$$
c_n=\int_0^L\psi_n^*\Psi\,\dd x
$$

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The new version**

$$
\hat H\ket{n}=E_n\ket{n}
$$

$$
\braket{m}{n}=\delta_{mn},\qquad \sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=\hat 1
$$

$$
\ket{\Psi}=\sum_nc_n\ket{n},\qquad c_n=\braket{n}{\Psi}
$$

**The wavefunctions are just components**: $\psi_n(x)=\braket{x}{n}$.

In the energy basis, $\hat H$ is a diagonal matrix:

$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}E_1&&&\\&E_2&&\\&&E_3&\\&&&\ddots\end{pmatrix}
$$

</div>
</DualView>

<Derivation title="Time evolution: three lines" level="基础" minutes={3} open={true}>

$$
\ket{\Psi(t)}=\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{\Psi(0)}
=\sum_nc_n\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{n}
=\sum_nc_n\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}
$$

Done. The third equality is [section 3.4](/en/ch/03-formalism/04-linear-operators/)'s rule "$f(\hat A)\ket{a}=f(a)\ket{a}$".

**Where does the sloshing of the probability density come from?**

$$
|\Psi(x,t)|^2=\Big|\sum_nc_n\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\braket{x}{n}\Big|^2
$$

Expand, and the cross terms carry a factor $\ee^{-\ii(E_n-E_m)t/\hbar}$. In the $n=m$ terms the phases cancel (nothing changes with time); the $n\ne m$ terms oscillate at the Bohr frequencies $\omega_{nm}=(E_n-E_m)/\hbar$.

[Section 2.5](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/05-stationary-states/) spent a whole section on "stationary states sit still, superpositions slosh". Here it is two lines — **and you can read off at a glance that the sloshing frequency is set entirely by the level spacing**.

</Derivation>

<Sim name="infinite-well" />

<Callout type="check" title="Check it against the simulation">
In the simulation, set $c_1$ and $c_2$ equal and watch the sloshing period; then switch to $c_1$ and $c_3$.

By the analysis above, the frequency ratio should be $(E_3-E_1)/(E_2-E_1)=8/3$. That was a quiz question back in [section 2.5](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/05-stationary-states/) — now you can read it straight off the formula, with no need to compute anything again.
</Callout>

## The harmonic oscillator: a purely algebraic solution

[Section 2.9](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/09-harmonic-oscillator/) already used ladder operators, but Dirac notation was not yet available then. Rewritten in the new language, the structure comes out far more clearly.

<Derivation title="The complete algebraic solution, without solving a single differential equation" level="进阶" minutes={10} open={true}>

**The only starting point**: $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$. Define

$$
\hat a=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x+\frac{\ii\hat p}{m\omega}\right),\qquad
\hat a^\dagger=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat x-\frac{\ii\hat p}{m\omega}\right)
$$

(Writing $\hat a^\dagger$ rather than $\hat a_\pm$ is the standard convention; note that $\hat a$ is **not Hermitian** — $\hat a^\dagger$ is its adjoint.)

**Step one: the commutator.**

$$
[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\cdot\frac{2\ii}{m\omega}[\hat p,\hat x]=1
$$

**Step two: the Hamiltonian.** Define the **number operator** $\hat N=\hat a^\dagger\hat a$; then

$$
\hat H=\hbar\omega\left(\hat N+\frac12\right)
$$

So the problem becomes: **find the eigenvalues of $\hat N$.**

**Step three: the eigenvalues of $\hat N$ are non-negative.** Let $\hat N\ket{\nu}=\nu\ket{\nu}$ with $\braket{\nu}{\nu}=1$:

$$
\nu=\bra{\nu}\hat a^\dagger\hat a\ket{\nu}=\big\|\hat a\ket{\nu}\big\|^2\ge0
$$

**Step four: the ladder property.** Using $[\hat N,\hat a]=-\hat a$ and $[\hat N,\hat a^\dagger]=+\hat a^\dagger$ (both follow directly from $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$):

$$
\hat N\big(\hat a\ket{\nu}\big)=(\nu-1)\big(\hat a\ket{\nu}\big),\qquad
\hat N\big(\hat a^\dagger\ket{\nu}\big)=(\nu+1)\big(\hat a^\dagger\ket{\nu}\big)
$$

**Step five: the ladder must have a bottom rung.** Applying $\hat a$ repeatedly produces eigenvalues $\nu-1,\nu-2,\dots$, but step three says eigenvalues are non-negative. The only way out is for the sequence to terminate somewhere — there must exist a state $\ket{0}$ with

$$
\hat a\ket{0}=0
$$

From $\hat N\ket{0}=\hat a^\dagger\hat a\ket{0}=0$, the lowest eigenvalue is $\nu=0$. The ladder property then gives all the eigenvalues:

$$
\nu=n,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots
$$

(If some $\nu$ were not an integer, repeated lowering would step past 0 and produce a negative eigenvalue — a contradiction.)

**Step six: the energy levels.**

$$
E_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right)
$$

**Step seven: the normalised ladder.** From $\|\hat a^\dagger\ket{n}\|^2=\bra{n}\hat a\hat a^\dagger\ket{n}=\bra{n}(\hat N+1)\ket{n}=n+1$:

$$
\hat a^\dagger\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\,\ket{n+1},\qquad \hat a\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\,\ket{n-1}
$$

$$
\ket{n}=\frac{(\hat a^\dagger)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0}
$$

**Every result came from a single commutation relation.** No Hermite polynomial appeared anywhere, and no differential equation was ever solved.

**Only if you want the wavefunctions** do you return to the position basis: write $\hat a\ket{0}=0$ in $\braket{x}{\cdot}$ form and you get a first-order ordinary differential equation,

$$
\left(x+\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\frac{\dd}{\dd x}\right)\psi_0(x)=0
\ \Longrightarrow\
\psi_0\propto\ee^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}
$$

A first-order equation — far easier than the original second-order one.

</Derivation>

<Sim name="harmonic-oscillator" />

<Callout type="insight" title="Why this method matters so much">
This algebra is not just a trick for the harmonic oscillator. It is a **template**, reused over and over throughout physics:

- **Angular momentum** ([chapter 5](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/)): from $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\ii\hbar\hat L_z$ define $\hat L_\pm=\hat L_x\pm\ii\hat L_y$, and the same ladder argument delivers all the allowed values of $l$ and $m$ — including half-integer spin, which the wavefunction method cannot produce at all.
- **Quantum field theory** ([section 8.5](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/05-second-quantization/)): $\hat a^\dagger$ becomes the operator that creates a particle, and $\ket{n}$ becomes "there are $n$ particles". "The $n$-th excited state" and "$n$ particles" become one and the same sentence.
- **Coherent states**: the eigenstates $\hat a\ket{\alpha}=\alpha\ket{\alpha}$ are the quantum description of laser light.

**The algebraic method reaches far beyond where the differential-equation method can go.** This is the most tangible profit of chapter 3.
</Callout>

## The uncertainty principle: from derivation to substitution

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Chapter 2**

All it could say was "the rigorous proof needs the generalised uncertainty relation", plus an intuitive Fourier-analysis origin.

For the infinite-well ground state specifically, it still had to grind through two integrals: $\langle x^2\rangle$ by two rounds of integration by parts, and $\langle p^2\rangle$ smuggled in via $2mE_1$.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Chapter 3**

$$
\sigma_A\sigma_B\ge\frac12\big|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\big|
$$

Substitute $[\hat x,\hat p]=\ii\hbar$:

$$
\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

**One step.** And the inequality holds for any pair of observables, not just $x$ and $p$.

</div>
</DualView>

<Derivation title="The oscillator ground state: σₓ and σ_p without a single integral" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

Invert the ladder-operator definitions:

$$
\hat x=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger),\qquad
\hat p=\ii\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}(\hat a^\dagger-\hat a)
$$

**First moments.** On $\ket{0}$, $\hat a\ket{0}=0$ and $\braket{0}{1}=0$, so

$$
\langle x\rangle=\bra{0}\hat x\ket{0}\propto\bra{0}(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger)\ket{0}=0+\braket{0}{1}=0
$$

and likewise $\langle p\rangle=0$.

**Second moments.**

$$
\hat x^2=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\big(\hat a^2+\hat a\hat a^\dagger+\hat a^\dagger\hat a+(\hat a^\dagger)^2\big)
$$

Take the expectation value on $\ket{0}$: the $\hat a^2$ and $(\hat a^\dagger)^2$ terms send $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{2}$ or annihilate it — orthogonal to $\bra{0}$ either way, so they contribute nothing; $\hat a^\dagger\hat a\ket{0}=0$; all that survives is $\hat a\hat a^\dagger\ket{0}=\ket{0}$:

$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}
$$

and in the same way

$$
\langle p^2\rangle=\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}
$$

**Multiply them together**:

$$
\sigma_x\sigma_p=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}=\frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

**The bound is saturated exactly.** The oscillator ground state is a minimum-uncertainty state — [section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) claimed that Gaussian wavepackets achieve $\hbar/2$ exactly, and here is the proof, without doing a single integral.

**The zero-point energy falls out for free**:

$$
E_0=\frac{\langle p^2\rangle}{2m}+\frac12m\omega^2\langle x^2\rangle
=\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}+\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}
$$

And you can see that kinetic and potential energy each contribute exactly half — the virial theorem, as it appears for the oscillator.

</Derivation>

## The Ehrenfest theorem: one line

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Chapter 2's approach**

Differentiate $\langle x\rangle$ with respect to time, substitute the Schrödinger equation, integrate by parts twice, argue the boundary terms away… most of a page.

Then do it all again for $\langle p\rangle$.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**With the Heisenberg equation**

$$
\frac{\dd\langle A\rangle}{\dd t}=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\big\langle[\hat H,\hat A]\big\rangle
$$

Take $\hat A=\hat x$ and use $[\hat p^2,\hat x]=-2\ii\hbar\hat p$:

$$
\frac{\dd\langle x\rangle}{\dd t}=\frac{\langle p\rangle}{m}
$$

Take $\hat A=\hat p$ and use $[V(\hat x),\hat p]=\ii\hbar V'(\hat x)$:

$$
\frac{\dd\langle p\rangle}{\dd t}=-\big\langle V'(\hat x)\big\rangle
$$

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Note that the right-hand side is still ⟨V′(x̂)⟩, not V′(⟨x⟩)">
The new language shortens the derivation, but **it changes none of the physics**. The distinction that [section 2.6](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/06-expectation-and-uncertainty/) took pains over is still here:

$$
\big\langle V'(\hat x)\big\rangle\ne V'\big(\langle x\rangle\big)\quad\text{in general}
$$

Only when $V'$ is approximately linear across the width of the wavepacket are the two equal — and only then does the expectation value truly follow a classical trajectory.

**This is a general lesson worth remembering**: changing language can save you computation, but it cannot save you physics. If a result seems to "disappear" in the new language, you have most likely dropped it.
</Callout>

## End of chapter

The three questions chapter 2 left open now have answers:

**Why do observables correspond to operators?** Because demanding "measured values are real, distinct outcomes are distinguishable, every state can be expanded" picks out exactly the Hermitian operators ([section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/)).

**By what right is $|c_n|^2$ a probability?** That one remains a postulate (the Born rule). But the spectral theorem guarantees $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$, so it is at least **self-consistent** — and Gleason's theorem goes further: in dimension $\ge3$ there is essentially no other choice.

**What actually happens in a measurement?** The projection postulate describes its effect but **does not explain its mechanism** ([section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/)). On this there is still no consensus — and it is not the sort of problem that computing more carefully can resolve.

With this language in hand, the pace can pick up considerably. [Chapter 4](/en/ch/04-1d-problems/) returns to one-dimensional problems — but this time with structural methods like symmetry and the transfer matrix, and with a numerical solver that handles arbitrary potentials.
