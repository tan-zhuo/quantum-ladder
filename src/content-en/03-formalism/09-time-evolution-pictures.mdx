Measurement is the one non-unitary step in the theory. The rest of the time, states evolve by postulate 4.

This section packages that evolution into a single operator — and along the way discovers something very practical.

## The evolution operator

The Schrödinger equation

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat H\ket{\psi(t)}
$$

is first order, so given $\ket{\psi(0)}$ every later instant is uniquely determined. Write that map as an operator:

$$
\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}
$$

<Derivation title="Derivation: the form of U(t)" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

Substitute $\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}$ into the Schrödinger equation:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd \hat U}{\dd t}\ket{\psi(0)}=\hat H\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}
$$

This holds for any initial state, so

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd\hat U}{\dd t}=\hat H\hat U,\qquad \hat U(0)=\hat 1
$$

**If $\hat H$ carries no explicit time dependence**, this is the operator version of a scalar differential equation, with solution

$$
\boxed{\ \hat U(t)=\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\ }
$$

The exponential of an operator is defined by its power series:

$$
\ee^{\hat A}\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\hat A^n}{n!}
$$

**Check**: differentiate term by term, $\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}=-\frac{\ii\hat H}{\hbar}\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}$, and substitute. (This step uses the fact that $\hat H$ commutes with itself, so it can be differentiated like an ordinary number.)

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="How to actually use it: in the energy basis">
An exponentiated operator looks intimidating, but in the eigenbasis of $\hat H$ it is **diagonal**, and the calculation collapses to one line.

Let $\hat H\ket{n}=E_n\ket{n}$ and expand the initial state as $\ket{\psi(0)}=\sum_n c_n\ket{n}$. By the "eigenvalues of a function" property from [section 3.4](/en/ch/03-formalism/04-linear-operators/), $\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\ket{n}=\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}$, so

$$
\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_n c_n\,\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}\ket{n}
$$

**This is exactly the result of [section 2.4](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/04-time-dependent-schrodinger/).** Back then the recipe read "expand in stationary states, give each term its own phase factor" — now we see that this is just $\hat U$ written out in the energy basis.

**The standard three steps for time-dependent problems**: expand in energy eigenstates → multiply each coefficient by $\ee^{-\ii E_nt/\hbar}$ → convert back to the position basis when needed. Every evolving superposition in chapter 2 ran on this recipe.
</Callout>

## Unitarity = probability conservation

<Derivation title="Proof that U is unitary, and what that means" level="基础" minutes={4}>

$\hat H$ is Hermitian, so

$$
\hat U^\dagger=\big(\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\big)^\dagger=\ee^{+\ii\hat H^\dagger t/\hbar}=\ee^{+\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}=\hat U^{-1}
$$

(conjugation sends $\ii\to-\ii$, and $\hat H^\dagger=\hat H$). That is,

$$
\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat 1
$$

which is the definition of **unitary**.

**Physical consequence one: probability conservation.**

$$
\braket{\psi(t)}{\psi(t)}=\bra{\psi(0)}\hat U^\dagger\hat U\ket{\psi(0)}=\braket{\psi(0)}{\psi(0)}=1
$$

Normalised once, normalised forever. What [section 2.2](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/02-normalization-and-current/) proved via the continuity equation takes one line here.

**Physical consequence two: inner products are preserved.**

$$
\braket{\phi(t)}{\psi(t)}=\braket{\phi(0)}{\psi(0)}
$$

**Orthogonal states stay orthogonal forever.** Two distinguishable states never evolve into indistinguishable ones — a fact that matters greatly in quantum information (the no-cloning theorem and quantum error correction both rely on it).

**Physical consequence three: reversibility.** $\hat U(-t)=\hat U(t)^{-1}$ — the evolution can be run backwards. Information is never lost.

**Here lies the contrast with measurement**: measurement is a projection, irreversible, and information genuinely disappears. The entire tension of the measurement problem sits right here ([section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/)).

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="When H depends on time">
If $\hat H$ carries explicit time dependence, $\hat U(t)=\ee^{-\ii\int\hat H\dd t/\hbar}$ is generally **wrong** — because $\hat H(t_1)$ and $\hat H(t_2)$ at different times need not commute, and exponentials cannot be merged that way.

The correct form requires the time-ordering operator:

$$
\hat U(t)=\mathcal T\exp\left(-\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int_0^t\hat H(t')\,\dd t'\right)
$$

$\mathcal T$ orders the operators later-to-the-left. Expanded, this is the Dyson series — the starting point of scattering theory and time-dependent perturbation theory ([section 7.5](/en/ch/07-approximation/05-time-dependent-perturbation/)).

Only when $[\hat H(t_1),\hat H(t_2)]=0$ for all times may $\mathcal T$ be dropped.
</Callout>

## The three pictures

Now for the most useful part of the section.

<Callout type="check" title="Start with a question">
What we ultimately compute is always an expectation value:

$$
\langle A\rangle(t)=\bra{\psi(t)}\hat A\ket{\psi(t)}
=\bra{\psi(0)}\hat U^\dagger\hat A\hat U\ket{\psi(0)}
$$

You may bracket it like this: $\big(\bra{\psi(0)}\hat U^\dagger\big)\hat A\big(\hat U\ket{\psi(0)}\big)$ — the time sits on the states.

Or like this: $\bra{\psi(0)}\big(\hat U^\dagger\hat A\hat U\big)\ket{\psi(0)}$ — the time sits on the operator.

**The two bracketings are exactly equivalent.** Those are the two pictures.
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The Schrödinger picture (the default)**

$$
\ket{\psi_S(t)}=\hat U(t)\ket{\psi(0)},\qquad \hat A_S=\text{constant}
$$

States move, operators sit still. This is what chapter 2 used throughout.

**The Heisenberg picture**

$$
\ket{\psi_H}=\text{constant},\qquad \hat A_H(t)=\hat U^\dagger\hat A_S\hat U
$$

Operators move, states sit still.

$$
\langle A\rangle(t)\ \text{is the same in both pictures}
$$

because they are merely two bracketings of one and the same expression.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The Heisenberg equation of motion**

Differentiate $\hat A_H=\hat U^\dagger\hat A_S\hat U$ (with $\hat A_S$ carrying no explicit $t$):

$$
\frac{\dd\hat A_H}{\dd t}=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\hat U^\dagger(\hat H\hat A_S-\hat A_S\hat H)\hat U
$$

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{\dd\hat A_H}{\dd t}=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[\hat H,\hat A_H]\ }
$$

(add a $\partial\hat A/\partial t$ term when $\hat A_S$ depends explicitly on time).

**Compare with classical mechanics**:

$$
\frac{\dd A}{\dd t}=\{A,H\}_{\text{Poisson}}
$$

The forms are exactly parallel; just make $\{\ ,\ \}\to\frac{1}{\ii\hbar}[\ ,\ ]$. **This is the recipe of canonical quantisation.**

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Conservation laws at a glance">
The Heisenberg equation delivers an important conclusion instantly:

$$
[\hat H,\hat A]=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\dd\hat A_H}{\dd t}=0
$$

**Observables that commute with the Hamiltonian are conserved.**

- $[\hat H,\hat H]=0$ → energy conservation (when $\hat H$ has no explicit time dependence)
- translation invariance → $[\hat H,\hat p]=0$ → momentum conservation
- rotation invariance → $[\hat H,\hat L]=0$ → angular-momentum conservation

This is **the quantum version of Noether's theorem**, and it is even more direct than the classical one: a symmetry shows up as "some operator commutes with $\hat H$", and the conservation law is read straight off the commutator.

It also answers the question from [section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/): why do conserved quantities stay put after measurement? Because they commute with $\hat H$ — in the Heisenberg picture they simply do not change with time.
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The interaction picture (the third)**

When $\hat H=\hat H_0+\hat V$, with $\hat H_0$ solvable and $\hat V$ a small perturbation:

$$
\ket{\psi_I(t)}=\ee^{\ii\hat H_0t/\hbar}\ket{\psi_S(t)}
$$

$$
\hat A_I(t)=\ee^{\ii\hat H_0t/\hbar}\hat A_S\ee^{-\ii\hat H_0t/\hbar}
$$

**Absorb the "easy" evolution generated by $\hat H_0$ into the operators, so the states are driven by $\hat V$ alone**:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\ket{\psi_I}=\hat V_I(t)\ket{\psi_I}
$$

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Which one to use**

| Picture | States | Operators | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Schrödinger | move | still | wave-packet evolution, intuitive pictures |
| Heisenberg | still | move | conservation laws, field theory, classical comparison |
| Interaction | move slowly | move fast | perturbation theory, scattering, time-dependent problems |

All three coincide at $t=0$ and make identical physical predictions. **The choice is purely a matter of which computes most easily.**

The interaction picture is the starting point of time-dependent perturbation theory and Fermi's golden rule ([sections 7.5](/en/ch/07-approximation/05-time-dependent-perturbation/)–[7.6](/en/ch/07-approximation/06-fermi-golden-rule/)).

</div>
</DualView>

<Derivation title="Example: the free particle in the Heisenberg picture" level="进阶" minutes={5}>

$\hat H=\hat p^2/2m$. Momentum first:

$$
\frac{\dd\hat p_H}{\dd t}=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[\hat H,\hat p]=0
$$

($\hat p$ commutes with $\hat p^2$.) So $\hat p_H(t)=\hat p(0)$ is constant — momentum conservation.

Now position:

$$
\frac{\dd\hat x_H}{\dd t}=\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[\hat H,\hat x]=\frac{\ii}{2m\hbar}[\hat p^2,\hat x]
$$

Using $[\hat p^2,\hat x]=\hat p[\hat p,\hat x]+[\hat p,\hat x]\hat p=-2\ii\hbar\hat p$:

$$
\frac{\dd\hat x_H}{\dd t}=\frac{\hat p}{m}
$$

Integrate:

$$
\hat x_H(t)=\hat x(0)+\frac{\hat p(0)}{m}t
$$

**Identical to the classical $x=x_0+vt$** — except every quantity is an operator.

The nice part: wave-packet spreading now takes one line. Using $[\hat x(0),\hat p(0)]=\ii\hbar$, expand $\sigma_x^2(t)=\langle\hat x_H^2\rangle-\langle\hat x_H\rangle^2$:

$$
\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+\frac{\sigma_p^2}{m^2}t^2+(\text{cross term})
$$

For a Gaussian packet with $\langle\Delta x\Delta p+\Delta p\Delta x\rangle=0$ initially, the cross term vanishes; substitute $\sigma_p=\hbar/2\sigma_0$:

$$
\sigma_x(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}
$$

**This is exactly the spreading formula of [section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/)**, which was obtained there by completing the square inside a Gaussian integral — rather tedious algebra. The Heisenberg picture turns it into a few lines.

</Derivation>

## What comes next

So far we have assumed we always know which state the system is in.

But what if we don't? An atom flying out of an oven with a completely random spin direction, say; or a system entangled with its environment, which has no state vector of its own at all.

The next section introduces the tool for these situations — it is also the gateway to [chapter 10](/en/ch/10-open-systems/).
