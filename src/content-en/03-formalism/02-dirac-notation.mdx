The previous section established that the state is a vector and the wavefunction its components. This section equips that idea with notation that fits it like a glove.

## The ket: the state itself

A quantum state is written

$$
\ket{\psi}
$$

read "ket psi". **It stands for the state itself, carrying no basis information whatsoever** — like the arrow of the previous section, not the $(3,4,0)$.

<Callout type="check" title="A correspondence that is easy to muddle">
$\ket{\psi}$ is **not** the same thing as $\psi(x)$.

$\psi(x)$ is the component of $\ket{\psi}$ in the position basis, written

$$
\psi(x)=\braket{x}{\psi}
$$

Likewise $\phi(k)=\braket{k}{\psi}$ and $c_n=\braket{n}{\psi}$.

**One and the same $\ket{\psi}$: whichever set of components you want, "dot" it with the corresponding basis.**
</Callout>

## The bra: the half you use for inner products

Every ket $\ket{\psi}$ comes with a bra $\bra{\psi}$. Snap the two together and you get the inner product:

$$
\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\int\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,\dd x
$$

(The word "bra-ket" comes from "bracket" — Dirac split it down the middle.)

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Three properties of the inner product**

$$
\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\braket{\psi}{\phi}^*
$$

Swapping the order takes the complex conjugate — because $\int\phi^*\psi$ and $\int\psi^*\phi$ are conjugates of each other.

$$
\braket{\psi}{\psi}\ge0,\quad\text{and}\ =0\iff\ket{\psi}=0
$$

The squared length is non-negative. A normalised state satisfies $\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1$.

$$
\bra{\phi}\big(a\ket{\psi_1}+b\ket{\psi_2}\big)=a\braket{\phi}{\psi_1}+b\braket{\phi}{\psi_2}
$$

Linear in the right slot, **anti**linear in the left (coefficients pulled out of the bra pick up a conjugate).

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Chapter 2's formulas, rewritten**

Normalisation:

$$
\int|\Psi|^2\dd x=1
\ \longrightarrow\
\braket{\Psi}{\Psi}=1
$$

Expansion coefficients:

$$
c_n=\int\psi_n^*\Psi\,\dd x
\ \longrightarrow\
c_n=\braket{n}{\Psi}
$$

Orthonormality:

$$
\int\psi_m^*\psi_n\dd x=\delta_{mn}
\ \longrightarrow\
\braket{m}{n}=\delta_{mn}
$$

Expectation value:

$$
\int\Psi^*\hat A\Psi\,\dd x
\ \longrightarrow\
\bra{\Psi}\hat A\ket{\Psi}
$$

</div>
</DualView>

## An operator sandwiched in the middle

$\hat A\ket{\psi}$ is another ket. Take its inner product with $\bra{\phi}$ and you get a **matrix element**:

$$
\bra{\phi}\hat A\ket{\psi}
$$

The deliberate ambiguity of this notation is a feature: you may read it as "the inner product of $\bra{\phi}$ with $(\hat A\ket{\psi})$", or as "the inner product of $(\bra{\phi}\hat A)$ with $\ket{\psi}$". Both readings give the same number, so no parentheses are needed.

**That is where the name matrix element comes from**: in a basis $\{\ket{n}\}$, $A_{mn}=\bra{m}\hat A\ket{n}$ is precisely the matrix representation of the operator.

## The outer product: snapped together the other way round

Put the ket on the left and the bra on the right:

$$
\ket{\phi}\bra{\psi}
$$

This is not a number but an **operator** — acting on any ket, it returns a ket:

$$
\big(\ket{\phi}\bra{\psi}\big)\ket{\chi}=\ket{\phi}\underbrace{\braket{\psi}{\chi}}_{\text{a number}}
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="The projection operator">
The most important special case is the outer product of $\ket{\psi}$ with itself (with $\ket{\psi}$ normalised):

$$
\hat P_\psi=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
$$

Acting on an arbitrary $\ket{\chi}$:

$$
\hat P_\psi\ket{\chi}=\ket{\psi}\braket{\psi}{\chi}
$$

**It projects $\ket{\chi}$ onto the direction of $\ket{\psi}$**, with projection length $\braket{\psi}{\chi}$. Exactly like $(\hat n\cdot\vec v)\hat n$ in three-dimensional space.

Two properties you can verify at a glance:

$$
\hat P_\psi^2=\ket{\psi}\underbrace{\braket{\psi}{\psi}}_{=1}\bra{\psi}=\hat P_\psi
$$

**Projecting twice is the same as projecting once** (after the first projection you are already on the line, so the second does nothing). This property is called **idempotence**, and it is the algebraic definition of what "projecting" means.

$$
\hat P_\psi^\dagger=\hat P_\psi
$$

Projection operators are Hermitian — [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/) will spell out what that means. The measurement postulate is written precisely in terms of projection operators ([section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/)).
</Callout>

## The completeness relation: the most useful identity in the chapter

Let $\{\ket{n}\}$ be an orthonormal, complete basis. "Complete" means any state can be expanded:

$$
\ket{\psi}=\sum_n c_n\ket{n},\qquad c_n=\braket{n}{\psi}
$$

Substitute the $c_n$ back in:

$$
\ket{\psi}=\sum_n \ket{n}\braket{n}{\psi}=\left(\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}\right)\ket{\psi}
$$

This holds for every $\ket{\psi}$, so the operator in parentheses is the identity:

$$
\boxed{\ \sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=\hat 1\ }
$$

The continuous-basis version swaps the sum for an integral:

$$
\int\ket{x}\bra{x}\,\dd x=\hat 1,\qquad \int\ket{p}\bra{p}\,\dd p=\hat 1
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="How to use it: to change basis, insert a 1">
Because multiplying by $\hat 1$ changes nothing, you may insert a completeness relation anywhere you like. **This is the single most-used move in the whole notation.**

**Example 1: the inner product as an integral.**

$$
\braket{\phi}{\psi}
=\bra{\phi}\hat 1\ket{\psi}
=\int\braket{\phi}{x}\braket{x}{\psi}\dd x
=\int\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,\dd x
$$

Chapter 2's definition of the inner product is here **derived**, not decreed.

**Example 2: a matrix element as an integral.**

$$
\bra{\phi}\hat A\ket{\psi}=\int\!\!\int\braket{\phi}{x}\bra{x}\hat A\ket{x'}\braket{x'}{\psi}\,\dd x\,\dd x'
$$

For a local operator like $\hat A=\hat x$, $\bra{x}\hat x\ket{x'}=x\,\delta(x-x')$ and the double integral collapses to a single one.

**Example 3: what the Fourier transform really is.**

$$
\psi(x)=\braket{x}{\psi}=\int\braket{x}{p}\braket{p}{\psi}\dd p=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int \ee^{\ii px/\hbar}\,\phi(p)\,\dd p
$$

using $\braket{x}{p}=\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ (the components of a momentum eigenstate in the position basis — the plane wave).

**The Fourier transform is not some external mathematical tool bolted on; it is the change-of-basis matrix between the position and momentum bases.**
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Two notational traps">
**1. What sits inside $\ket{\psi}$ is a label, not a variable.** $\ket{n}$, $\ket{\uparrow}$, $\ket{0}$, $\ket{\text{cat alive}}$ are all legal — whatever goes inside the angle bracket merely names the state. A common beginner's mistake is to write $\ket{\psi(x)}$, which stuffs the components into the label.

**2. Coefficients leaving a bra pick up a conjugate.** If $\ket{\chi}=a\ket{\psi}$, then $\bra{\chi}=a^*\bra{\psi}$. Forget that conjugate and your probabilities come out complex.
</Callout>
