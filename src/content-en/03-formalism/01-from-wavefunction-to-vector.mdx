Chapter 2 ended with three questions left hanging: why do observables correspond to operators? What actually happens during a measurement? And what entitles $|c_n|^2$ to be a probability?

Answering them requires switching to a new language. This section explains **why the switch is unavoidable**.

## A clue we have already met

In [section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) we described one and the same free particle with two functions:

$$
\Psi(x)\quad\text{and}\quad \phi(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\Psi(x)\ee^{-\ii kx}\dd x
$$

The two carry **exactly the same** information: knowing either one lets you compute the other, and normalisation holds on both sides (Parseval's theorem).

<Callout type="check" title="Pause and think about what this means">
If $\Psi(x)$ and $\phi(k)$ hold the same thing, then **neither of them is that thing itself**.

They are more like two ways of writing down a single object. What is that object?
</Callout>

## A finite-dimensional analogy

Step back first to a situation you already know well.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**An arrow in three-dimensional space.** In Cartesian coordinates it reads $(3,4,0)$; rotate the axes by $53°$ about the $z$ axis and the same arrow becomes $(5,0,0)$.

The three numbers changed. **The arrow did not.** Neither did its length $|\vec v|=5$.

"$(3,4,0)$" is not the vector — it is **the vector's components in one particular basis**. The vector itself is the geometric object.

</div>
<div slot="right">

$$
\vec v=\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_i\,\hat e_i,\qquad v_i=\hat e_i\cdot\vec v
$$

Switch to a basis $\{\hat e_i'\}$ and the components become $v_i'=\hat e_i'\cdot\vec v$, but

$$
\vec v=\sum_i v_i\hat e_i=\sum_i v_i'\hat e_i'
$$

**Same $\vec v$, two sets of books.**

</div>
</DualView>

Now carry this picture over to quantum mechanics:

$$
\underbrace{\Psi(x)}_{\text{components in the position basis}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\phi(k)}_{\text{components in the momentum basis}}
\qquad
\underbrace{c_n}_{\text{components in the energy basis}}
$$

Three sets of numbers, one state.

<Callout type="insight" title="Read x as an index">
What makes the analogy really land is the following change of reading.

A vector's components are written $v_i$, with $i$ running over 1, 2, 3. A function's values are written $\Psi(x)$, with $x$ running over all real numbers.

**Read $x$ as a continuous index**, and $\Psi$ becomes a vector with "infinitely many components":

$$
v_i \longleftrightarrow \Psi(x)
$$

$$
\sum_i \longleftrightarrow \int\dd x
$$

$$
\vec u\cdot\vec v=\sum_i u_i^*v_i \longleftrightarrow \int\Phi^*(x)\Psi(x)\,\dd x
$$

Every one of those integrals from chapter 2 can now be read as an inner product. **The normalisation condition $\int|\Psi|^2\dd x=1$ says simply that the state vector has length 1.**
</Callout>

## The energy basis: components become a list of numbers

The position and momentum bases are both continuous — the index runs over the whole real line. But bound-state problems come with a more convenient basis.

In [section 2.7](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/07-infinite-well/) we expanded an arbitrary initial state in eigenstates:

$$
\Psi(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\psi_n(x),\qquad c_n=\int\psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x)\,\dd x
$$

Compare with the vector expansion $\vec v=\sum_i v_i\hat e_i$, $v_i=\hat e_i\cdot\vec v$ — **the form is identical**.

So in the energy basis, the components of the same state are a list of numbers $(c_1,c_2,c_3,\dots)$.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Three sets of components, one state**

| Basis | Components | Index |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position $\{\ket{x}\}$ | $\Psi(x)$ | continuous |
| Momentum $\{\ket{p}\}$ | $\phi(k)$ | continuous |
| Energy $\{\ket{n}\}$ | $c_n$ | discrete |

No set is more "real" than any other. Which one to pick depends only on **which makes the current problem easiest**: the energy basis for bound states, the momentum basis for free propagation, the position basis for plotting probability distributions.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Normalisation looks the same in all three sets of books**

$$
\int|\Psi(x)|^2\dd x=\int|\phi(k)|^2\dd k=\sum_n|c_n|^2=1
$$

That is no coincidence: all three are one and the same sentence — **the state vector has length 1** — written out in different bases.

A vector's length does not depend on the coordinate system, so the three equations must hold together.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="What the new language buys you">
This point of view is not for show. It delivers at least three concrete benefits:

1. **Formulas get shorter.** Proving that energy eigenvalues are real took [section 2.3](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/03-time-independent-schrodinger/) half a page of integrals and integration by parts. In the new language it reads "eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real" — three lines, as [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/) will show.
2. **It handles systems that have no wavefunction.** Electron spin has only two states; no $\psi(x)$ of any kind can describe it. But it has a two-dimensional state vector, and $2\times2$ matrices are all you need. [Chapter 5](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/) runs entirely on this.
3. **It exposes structure.** In wavefunction language, "observables correspond to operators" looks like a bare decree; in vector language it becomes "observables correspond to a special class of linear transformations" — and which class that is can be derived.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Where the analogy needs care">
Treating functions as vectors is safe most of the time, but two spots demand caution:

**1. Infinite-dimensional is not "finite-dimensional with a very large N".** Results like "at most $N$ linearly independent vectors" break down, and convergence becomes a genuine issue. What $\sum_n c_n\psi_n$ converges to, and in what sense, takes extra mathematics. [Section 3.3](/en/ch/03-formalism/03-hilbert-space/) fills this in.

**2. The "basis vectors" of a continuous basis are not legitimate states.** The "wavefunction" corresponding to $\ket{x}$ is $\delta(x-x_0)$, and $\int|\delta|^2$ diverges — it cannot be normalised. The same goes for $\ket{p}$ (which is exactly why the plane waves of [section 2.10](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) are unnormalisable).

They are useful bookkeeping devices, but they represent no physical state. This too is left for section 3.3.
</Callout>

## What comes next

If we are going to speak the language of "vectors, bases, components, inner products" for the long haul, we need matching notation — ideally something much shorter to write than $\int\Phi^*(x)\Psi(x)\dd x$.

The notation Dirac designed for the job is one of the most successful notational inventions in all of physics.
