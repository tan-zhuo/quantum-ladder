The previous two sections have leaned on "states are vectors" throughout, but a few things were stepped around:

- Does the infinite series $\ket{\psi}=\sum_n c_n\ket{n}$ converge? Converge to what?
- $\ket{x}$ cannot be normalised — what entitles it to serve as a basis?
- What exactly does "complete" mean?

This section fills those in. **Only the parts that affect physical conclusions** — for rigorous functional analysis, see a mathematics text.

## What the space of states must satisfy

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Condition one: it is a linear space.**

States can be added and multiplied by complex numbers, and the result is again a state. This comes straight from the linearity of the Schrödinger equation, and is the mathematical statement of the [superposition principle](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/04-time-dependent-schrodinger/).

**Condition two: it has an inner product.**

$\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ is defined and satisfies the three properties of the previous section. Without an inner product there is no normalisation, no orthogonality, no probability — the Born rule would have nowhere to live.

**Condition three: it is complete (in the analyst's sense).**

Every Cauchy sequence converges to an element of the space.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Definition**

A space satisfying all three is a **Hilbert space**, usually written $\mathcal H$.

**The first postulate of quantum mechanics** ([section 3.7](/en/ch/03-formalism/07-postulates/)) reads:

> The state of a physical system is described by a normalised vector in some Hilbert space.

$$
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal H,\qquad \braket{\psi}{\psi}=1
$$

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="The word 'complete' has two meanings — keep them apart">
The ambiguity exists in English just as it does in Chinese, and beginners step on it almost without fail:

**Complete in the analyst's sense (complete metric space)**: every Cauchy sequence has a limit, and the limit lies in the space. This is part of the definition of a Hilbert space, and it says **the space has no holes**.

**Complete as a basis (complete basis)**: $\{\ket{n}\}$ spans the whole space, every state can be expanded, i.e. $\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=\hat 1$. This says **the basis is big enough**.

The two are unrelated. This section is about the first; the completeness relation of the previous section is about the second.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Why the third condition matters: a concrete hole">
The first two conditions feel natural; the third looks like mathematical fussiness. It is not.

Consider the space of all continuous functions on the interval $[-1,1]$, with the inner product $\int f^*g\,\dd x$. Take a sequence of ever-steeper continuous functions:

$$
f_n(x)=\begin{cases}0,&-1\le x<0\\ nx,&0\le x\le 1/n\\ 1,&1/n<x\le 1\end{cases}
$$

In the distance induced by the inner product, $\{f_n\}$ is a Cauchy sequence — its members get ever closer to one another. But the limit is a step function, **discontinuous**, and not in the space.

**The space has a hole.** And in quantum mechanics we take limits constantly: infinite series expansions, continuum limits of wave packets, time evolution. If the space has holes, a perfectly legitimate limiting process may produce something that "is not a state".

$L^2$ plugs those holes, at the cost of containing many discontinuous — even nowhere-differentiable — functions. Physically that costs nothing: real wavefunctions are always smooth, and the extra elements simply guarantee that limits always have somewhere to land.
</Callout>

## L²: square-integrable functions

The state space for a single particle in one dimension is

$$
L^2(\mathbb R)=\left\{\psi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C\ \middle|\ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x)|^2\dd x<\infty\right\}
$$

equipped with the inner product $\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\int\phi^*\psi\,\dd x$.

<Callout type="check" title="Why 'square-integrable', exactly">
This condition was not picked at random — it is precisely what the Born rule demands.

For $|\psi|^2$ to serve as a probability density, $\int|\psi|^2\dd x$ must be a finite number (which you then divide out to normalise). If the integral diverges, no amount of rescaling produces a distribution with total probability 1.

**"Normalisable" and "square-integrable" are one and the same thing.** The space $L^2$ was singled out by the Born rule, not by mathematical accident.
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**What is in $L^2$, and what is not**

In: every bound state, every wave packet, every state that can physically be prepared.

Not in:
- plane waves $\ee^{\ii kx}$ ($\int 1\,\dd x=\infty$)
- $\delta(x-x_0)$ ($\int\delta^2$ diverges)
- constants, polynomials, anything that fails to decay

Note that $\psi\in L^2$ does **not** require $\psi(x)\to0$ pointwise, nor continuity — only that the integral converges. Physical wavefunctions are additionally required to be smooth enough, but that comes from the Schrödinger equation (which contains second derivatives), not from $L^2$.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Separability: why the basis can be countable**

$L^2$ is **separable**: it admits a **countable** orthonormal complete basis.

This property is enormously practical — it guarantees that the "list of numbers" expansion

$$
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\ket{n}
$$

always works, with no need for uncountably many coefficients.

The infinite well's $\{\psi_n\}$ and the harmonic oscillator's $\{\psi_n\}$ are both bases of this kind. **Any $L^2$ function can be expanded in harmonic-oscillator eigenstates**, even one that has nothing to do with the oscillator.

</div>
</DualView>

## The continuous spectrum: the "eigenstates" that live outside the space

Now for the question we have been dodging.

The solutions of the position operator's eigenvalue equation $\hat x\ket{x_0}=x_0\ket{x_0}$ are, in the position basis, $\delta(x-x_0)$. The momentum operator's eigenstates are plane waves. **Neither belongs to $L^2$.**

<Callout type="misconception" title="'Not in the space' does not mean 'useless'">
A common misconception: since $\ket{x}$ is not a legitimate state, using it must be wrong.

The right attitude: $\ket{x}$ and $\ket{p}$ are **generalised eigenstates** — extraordinarily useful **bookkeeping devices** that represent no preparable physical state.

Analogy: the $\delta$ function is not a function, yet under an integral sign it works beautifully. In the same way, $\ket{x}$ only means something when it gets "integrated away":

$$
\ket{\psi}=\int\psi(x)\ket{x}\,\dd x
$$

The left-hand side is a legitimate state; no individual term on the right is. There is no contradiction — just as a finite integral can be built by accumulating infinitely many infinitesimals.
</Callout>

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Normalisation becomes a δ function**

Discrete spectrum:

$$
\braket{m}{n}=\delta_{mn}
$$

Continuous spectrum:

$$
\braket{x}{x'}=\delta(x-x'),\qquad \braket{p}{p'}=\delta(p-p')
$$

This is called **δ normalisation**. Formally it parallels the discrete case (Kronecker δ replaced by Dirac δ): every formula carries over, with sums traded for integrals.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Mixed spectra**

Some Hamiltonians have both a discrete and a continuous spectrum — the finite well of [section 2.8](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/08-finite-well/) is one: finitely many bound states at negative energy, plus a continuum of scattering states at positive energy.

The completeness relation then reads

$$
\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}+\int\ket{E}\bra{E}\,\dd E=\hat 1
$$

Hydrogen works the same way: the discrete ladder $-13.6/n^2$, plus the ionised continuum at $E>0$.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="The rigorous version: rigged Hilbert space">
Mathematics has a clean framework for all this, the **rigged Hilbert space** (also known as a Gelfand triple):

$$
\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'
$$

$\Phi$ is a smaller space of "very well-behaved functions" (say, rapidly decreasing ones), and $\Phi'$ is its dual, which contains generalised objects like $\delta$ functions and plane waves. The generalised eigenstates live in $\Phi'$.

This framework makes every formal manipulation above rigorous. **You do not need it for physics** — the δ-normalisation rules work perfectly well — but it is reassuring to know the whole thing can be made rigorous. Von Neumann put quantum mechanics on a rigorous Hilbert-space and spectral-theory footing back in 1932; the rigged Hilbert space came later (Gelfand and others, 1950s–60s) and brought the δ-functions and plane waves of the physicist's formal manipulations into the rigorous fold as well.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The space is ready, and the states live inside it. The next question: **what operations can be performed on this space**?

The answer is linear operators. And among all linear operators, only a very small class earns the title of "observable" — chapter 2's unexplained decree begins, from the next section on, to become a derivable conclusion.
