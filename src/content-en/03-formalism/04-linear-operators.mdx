The previous section settled where the states live. This section asks: **what can be done on that space?**

## An operator = a map from states to states

A **linear operator** $\hat A$ sends each ket to another ket:

$$
\hat A\ket{\psi}=\ket{\phi}
$$

Linearity means

$$
\hat A\big(a\ket{\psi_1}+b\ket{\psi_2}\big)=a\hat A\ket{\psi_1}+b\hat A\ket{\psi_2}
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="Why only linear operators">
Because the Schrödinger equation is linear.

If $\ket{\psi_1}$ and $\ket{\psi_2}$ evolve into $\ket{\psi_1(t)}$ and $\ket{\psi_2(t)}$ respectively, the superposition $a\ket{\psi_1}+b\ket{\psi_2}$ must evolve into $a\ket{\psi_1(t)}+b\ket{\psi_2(t)}$. Only a linear operator can do that.

Introduce a nonlinear term and the superposition principle collapses — and the superposition principle is the entire source of double-slit interference and of quantum computing's parallelism. **Every experiment to date is consistent with strict linearity** — dedicated high-precision tests have been run (searching, for instance, for a logarithmic nonlinearity in the Schrödinger equation), and the bounds are extremely tight.
</Callout>

## In a basis, an operator is a matrix

Take an orthonormal complete basis $\{\ket{n}\}$ and define

$$
A_{mn}=\bra{m}\hat A\ket{n}
$$

Arrange these numbers into a matrix. It determines everything about $\hat A$:

<Derivation title="Derivation: how the matrix elements fix the operator's action" level="基础" minutes={4}>

Let $\ket{\psi}=\sum_n c_n\ket{n}$ and $\ket{\phi}=\hat A\ket{\psi}=\sum_m d_m\ket{m}$. To find $d_m$:

$$
d_m=\braket{m}{\phi}=\bra{m}\hat A\ket{\psi}
$$

Insert a completeness relation $\sum_n\ket{n}\bra{n}=\hat 1$ to the right of $\hat A$:

$$
d_m=\sum_n\bra{m}\hat A\ket{n}\braket{n}{\psi}=\sum_n A_{mn}c_n
$$

$$
\boxed{\ d_m=\sum_n A_{mn}c_n\ }
$$

**This is exactly a matrix multiplying a column vector.** An operator acting on a state is, in component language, matrix multiplication.

Likewise, a product of two operators corresponds to a product of matrices:

$$
(AB)_{mn}=\bra{m}\hat A\hat B\ket{n}=\sum_k A_{mk}B_{kn}
$$

(again by inserting a $\hat 1$).

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="The matrix depends on the basis; the operator does not">
Switch to another basis and the matrix of the same $\hat A$ looks completely different.

This is the arrow of [section 3.1](/en/ch/03-formalism/01-from-wavefunction-to-vector/) all over again: $\hat A$ is the geometric object, and the matrix is its bookkeeping in one particular set of coordinates.

Only **basis-independent quantities** carry physical meaning. The two most used are the **trace** $\operatorname{Tr}\hat A=\sum_n A_{nn}$ and the **eigenvalues** — the same in every basis. That is also why the next step is to find the eigenvalues.
</Callout>

## The eigenvalue problem

$$
\hat A\ket{a}=a\ket{a}
$$

$\ket{a}$ is called an **eigenvector**, and $a$ an **eigenvalue**.

The geometric meaning is intuitive: most vectors, when hit by $\hat A$, change both length and direction; **the eigenvectors are the special directions that only get rescaled, never turned**.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**How to solve it (finite dimensions)**

Write the equation as $(\hat A-a\hat 1)\ket{a}=0$. For a non-zero solution the matrix must be singular:

$$
\det(A-a I)=0
$$

This is a degree-$N$ polynomial in $a$ (the **characteristic equation**) with $N$ roots (counted with multiplicity). Substitute each root back and solve the homogeneous system for the eigenvectors.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Example: the x component of spin**

$$
\hat S_x=\frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Characteristic equation $a^2-(\hbar/2)^2=0$, eigenvalues $a=\pm\hbar/2$.

Substituting back gives the eigenvectors

$$
\ket{\pm_x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \pm1\end{pmatrix}
$$

In the $\ket{\pm_z}$ basis, these are precisely the states "spin up/down along x". [Chapter 5](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/) will use them, and these are the very directions the Bloch-sphere scene draws.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Chapter 2's equations were eigenvalue problems all along">
The time-independent Schrödinger equation

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\dd^2\psi}{\dd x^2}+V\psi=E\psi
$$

reads, in the new language,

$$
\hat H\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}
$$

**Energy levels = eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian; stationary states = its eigenvectors.**

What [section 2.3](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/03-time-independent-schrodinger/) phrased as "only special values of $E$ admit normalisable solutions" here reads "the eigenvalues are discrete". And the discreteness comes from restricting the operator to $L^2$ (the boundary conditions) — automatic for a finite matrix (an $N\times N$ matrix has at most $N$ eigenvalues), but something that must be proved in infinite dimensions.
</Callout>

## Degeneracy

If a single eigenvalue $a$ corresponds to several linearly independent eigenvectors, the eigenvalue is called **degenerate**, and the degeneracy is the dimension of the space those eigenvectors span.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Eigenspaces**

Suppose $\ket{a_1},\dots,\ket{a_g}$ all belong to the eigenvalue $a$. Any linear combination of them is again an eigenvector:

$$
\hat A\big(\textstyle\sum_i c_i\ket{a_i}\big)=a\big(\textstyle\sum_i c_i\ket{a_i}\big)
$$

So they span a $g$-dimensional subspace $\mathcal H_a$, called the **eigenspace**.

**Note**: with degeneracy, "the eigenvector belonging to $a$" is not unique — there is not even a natural way to choose one. You are free to pick any basis inside $\mathcal H_a$. That freedom becomes crucial in perturbation theory ([section 7.2](/en/ch/07-approximation/02-degenerate-perturbation/)).

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Degeneracy always travels with symmetry**

The $n=2$ level of hydrogen holds 4 states ($2s$ and three $2p$), all with the same energy.

Why? Because the Hamiltonian has symmetries:

- spherical symmetry → degeneracy in $m$ ($2p_x,2p_y,2p_z$ share one energy)
- an extra symmetry peculiar to the Coulomb potential → degeneracy in $l$ ($2s$ and $2p$ share one energy)

Among common central potentials, the second is special to the Coulomb potential (and the 3D isotropic harmonic oscillator); swap in a generic central potential (say the screened potential in a many-electron atom) and the $l$ degeneracy breaks — which is precisely where the structure of the periodic table comes from. See [section 6.4](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/04-energy-levels/).

**Break the symmetry and the degeneracy lifts**: a magnetic field breaks spherical symmetry, the $m$ degeneracy lifts, spectral lines split — the Zeeman effect.
</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="check" title="A small result worth verifying yourself">
If $\hat A\ket{a}=a\ket{a}$, then $\ket{a}$ is also an eigenvector of $\hat A^2$, $\hat A^3$ and $\ee^{\hat A}$, with eigenvalues $a^2$, $a^3$ and $\ee^{a}$.

The proof is one line: $\hat A^2\ket{a}=\hat A(a\ket{a})=a\hat A\ket{a}=a^2\ket{a}$.

**Corollary**: any function $f(\hat A)$ of $\hat A$ shares its eigenvectors, with eigenvalues $f(a)$. [Section 3.9](/en/ch/03-formalism/09-time-evolution-pictures/) uses this directly to construct $\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}$ — the time-evolution operator shares eigenstates with the Hamiltonian, which is why stationary states "hold still".
</Callout>

## What comes next

There are infinitely many kinds of linear operator, but an observable can only belong to a very small class — it must guarantee real measurement results, and distinct results must be distinguishable.

The next section pins down exactly which class that is — and, along the way, redoes in three lines the proof that took [section 2.3](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/03-time-independent-schrodinger/) the better part of a page.
