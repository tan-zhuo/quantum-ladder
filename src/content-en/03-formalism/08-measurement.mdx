The previous section laid the measurement postulate on the table. This section takes it apart.

## What the postulate says

Let the observable $\hat A$ have the spectral decomposition $\hat A=\sum_n a_n\hat P_n$, where $\hat P_n$ projects onto the eigenspace of the eigenvalue $a_n$.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Two things, happening at once**

**(1) Outcome and probability**

$$
P(a_n)=\bra{\psi}\hat P_n\ket{\psi}=\big\|\hat P_n\ket{\psi}\big\|^2
$$

In the non-degenerate case $\hat P_n=\ket{a_n}\bra{a_n}$, and this reduces to the familiar $P=|\braket{a_n}{\psi}|^2$.

**(2) The post-measurement state**

$$
\ket{\psi'}=\frac{\hat P_n\ket{\psi}}{\big\|\hat P_n\ket{\psi}\big\|}
$$

The denominator is just renormalisation.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The probabilities sum to 1 automatically**

$$
\sum_n P(a_n)=\bra{\psi}\Big(\sum_n\hat P_n\Big)\ket{\psi}=\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1
$$

using completeness, $\sum_n\hat P_n=\hat 1$.

**No coincidence**: it is a direct consequence of the spectral theorem of [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/) — probability normalisation comes free with "observables must be Hermitian".
</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="Repeatability: why the projection step is indispensable">
With only the Born rule and no projection step, the theory would clash with a very basic experimental fact.

**The fact**: repeat the same measurement immediately and you always get the same result. Find the electron in $[a,b]$, measure again at once, and it is still in $[a,b]$.

With projection this is automatic: after the first measurement the state is already $\ket{a_n}$, and

$$
P(a_n\ \text{again}) = |\braket{a_n}{a_n}|^2 = 1
$$

**That is the precise experimental meaning of "collapse"** — not some mysterious event, but the testable statement "a second measurement must return the same result".
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="But mind the word 'immediately'">
Only when $[\hat A,\hat H]=0$ does the post-measurement state remain an eigenstate of $\hat A$. Otherwise it evolves away under the Schrödinger equation, and a later measurement no longer guarantees the same result.

Example: the electron is found in $[a,b]$ (a position measurement), but $[\hat x,\hat H]\ne0$, so the wave packet starts spreading at once. A few multiples of $\tau=2m\sigma^2/\hbar$ later, it may well be elsewhere.

**Conserved quantities (observables commuting with $\hat H$) are the exception**: energy and total angular momentum, once measured, stay put.
</Callout>

## See it with your own hands

<Sim name="infinite-well" />

Switch the mode to "superposition" and make both $c_1$ and $c_2$ non-zero. By postulate 3, measuring the energy now:

- gives $E_1$ with probability $|c_1|^2$ and $E_2$ with probability $|c_2|^2$;
- once $E_1$ is obtained, the state instantly becomes pure $\psi_1$ — **the probability density stops sloshing**.

That is exactly what "dragging $c_2$ to 0" does in the simulation. The difference: you drag by hand, whereas the measurement happens at random — but once it happens, the outcome is the same.

<Callout type="check" title="A point worth thinking through">
Before the measurement, $\langle E\rangle=|c_1|^2E_1+|c_2|^2E_2$ — a number somewhere between $E_1$ and $E_2$.

But **no single measurement ever returns that number** — only $E_1$ or $E_2$.

$\langle E\rangle$ is the average over many repeated measurements, and in general it equals no eigenvalue at all. The misconception card at the start of [section 2.6](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/06-expectation-and-uncertainty/) — "the expectation value is not the most likely result" — was about exactly this.
</Callout>

## What the postulate does **not** say

This may be the most important part of the section.

<Callout type="misconception" title="Questions the projection postulate leaves unanswered">
**1. At which step does the collapse happen?**

The postulate only says "after the measurement" — without defining what counts as a measurement. When the particle hits the detector? When the current is amplified? When the data are written to disk? When somebody looks at the screen?

On this the postulate is **entirely silent**.

**2. How long does the collapse take?**

The postulate says "immediately". But real measurement processes take time. The theory gives no dynamics of collapse — because it is not a dynamical process at all; it is a substitution written into a postulate.

**3. Why this outcome and not that one?**

The postulate gives only probabilities. As for why this particular run gave $E_1$, the theory says: there is no reason.

This is **fundamental randomness**, not "we just don't know". Bell experiments ([section 9.4](/en/ch/09-quantum-information/04-bell-chsh/)) have ruled out, at extremely high confidence, local hidden-variable stories of the kind "the answer was there all along, we simply had not looked".
</Callout>

<Callout type="misconception" title="Three widely circulated wrong claims">
**"Consciousness causes collapse."** Wigner once took this seriously, and later abandoned it himself. It has no experimental support whatsoever, and it faces an awkward question: before conscious observers existed, did any collapse ever happen in the universe? Mainstream physics does not adopt this view.

**"Measurement disturbs because the detector gives the particle a knock."** Sometimes true, but not the essence. The quarter-wave-plate scheme of [section 1.6](/en/ch/01-history/06-double-slit/) exchanges no momentum, yet still wipes out the interference. What does the work is **the recording of information**, not the exchange of momentum.

**"Collapse is faster than light, so it violates relativity."** The collapse of an entangled state is indeed "nonlocal", but **it cannot be used to send signals**. Alice's outcomes look completely random to Bob; the statistics in his hands do not change no matter what Alice does. Only by comparing records over a classical channel can the correlations be seen — and classical channels are not faster than light. This is the **no-communication theorem**, worked out in [section 9.5](/en/ch/09-quantum-information/05-teleportation/).
</Callout>

## The precise location of the measurement problem

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Treat the apparatus as a quantum system too**

Let the measured system start in $\ket{\psi}=c_1\ket{a_1}+c_2\ket{a_2}$ and the apparatus in $\ket{M_0}$.

By postulate 4 (unitary evolution), a suitable interaction gives

$$
\ket{\psi}\ket{M_0}\longrightarrow c_1\ket{a_1}\ket{M_1}+c_2\ket{a_2}\ket{M_2}
$$

The apparatus has faithfully recorded the result — **but what it recorded is a superposition**.

The Schrödinger equation delivers "a pointer aiming at two readings at once". And nobody has ever seen such a thing.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Where the seam is**

$$
\underbrace{c_1\ket{a_1}\ket{M_1}+c_2\ket{a_2}\ket{M_2}}_{\text{what postulate 4 gives}}
$$

$$
\Big\downarrow\ ?
$$

$$
\underbrace{\ket{a_1}\ket{M_1}\ \text{or}\ \ket{a_2}\ket{M_2}}_{\text{what we see}}
$$

This step **cannot** come from postulate 4: unitary evolution is linear, it turns superpositions into superpositions, and it never singles out one term.

Schrödinger's cat is the same argument with the apparatus swapped for a cat.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="What decoherence achieves — and what it does not">
The apparatus is not isolated: it interacts ceaselessly with its environment (air molecules, thermal radiation, internal degrees of freedom). Write the environment in too:

$$
c_1\ket{a_1}\ket{M_1}\ket{E_1}+c_2\ket{a_2}\ket{M_2}\ket{E_2}
$$

For a macroscopic apparatus the two environment states become orthogonal almost instantly, $\braket{E_1}{E_2}\approx0$. Trace out the environment and the off-diagonal elements of the apparatus's reduced density matrix are exponentially suppressed:

$$
\rho_M\to\begin{pmatrix}|c_1|^2&\approx0\\ \approx0&|c_2|^2\end{pmatrix}
$$

The timescale is absurdly short: for a dust grain in air, about $10^{-31}$ seconds.

**What decoherence solves**: why macroscopic objects never show interference between superposed states; why "pointer position", and not some other basis, gets singled out (the **preferred-basis problem**); why collapse looks instantaneous.

**What decoherence does not solve**: the matrix above is a **mixed state**, read as "it is 1 or 2, we just don't know which". But mathematically it is still part of the full entangled state — the global state remains a superposition. **Why we experience only one outcome**, decoherence does not answer.

This is worth stating plainly, because "decoherence solved the measurement problem" is a widespread oversimplification. It shrank the problem from "why no macroscopic superpositions" to "why a single outcome" — enormous progress, but not the end. [Section 10.5](/en/ch/10-open-systems/05-decoherence/) carries out the full calculation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="What each interpretation gives up">
| Interpretation | What it gives up | The price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Copenhagen | asking about the collapse mechanism | "measurement" remains forever a primitive |
| Many-worlds | postulate 3 (a unique outcome) | all branches actually exist; the origin of probability needs a separate argument |
| Bohmian mechanics | locality | explicitly nonlocal, sitting awkwardly with relativity |
| GRW / spontaneous collapse | strictly unitary evolution | modifies the Schrödinger equation; falsifiable in principle |
| Relational / QBism | objectivity of the state | the state becomes "observer-relative" or "a belief" |

**Look at the last column.** These are not "matters of taste" — each pays a genuine price somewhere, and the GRW family actually makes predictions that differ from standard quantum mechanics, with experiments steadily tightening the bounds.

**All interpretations give identical predictions for every experiment so far**, so this is not a problem that more precise calculation can settle. See [section 13.7](/en/ch/13-frontier/07-foundations/).
</Callout>

## What comes next

Measurement is the one non-unitary step in the theory. The rest of the time, states evolve by postulate 4.

The next section packages that evolution into a single operator and introduces something very practical: **whether the time dependence sits on the states or on the operators is a free choice.**
