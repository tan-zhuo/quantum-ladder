By now the tools are all in place. This section does a job of tidying up: **compress the entire theory into the fewest possible assumptions.**

It is worth being clear about why this matters. At this point you hold a pile of facts: the Born rule, the Schrödinger equation, operators, eigenvalues, collapse... They do not all have the same standing — some are starting points that must simply be granted, others follow from those starting points. **Fail to draw that line, and you end up memorising derivable results as if they were mysterious decrees.**

## The five postulates

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate 1: states">
The state of an isolated physical system is completely described by a normalised vector $\ket{\psi}$ in some Hilbert space $\mathcal H$.

$$
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal H,\qquad \braket{\psi}{\psi}=1
$$

Two vectors differing by a global phase, $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ee^{\ii\alpha}\ket{\psi}$, describe **the same** physical state.

**The words "completely described" carry real content**: they assert that no other, finer description exists — no extra variables hiding underneath. This is not self-evident; it is an assumption that experiment can test, and [the Bell tests of section 9.4](/en/ch/09-quantum-information/04-bell-chsh/) test exactly this.

**Experimental evidence**: every manifestation of the superposition principle, from double-slit interference to quantum computing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate 2: observables">
Every measurable physical quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator $\hat A$ on $\mathcal H$. The possible results of a measurement are only the eigenvalues of $\hat A$.

$$
\hat A\ket{a_n}=a_n\ket{a_n},\qquad \hat A^\dagger=\hat A
$$

**Experimental evidence**: measured values of energy levels, angular momentum and spin projections are discrete, and always real. Atomic spectra are the most direct evidence.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate 3: measurement (Born rule + projection)">
With the system in $\ket{\psi}$, the probability that measuring $\hat A$ yields the eigenvalue $a_n$ is

$$
P(a_n)=|\braket{a_n}{\psi}|^2
$$

(In the degenerate case, $P(a_n)=\|\hat P_n\ket{\psi}\|^2$, with $\hat P_n$ the projector onto that eigenspace.)

Immediately after the measurement, the state becomes

$$
\ket{\psi}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{\hat P_n\ket{\psi}}{\|\hat P_n\ket{\psi}\|}
$$

**Experimental evidence**: the randomness of individual measurement outcomes, the convergence of frequencies to $|c_n|^2$ over many repetitions, and the fact that an immediately repeated measurement always returns the same result. [Section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/) is devoted to this postulate.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate 4: evolution">
When not being measured, the state evolves by the Schrödinger equation:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\ket{\psi(t)}=\hat H\ket{\psi(t)}
$$

where $\hat H$ is the system's Hamiltonian operator.

**Experimental evidence**: every time-dependent phenomenon — Rabi oscillations, spin precession, molecular vibrational spectra.
</Callout>

<Callout type="insight" title="Postulate 5: identical particles">
Under exchange of identical particles, a many-particle state is either completely symmetric (**bosons**) or completely antisymmetric (**fermions**):

$$
\hat P_{12}\ket{\psi}=\pm\ket{\psi}
$$

**Experimental evidence**: the Pauli exclusion principle and the periodic table, Bose-Einstein condensation, the statistics of blackbody radiation. [Chapter 8](/en/ch/08-identical-particles/) develops this.
</Callout>

## What is a corollary, not a postulate

This list matters just as much as the postulates themselves.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**All of these are derived:**

- **Eigenvalues are real** ← Hermiticity in postulate 2 ([section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/))
- **Eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal** ← same source
- **Eigenvectors form a complete basis** ← the spectral theorem
- **$\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$** ← completeness + normalisation
- **The uncertainty principle** ← postulates 1, 2 + commutation relations ([section 3.6](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/))
- **Expectation values in a stationary state are time-independent** ← postulate 4
- **Probability conservation / the continuity equation** ← postulate 4 + Hermiticity of $\hat H$
- **Ehrenfest's theorem** ← postulates 3, 4

</div>
<div slot="right">

**These are genuine assumptions:**

- States are vectors in a Hilbert space (**why complex numbers?**)
- Observables are Hermitian operators
- $|\braket{a_n}{\psi}|^2$ is a probability (**why the modulus squared?**)
- Evolution is generated by $\hat H$
- Identical particles come with only two exchange symmetries

The two "why"s in parentheses still have no generally accepted derivation from anything more fundamental. There are beautiful partial results — Gleason's theorem proves that in dimension $\ge3$, any probability assignment satisfying basic consistency requirements **must** take the Born-rule form — but it presupposes the Hilbert-space structure itself.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="Do postulates 3 and 4 contradict each other?">
This is quantum mechanics' most famous fault line, and it pays to see it clearly early.

| | Postulate 4 (evolution) | Postulate 3 (measurement) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linear? | yes | no (renormalisation required) |
| Unitary? | yes | no |
| Reversible? | yes | no |
| Deterministic? | yes (the state's evolution is fully determined) | no (outcomes are random) |
| Continuous? | yes | no (an instantaneous jump) |

**The two postulates have exactly opposite properties.** And the theory never says: when do you use which?

"Measurement" enters the postulates as an **undefined primitive**. A measuring apparatus is itself made of atoms and should obey postulate 4. So at which step does the collapse actually happen?

This is the **measurement problem**. It is not a technical difficulty that finer calculation can dissolve, but a seam in the structure of the theory. The disagreements among interpretations of quantum mechanics concentrate almost entirely here:

- **Copenhagen**: accept the seam, treat measurement as primitive, ask no further.
- **Many-worlds**: delete postulate 3 and keep only postulate 4, at the price of all branches actually existing.
- **Decoherence**: explains why collapse **appears** to happen ([section 10.5](/en/ch/10-open-systems/05-decoherence/)), but still needs an extra story for why we see only one outcome.
- **Bohmian mechanics / GRW**: modify the theory itself, each paying its own price.

[Section 3.8](/en/ch/03-formalism/08-measurement/) and [section 13.7](/en/ch/13-frontier/07-foundations/) discuss this in more detail. **What to remember here: this is not something you failed to understand — this is simply where the subject stands.**
</Callout>

<Callout type="check" title="A common puzzle: where did the wavefunction go?">
A frequent question at this point: how come the five postulates never mention the wavefunction?

Because the wavefunction is **derived**. By postulate 1 the state is $\ket{\psi}$; by postulate 2 position is an observable $\hat x$ with a set of (generalised) eigenstates $\{\ket{x}\}$; hence

$$
\psi(x)\equiv\braket{x}{\psi}
$$

**The wavefunction is just the state's components in the position basis** — nothing more. Chapter 2 started from it because it is concrete and can be drawn; but in the logical structure it is not a fundamental object.

Likewise, Schrödinger's partial differential equation is postulate 4 written out in the position basis:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\braket{x}{\psi}=\bra{x}\hat H\ket{\psi}
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x)\psi
$$
</Callout>
