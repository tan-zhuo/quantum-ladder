The solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, $\Psi(t)=\ee^{-\ii\hat Ht/\hbar}\Psi(0)$, takes one line to write and considerable trouble to compute: inside $\hat H=\hat T+\hat V$, the kinetic term $\hat T$ is a differential operator in the position representation. The observation behind the **split-operator method**:

- $\ee^{-\ii\hat V\Delta t/\hbar}$ is diagonal in the **position representation** — a pointwise phase multiplication, essentially free;
- $\ee^{-\ii\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}$ is diagonal in the **momentum representation** — also a pointwise phase;
- the change of basis between the two representations is exactly the Fourier transform ([A.2](/en/ch/appendix-a-math/02-fourier/)), and the FFT does it in $O(N\log N)$.

So each evolution step = multiply a phase → FFT → multiply a phase → inverse FFT. The animations in the main text — the [wave packet hitting a barrier](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/11-tunneling/), the [spreading free packet](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) — are all this algorithm running.

## The splitting formula and its error order

The trouble is that $\hat T$ and $\hat V$ **do not commute**, so
$\ee^{-\ii(\hat T+\hat V)\Delta t/\hbar}\ne\ee^{-\ii\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}\ee^{-\ii\hat V\Delta t/\hbar}$.
But for small $\Delta t$ they can be approximately pulled apart, and the symmetry of the split determines the accuracy:

<Derivation title="Trotter versus Strang: why the symmetric split earns an extra order" level="进阶" minutes={6}>

Write $\hat A=-\ii\hat T\Delta t/\hbar$ and $\hat B=-\ii\hat V\Delta t/\hbar$, both small quantities of order $O(\Delta t)$. The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula (when two exponentials merge into one, the commutator crashes the party):

$$
\ee^{\hat A}\ee^{\hat B}=\exp\left(\hat A+\hat B+\tfrac12[\hat A,\hat B]+\cdots\right)
$$

**Naive split (Trotter)**: $\ee^{\hat A}\ee^{\hat B}$ differs from the target $\ee^{\hat A+\hat B}$ by $\tfrac12[\hat A,\hat B]=O(\Delta t^2)$ — second-order error per step, but covering a fixed total time takes $t/\Delta t$ steps, so the global error is $O(\Delta t)$: a first-order method.

**Symmetric split (Strang)**: split the potential in half and sandwich the kinetic term,

$$
\ee^{\hat B/2}\,\ee^{\hat A}\,\ee^{\hat B/2}
=\exp\left(\hat A+\hat B+O(\Delta t^3)\right)
$$

The $O(\Delta t^2)$ commutator term is cancelled by the symmetry (left and right are mirror images, so even-order error terms flip sign and cancel), giving third-order error per step and **global error $O(\Delta t^2)$**: a second-order method. The extra cost is essentially zero — the two adjacent "half potential steps" of consecutive steps can even be merged into one.

</Derivation>

The complete flow of one evolution step (Strang splitting):

$$
\Psi\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\,\ee^{-\ii V\Delta t/2\hbar}\ }
\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{FFT}\ }
\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\,\ee^{-\ii\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}\ }
\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{FFT}^{-1}\ }
\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\,\ee^{-\ii V\Delta t/2\hbar}\ }\ \Psi'
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="Exact norm preservation, for free">
Each step performs only two kinds of operation: pointwise multiplication by unit-modulus phases, and the unitary FFT. So **no matter how large Δt is, total probability is conserved to machine precision** — the error appears only in the phases (energies), never as the embarrassingly visible disease of "leaking probability". By contrast, naive explicit difference schemes (e.g. a plain Euler step in time) are unstable: the norm grows exponentially.
</Callout>

## Complete code: a wave packet hits a square barrier

A packet with $E\approx V_0$ strikes a barrier and roughly half passes, half bounces back (natural units $\hbar=m=1$):

```python
import numpy as np

# ---- Grid ----
L, N = 40.0, 1024
x = np.linspace(-L/2, L/2, N, endpoint=False)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
k = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=dx)     # momentum grid in FFT convention

# ---- Potential: a square barrier in the middle ----
V0, a = 2.0, 1.0
V = np.where(np.abs(x) < a / 2, V0, 0.0)

# ---- Initial state: Gaussian packet incident from the left (central momentum k0, kinetic energy k0²/2 = 2 = V0) ----
x0, k0, sigma = -10.0, 2.0, 1.5
psi = np.exp(-(x - x0)**2 / (4 * sigma**2) + 1j * k0 * x)
psi /= np.sqrt(np.sum(np.abs(psi)**2) * dx)   # normalise

# ---- Precompute the evolution factors (Strang splitting: V/2 → T → V/2) ----
dt, steps = 0.005, 2000
expV_half = np.exp(-0.5j * V * dt)
expT      = np.exp(-0.5j * k**2 * dt)

for _ in range(steps):
    psi = expV_half * psi                        # half step of potential
    psi = np.fft.ifft(expT * np.fft.fft(psi))    # full kinetic step (momentum space)
    psi = expV_half * psi                        # half step of potential

# ---- Checks: norm conservation + transmission/reflection probabilities ----
norm = np.sum(np.abs(psi)**2) * dx
T = np.sum(np.abs(psi[x >  a/2])**2) * dx
R = np.sum(np.abs(psi[x < -a/2])**2) * dx
print(f"after {steps} steps  norm = {norm:.12f}")
print(f"transmission T = {T:.4f}   reflection R = {R:.4f}   T + R = {T+R:.4f}")
```

Measured output:

```text
after 2000 steps  norm = 1.000000000000
transmission T = 0.5119   reflection R = 0.4865   T + R = 0.9984
```

The key lines:

- `np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=dx)`: gives the wavenumber corresponding to each array index in FFT convention (the first half is positive $k$, the second half negative $k$); multiplied by $2\pi$ it is the momentum grid. **Hand-rolling the $k$ array yourself is this algorithm's number-one source of bugs** — get the ordering wrong and the packet shatters instantly.
- `endpoint=False`: the FFT implies periodic boundaries; $x=-L/2$ and $x=+L/2$ are the same point and must not be counted twice.
- The evolution factors are **precomputed** outside the loop: each step is then only pointwise multiplications and two FFTs.
- The norm is exact to 12 digits — norm preservation is structural, not dependent on $\Delta t$ being small.
- $T+R=0.9984$ is not an error: the missing 0.0016 is the probability still lingering inside the barrier region $\lvert x\rvert<a/2$.
- Plot `np.abs(psi)**2` every few steps along the way, and you have the tunnelling animation from the main text.

## Convergence-order check

Evolve a coherent state in the oscillator potential, using a tiny-step solution as reference, and watch the error against $\Delta t$:

```python
import numpy as np

L, N = 40.0, 1024
x = np.linspace(-L/2, L/2, N, endpoint=False)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
k = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=dx)
V = 0.5 * x**2                        # oscillator potential

psi0 = np.exp(-(x - 3.0)**2 / 2)      # coherent state: sloshes back and forth without changing shape
psi0 = psi0 / np.sqrt(np.sum(np.abs(psi0)**2) * dx)

def evolve(psi, dt, steps):
    expV_half = np.exp(-0.5j * V * dt)
    expT = np.exp(-0.5j * k**2 * dt)
    for _ in range(steps):
        psi = expV_half * np.fft.ifft(expT * np.fft.fft(expV_half * psi))
    return psi

T_total = 2.0
ref = evolve(psi0.copy(), T_total / 6400, 6400)   # tiny step as reference solution

print("   dt        error ‖ψ - ψ_ref‖    ratio")
prev = None
for steps in [50, 100, 200, 400]:
    psi = evolve(psi0.copy(), T_total / steps, steps)
    err = np.sqrt(np.sum(np.abs(psi - ref)**2) * dx)
    ratio = f"{prev/err:.2f}" if prev else "  --"
    print(f" {T_total/steps:.4f}     {err:.3e}      {ratio}")
    prev = err
```

Measured output:

```text
   dt        error ‖ψ - ψ_ref‖    ratio
 0.0400     1.162e-03        --
 0.0200     2.905e-04      4.00
 0.0100     7.257e-05      4.00
 0.0050     1.809e-05      4.01
```

Halve the step, the error drops precisely to a quarter — the $O(\Delta t^2)$ global error of Strang splitting, in perfect agreement with the derivation.

<Callout type="warning" title="Three traps — all descendants of 'the FFT implies periodic boundaries'">
1. **The packet exits on the right and sneaks back in on the left.** The box is a ring. Run too long and the transmitted wave wraps around to contaminate the reflected one. Remedies: a bigger box, stopping the clock in time, or an **absorbing layer** at both ends — a stretch of imaginary potential $-\ii\Gamma(x)$ (it deliberately breaks norm preservation, "eating" the probability that reaches the edge, mimicking a particle gone for good).
2. **Momentum has a ceiling too.** The largest momentum the grid can represent is $k_{\max}=\pi/dx$. When the packet's momentum approaches it, aliasing sets in — high-momentum components masquerade as backwards low-momentum ones, and inexplicable ripples appear. Remedy: ensure $\lvert k_0\rvert+3/\sigma\ll\pi/dx$, or refine the grid.
3. **Spurious ringing at potential jumps.** The sharp edges of a square barrier contain arbitrarily high Fourier components; truncated by the grid, they leave small oscillations near the edges. The physical conclusions ($T$, $R$) are usually unaffected, but smooth the barrier edges if you want to plot wavefunction details.
</Callout>
