Last section's autopsy left exactly one way out: for positive-definite probability, the equation must be first order in time; for relativistic covariance, it must then be first order in space too. But in the dispersion relation $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$, both $E$ and $p$ appear squared — a first-order equation amounts to **taking the square root** of this sum of squares. In 1928, the 25-year-old Dirac decided to take the root by brute force.

## Taking the square root: an algebra problem

First state the goal precisely. We want an equation of the form

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\hat H\psi,\qquad
\hat H=c\,\vec\alpha\cdot\hat{\vec p}+\beta\,mc^2
=c\sum_{k=1}^3\alpha_k\hat p_k+\beta\,mc^2
$$

First order in time (the left side copied from Schrödinger), first order in space ($\hat p=-\ii\hbar\nabla$ appears only to the first power). $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3,\beta$ are four undetermined "coefficients" — do not presume yet that they are numbers. There is only one constraint, but it is iron: $\hat H$ squared must return the relativistic relation, $\hat H^2=c^2\hat p^2+m^2c^4$, or the solutions will not obey the correct energy-momentum relation.

<Derivation title="Square once, and the whole algebra is forced out" level="基础" minutes={8} open={true}>

**Step 1: expand $\hat H^2$ honestly.** Keep the order of $\alpha,\beta$ intact (we do not yet know whether they commute):

$$
\hat H^2=c^2\sum_{j,k}\alpha_j\alpha_k\,\hat p_j\hat p_k
+mc^3\sum_k(\alpha_k\beta+\beta\alpha_k)\,\hat p_k
+\beta^2m^2c^4
$$

In the first term $\hat p_j\hat p_k=\hat p_k\hat p_j$ (momentum components commute with each other), so only the symmetric combination acts, and it can be written $\frac12(\alpha_j\alpha_k+\alpha_k\alpha_j)\hat p_j\hat p_k$.

**Step 2: compare with the target term by term.** Demanding $\hat H^2=c^2(\hat p_1^2+\hat p_2^2+\hat p_3^2)+m^2c^4$:

- The cross terms in $\hat p_j\hat p_k$ ($j\neq k$) must vanish, and the squared terms must have coefficient 1:

$$
\alpha_j\alpha_k+\alpha_k\alpha_j=2\delta_{jk}
$$

- The terms linear in $\hat p_k$ must vanish, and the constant term must have coefficient 1:

$$
\alpha_k\beta+\beta\alpha_k=0,\qquad \beta^2=1
$$

**Step 3: read off the conclusion — they cannot be numbers.** The condition $\alpha_1\alpha_2=-\alpha_2\alpha_1$ is called **anticommutation**: swapping the order flips the sign. Two non-zero ordinary numbers always commute under multiplication and can never anticommute. Dirac's flash of insight: **let them be matrices** — matrix multiplication does not commute anyway — and $\psi$ correspondingly becomes a multi-component column vector.

**Step 4: how big must the matrices be?** Three quick reconnaissance results:

1. **Candidates lie ready to hand**: the Pauli matrices $\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$ ([section 5.3](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/03-spin-half/)) satisfy exactly $\sigma_j\sigma_k+\sigma_k\sigma_j=2\delta_{jk}$ — but there are only **three** of them, and we need **four** mutually anticommuting matrices. No fourth can be scraped together in 2×2 (any 2×2 matrix expands in $1,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$, and the identity commutes with everything, so no anticommuting fourth member exists).
2. **Zero trace**: from $\alpha_k=-\beta\alpha_k\beta$ (the anticommutation relation rewritten using $\beta^2=1$), taking the trace gives $\mathrm{tr}\,\alpha_k=-\mathrm{tr}\,\alpha_k=0$; likewise $\mathrm{tr}\,\beta=0$.
3. **Eigenvalues ±1**: $\alpha_k^2=\beta^2=1$ means the eigenvalues can only be $\pm1$; zero trace then demands equal numbers of $+1$ and $-1$ — the dimension must be **even**.

Two dimensions insufficient, odd dimensions out — the next candidate is **4×4**. Four dimensions indeed suffice, and the standard choice (the Dirac representation) is written in 2×2 blocks:

$$
\alpha_k=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_k\\ \sigma_k&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\beta=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\ 0&-I\end{pmatrix}
$$

(Verify the three sets of relations using $\sigma_j\sigma_k+\sigma_k\sigma_j=2\delta_{jk}$ — a two-minute exercise.)

</Derivation>

So $\psi$ is a **four-component** column vector, called a Dirac **spinor** — and the "four" is no accident: we shall see shortly that it = two spin components × two energy branches. Writing the momentum operator back as a derivative gives the **Dirac equation**:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
=\bigl(-\ii\hbar c\,\vec\alpha\cdot\nabla+\beta mc^2\bigr)\psi
$$

It immediately makes good on its promise: being first order in time, one can define $\rho=\psi^\dagger\psi=\sum_{a=1}^4|\psi_a|^2\geq0$ — a **positive-definite probability density**, paired with the current $\vec j=c\,\psi^\dagger\vec\alpha\,\psi$ satisfying the continuity equation. The KG knot is untied.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**The covariant form.** Multiply the equation through by $\beta$, reorganise the time and space terms, and define $\gamma^0=\beta$, $\gamma^k=\beta\alpha_k$; the equation becomes one line:

$$
\ii\hbar\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi=mc\,\psi
$$

Time and space derivatives stand side by side on completely equal footing — the first genuine fulfilment of the "spacetime equality" demanded at the start of this chapter. The γ matrices in block form:

$$
\gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&-I\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\gamma^k=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma_k\\-\sigma_k&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

</div>
<div slot="right">

**The whole algebra condensed into one line.** The three sets of anticommutation relations merge into

$$
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu+\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu=2\eta^{\mu\nu}
$$

where $\eta^{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ is the Minkowski metric: the γ matrices are "the square root of the spacetime metric", and the Dirac equation is a square root taken of spacetime geometry itself. (This structure is called a Clifford algebra, invented by mathematicians half a century before Dirac — it waited in the storeroom for its physics.)

</div>
</DualView>

## The climax: spin emerges on its own

So far the $\sigma_k$ have been mere building bricks — nowhere have we **assumed** that the electron has spin. Now run a routine check: is the angular momentum of a free particle conserved?

<Derivation title="Orbital angular momentum is not conserved — unless spin is added" level="进阶" minutes={8}>

**Step 1: check the orbital angular momentum.** Take $\hat L_z=\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x$ and commute it with $\hat H=c\vec\alpha\cdot\hat{\vec p}+\beta mc^2$ (the $\beta mc^2$ term involves neither position nor momentum):

$$
[\hat L_z,\hat H]=c\,\alpha_j[\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x,\hat p_j]
=c\bigl(\alpha_x[\hat x,\hat p_x]\hat p_y-\alpha_y[\hat y,\hat p_y]\hat p_x\bigr)
=\ii\hbar c\,(\alpha_x\hat p_y-\alpha_y\hat p_x)
$$

**Not zero.** In Schrödinger theory the free particle's $\hat L_z$ is ironclad conserved; in Dirac theory it is not — angular momentum of an isolated system is leaking. Where to?

**Step 2: recover the missing piece.** Define, using block Pauli matrices,

$$
\Sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_z&0\\0&\sigma_z\end{pmatrix}
$$

Using $\sigma_x\sigma_y=\ii\sigma_z$ and its cousins, compute block by block (exercise: only 2×2 block multiplication is needed):

$$
[\Sigma_z,\hat H]=c\,[\Sigma_z,\vec\alpha\cdot\hat{\vec p}\,]
=-2\ii c\,(\alpha_x\hat p_y-\alpha_y\hat p_x)
$$

Differing from Step 1's result only by a factor $-2/\hbar$.

**Step 3: combine.** Define $\hat J_z=\hat L_z+\frac{\hbar}{2}\Sigma_z$; then

$$
[\hat J_z,\hat H]=\ii\hbar c(\alpha_x\hat p_y-\alpha_y\hat p_x)
+\frac{\hbar}{2}\cdot(-2\ii c)(\alpha_x\hat p_y-\alpha_y\hat p_x)=0
$$

**What is conserved is $\hat{\vec J}=\hat{\vec L}+\frac\hbar2\vec\Sigma$.** The added piece $\frac\hbar2\vec\Sigma$ is an intrinsic angular momentum: each component has eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$, and the sum of squares is $\frac34\hbar^2=s(s+1)\hbar^2$ with $s=\frac12$. This is precisely the spin 1/2 that chapter 5 **rammed** into the theory at the demand of the Stern-Gerlach experiment — and here it walks out of the equation's algebra by itself.

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="The ancestry of spin, hereby established">
Recall chapter 5: spin was forced into the theory with the experiments holding its head down — the twin spots of Stern-Gerlach, the anomalous Zeeman effect — driving Pauli to add two components to the wavefunction with no explanation whatsoever: "that's just how it is."

The Dirac equation supplies the explanation: **demand "relativistic covariance + first order in time + positive-definite probability", and the minimal algebraic structure that solves it automatically carries an intrinsic angular momentum of ħ/2.** Spin is not a spinning little ball, not an extra postulate, but the inevitable product of the compatibility conditions between relativity and quantum mechanics. A mathematical structure invented purely to "take a square root" handed over the most baffling experimental fact of the preceding decade — one of theoretical physics' rare shining moments. (An honest footnote: a similar linearisation of the Schrödinger equation also yields spin, so strictly speaking it is not "exclusively relativistic"; but its first appearance as a **consequence** is here. Section 12.5 has an even harder gift: $g=2$.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't celebrate yet: the negative-energy solutions remain">
Insert plane waves into the Dirac equation and each momentum still yields $E=\pm\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}$ — the four components are exactly the four combinations "spin up/down × positive/negative energy". Taking the square root disposed of negative probability, but not of negative energy. And now the problem is more dangerous: since levels lower than $+mc^2$ exist (all the way down to $-\infty$), why doesn't the electron in an atom radiate photons and tumble down forever? By what right does a hydrogen atom survive longer than a nanosecond? Dirac was cornered by his own equation — next section, watch him turn the tables.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The Dirac equation won the probability but took on a heavier debt: the spectrum has no floor. Every electron should, by rights, radiate its way down toward $E=-\infty$, and the universe should contain no stable atoms. Dirac's escape was outrageous — redefine the "vacuum" as a **sea filled with negative-energy electrons**. This seemingly absurd picture will predict the first antimatter in human history.
