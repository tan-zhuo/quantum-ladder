In chapter 5 the experiments held our heads down and made us accept two things: the electron has spin $\hbar/2$; and its magnetic moment carries a strange factor $g\approx2$ — for the same angular momentum, spin produces **twice** the magnetic moment that orbital motion does ([section 5.4](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/04-stern-gerlach/)). In chapter 6 we then stuffed three "relativistic patches" into the hydrogen atom by hand to piece together the fine structure ([section 6.6](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/06-fine-structure/)). Each time the numbers came out right; each time we could not say **why**.

This section settles all the accounts at once: every one of those "hand additions" is an automatic consequence of the Dirac equation. There is only one method — put the equation in an electromagnetic field and expand order by order in powers of $v/c$.

## The zeroth-order surprise: g = 2

The electromagnetic field is plugged in exactly as in classical mechanics and Schrödinger theory (minimal coupling): $\hat{\vec p}\to\hat{\vec\pi}=\hat{\vec p}-q\vec A$, plus the scalar-potential energy $q\varphi$:

$$
\ii\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}
=\bigl[c\,\vec\alpha\cdot\hat{\vec\pi}+\beta mc^2+q\varphi\bigr]\psi
$$

Note carefully: **no** magnetic moment has been put in — the coupling says not one word about spin.

<Derivation title="From the Dirac equation to the Pauli equation: the birth of g=2" level="进阶" minutes={12} open={true}>

**Step 1: split into upper and lower halves.** Following the block structure of section 12.3, write the four-component spinor as two two-component spinors $\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\chi\\ \eta\end{pmatrix}$, and peel off the dominant rest-energy phase: $\psi\to\ee^{-\ii mc^2t/\hbar}\psi$. Substitute into the equation, use the block forms of $\alpha_k,\beta$, and obtain the coupled pair

$$
\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\chi=c\,\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi}\,\eta+q\varphi\,\chi
$$

$$
\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\eta=c\,\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi}\,\chi+q\varphi\,\eta-2mc^2\eta
$$

**Step 2: identify the large and small components.** In the second equation, $-2mc^2\eta$ dwarfs everything else: at low speeds both $\ii\hbar\partial_t\eta$ and $q\varphi\eta$ (an energy of order $\sim mv^2$ times $\eta$) are far smaller than it, so

$$
\eta\approx\frac{\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi}}{2mc}\,\chi
\;\sim\;\frac{v}{2c}\,\chi
$$

$\eta$ is smaller than $\chi$ by a factor of $v/c$ — call $\chi$ the **large component** (the Pauli two-component wavefunction of the non-relativistic electron) and $\eta$ the **small component**.

**Step 3: substitute back.** Insert $\eta$ into the first equation:

$$
\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\chi
=\left[\frac{(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi})^2}{2m}+q\varphi\right]\chi
$$

**Step 4: the single most important piece of algebra in the whole story.** Use the Pauli-matrix identity $(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec a)(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec b)=\vec a\cdot\vec b+\ii\vec\sigma\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b)$:

$$
(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi})^2=\hat\pi^2+\ii\,\vec\sigma\cdot(\hat{\vec\pi}\times\hat{\vec\pi})
$$

An ordinary vector crossed with itself is zero — but the components of $\hat{\vec\pi}$ do not commute with one another ($\hat{\vec p}$ does not commute with $\vec A(\vec x)$)! Compute component by component:

$$
(\hat{\vec\pi}\times\hat{\vec\pi})_z=\hat\pi_x\hat\pi_y-\hat\pi_y\hat\pi_x
=-q\bigl([\hat p_x,A_y]+[A_x,\hat p_y]\bigr)
=\ii\hbar q\Bigl(\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}\Bigr)
=\ii\hbar q\,B_z
$$

Assembling the three components: $\hat{\vec\pi}\times\hat{\vec\pi}=\ii\hbar q\vec B$. Substituting back:

$$
(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi})^2=\hat\pi^2-q\hbar\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B
$$

**Step 5: read off the Pauli equation and the g factor.**

$$
\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\chi
=\left[\frac{\hat\pi^2}{2m}+q\varphi
-\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B\right]\chi
$$

The last term is a magnetic-moment energy $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$, where (using $\hat{\vec S}=\frac\hbar2\vec\sigma$)

$$
\vec\mu=\frac{q\hbar}{2m}\vec\sigma=\underbrace{2}_{g}\cdot\frac{q}{2m}\hat{\vec S}
$$

Compare the orbital moment $\vec\mu_L=\frac{q}{2m}\hat{\vec L}$ (coefficient $\frac{q}{2m}$, no factor of 2): **the g factor of the spin magnetic moment comes out exactly 2.** At no step was a magnetic moment put into the theory — it grew by itself out of the algebra of $(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat{\vec\pi})^2$, and its value matches the Stern-Gerlach and anomalous-Zeeman measurements. Before 1928, $g=2$ was a purely empirical number; after 1928, it is a homework derivation.

</Derivation>

## First order: the fine-structure trio arrives complete

Push the approximation one order further (keep the $1/c^2$ corrections in the equation for $\eta$; the systematic version of this expansion is called the Foldy-Wouthuysen transformation — tedious algebra, no new ideas), and for the hydrogen atom ($q\varphi=V(r)$ the Coulomb potential) the effective Hamiltonian gains three extra terms:

$$
\hat H_{1/c^2}
=\underbrace{-\frac{\hat p^4}{8m^3c^2}}_{\text{relativistic kinetic correction}}
+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{\dd V}{\dd r}\,\hat{\vec S}\cdot\hat{\vec L}}_{\text{spin-orbit coupling}}
+\underbrace{\frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V}_{\text{Darwin term}}
$$

Check the books term by term — every one is an old acquaintance from section 6.6:

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Where they came from back then (section 6.6):**

1. Kinetic correction: expand $\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}$ to the next order and add it in **by hand**.
2. Spin-orbit coupling: in the electron's frame the nucleus circles the electron and produces a magnetic field acting on the spin moment — but the coefficient computed this way came out **twice too large**, and had to be patched by hand with the never-quite-explained "Thomas precession factor of 1/2" before it matched experiment.
3. Darwin term: back then no semiclassical origin could be given at all; one simply conceded "it has to be added anyway", with a hand-waving story that the "trembling" of the electron's position (Zitterbewegung) smears out the potential it feels.

Three patches, three pedigrees, each fending for itself.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Where they come from now (this section):**

All three terms emerge together from **one and the same** expansion of one and the same Dirac equation —

1. Kinetic correction: from the expansion of the energy square root, with the coefficient automatically right.
2. Spin-orbit term: **the coefficient is born carrying Thomas's 1/2**, because the expansion contains both effects at once — the magnetic interaction and the frame precession.
3. Darwin term: from the non-locality of the large-small component coupling; it acts only where $\nabla^2V\neq0$ — for the Coulomb potential, at the origin — so it touches only $s$ states, in seamless agreement with the spectroscopic data.

The merged levels depend only on $n$ and the total angular momentum $j$:

$$
E_{nj}=-\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}
\left[1+\frac{\alpha^2}{n^2}\Bigl(\frac{n}{j+1/2}-\frac34\Bigr)\right]
$$

Identical, term for term, with the formula pieced together in section 6.6.

</div>

<Callout type="insight" title="This chapter's thesis, in one sentence">
**Spin is not an accessory of quantum mechanics; it is a gift from relativity.**

The spin of chapter 5, the $g=2$, the fine-structure trio of chapter 6, even chapter 8's "fermions obey the exclusion principle" — all these "postulates", carried one by one from experiment into the theory, become **theorems** under the joint demands of "quantum mechanics + special relativity + a stable vacuum". The last of them (the spin-statistics theorem: half-integer spin must mean fermions, integer spin must mean bosons) was proved by Pauli in 1940 within relativistic quantum field theory — and its root lies precisely in relativity's causal structure (measurements at spacelike separation cannot influence one another). In non-relativistic quantum mechanics it can only be a postulate; the moment relativity enters, it is the only self-consistent choice. The stability of the material world — atoms that do not collapse, a periodic table that exists, white dwarfs that hold up against gravity — hangs, in the end, on Lorentz symmetry.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="g is not exactly 2 — and that is good news">
Experiment measures the electron's g factor to be

$$
g=2.002\,319\,304\,360\ldots
$$

After Dirac's 2 comes a trailing string of digits, a relative deviation of about one part in a thousand. This is not the Dirac equation getting its sums wrong — it is the equation's limit of validity showing through: the electron is forever immersed in the quantum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field, ceaselessly emitting and reabsorbing virtual photons, and its magnetic moment is lightly dressed as a result. That is already an effect of the **field**, beyond the reach of any single-particle equation. In 1948 Schwinger computed the first-order correction with quantum electrodynamics,

$$
\frac{g-2}{2}=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\approx0.001\,161\ldots
$$

in agreement with experiment; today theory and experiment agree to **parts in a trillion** — the most precisely tested prediction in the history of physics. That little tail on $g-2$ is quantum field theory's first calling card, slipped under the door.
</Callout>
