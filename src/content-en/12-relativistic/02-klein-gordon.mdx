Last section's diagnosis was written plainly: the disease is $E=p^2/2m$. The prescription seems ready-made — chapter 2's translation rules for building the Schrödinger equation have not expired:

$$
E\;\to\;\ii\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad
\vec p\;\to\;-\ii\hbar\nabla
$$

Just plug them into the **genuine** relativistic relation $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$, and done? Schrödinger himself tried exactly this route first, in 1926 (before he even published his non-relativistic equation), and Klein and Gordon published it formally the same year. In this section we walk the route ourselves and see where it succeeds and where it stumbles — and the way it stumbles is highly instructive.

## Constructing the equation

<Derivation title="From dispersion relation to the Klein–Gordon equation" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

**Step 1: direct translation.** Substitute the translation rules into $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$ and let both sides act on a wavefunction $\phi(x,t)$:

$$
-\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2}
=-\hbar^2c^2\nabla^2\phi+m^2c^4\phi
$$

**Step 2: tidy into standard form.** Divide by $-\hbar^2c^2$ and rearrange:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2}-\nabla^2\phi
+\Bigl(\frac{mc}{\hbar}\Bigr)^2\phi=0\;}
$$

This is the **Klein–Gordon equation**. Identify the parts: the first two terms together are the wave-equation operator (the d'Alembertian $\Box$) — second derivatives in both time and space, paired in the correct $1/c^2$ ratio: **Lorentz covariant, box ticked**. The coefficient of the third term is the inverse square of the Compton wavelength we just met, $1/\lambda_C^2$: the mass's sole role in the equation is to endow the wave equation with an intrinsic length scale.

**Step 3: two limiting self-checks.**
- $m=0$: it degenerates to the wave equation $\Box\phi=0$, whose solutions travel at the speed of light — the equation a photon should satisfy.
- Insert the plane wave $\phi=\ee^{\ii(px-Et)/\hbar}$ and the equation demands

$$
E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4\;\Longrightarrow\;E=\pm\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}
$$

The dispersion relation returns exactly to our starting point — but note the $\pm$: **every momentum comes paired with a negative-energy solution**. File that away; this section is trying a different case first.

</Derivation>

## The crime scene: where did the probability go?

A candidate "quantum-mechanical equation" must hand over two things: a probability density $\rho$ and a probability current $\vec j$ satisfying the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\vec j=0$ (probability neither appears from nowhere nor vanishes — recall [section 2.2](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/02-normalization-and-current/)). The Schrödinger equation hands over $\rho=|\Psi|^2\geq0$, qualified by birth. What about KG?

<Derivation title="Deriving the KG conserved current — and the autopsy" level="进阶" minutes={9}>

**Step 1: the standard trick for a conservation law.** Exactly the move of section 2.2: multiply the equation by $\phi^*$ and subtract $\phi$ times the conjugate equation. The KG equation and its complex conjugate are

$$
\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\phi-\nabla^2\phi+\frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\phi=0,\qquad
\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\phi^*-\nabla^2\phi^*+\frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\phi^*=0
$$

$\phi^*\times$(first) $-\;\phi\times$(second) — the mass terms cancel:

$$
\frac1{c^2}\bigl(\phi^*\partial_t^2\phi-\phi\,\partial_t^2\phi^*\bigr)
-\bigl(\phi^*\nabla^2\phi-\phi\nabla^2\phi^*\bigr)=0
$$

Both brackets can be written as total derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Bigl[\frac1{c^2}\bigl(\phi^*\partial_t\phi-\phi\,\partial_t\phi^*\bigr)\Bigr]
-\nabla\cdot\bigl(\phi^*\nabla\phi-\phi\nabla\phi^*\bigr)=0
$$

**Step 2: normalise the coefficient and recognise the current.** Multiply by $\frac{\ii\hbar}{2m}$ so the spatial part matches the Schrödinger probability current:

$$
\rho=\frac{\ii\hbar}{2mc^2}\Bigl(\phi^*\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}-\phi\frac{\partial\phi^*}{\partial t}\Bigr),
\qquad
\vec j=\frac{\hbar}{2m\ii}\bigl(\phi^*\nabla\phi-\phi\nabla\phi^*\bigr)
$$

$\vec j$ is identical to Schrödinger's probability current — familiar and reassuring. But $\rho$ has changed: it is no longer $|\phi|^2$ but a combination involving the **time derivative**.

**Step 3: the autopsy.** Insert the plane wave $\phi=N\ee^{\ii(\vec p\cdot\vec x-Et)/\hbar}$ (so $\partial_t\phi=-\ii E\phi/\hbar$):

$$
\rho=\frac{\ii\hbar}{2mc^2}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{2\ii E}{\hbar}\Bigr)|N|^2
=\frac{E}{mc^2}\,|N|^2
$$

Positive-energy solutions ($E>0$): $\rho>0$, all is well, and at low speed $E\to mc^2$, $\rho\to|N|^2$, joining smoothly onto Schrödinger — good. Negative-energy solutions ($E<0$): $\rho<0$. **The probability density is negative.**

And as we just established, the negative-energy solutions make up fully half of the solution space and cannot be thrown away (discard half the solutions and the remaining family is incomplete — you cannot even assemble an arbitrary initial wave packet).

</Derivation>

<Callout type="insight" title="To the root: negative probability is the inevitable child of 'second order in time'">
This is not a flaw in the derivation technique; it is structural necessity. Seeing this clearly matters most of all:

The Schrödinger equation is **first** order in time: given $\Psi(x,0)$ the future is uniquely determined, and $\rho=|\Psi|^2$ contains no time derivative — it could not go negative if it tried.

The KG equation is **second** order in time — like Newton's equation, it needs **two** pieces of initial data: $\phi(x,0)$ **and** $\partial_t\phi(x,0)$, independent of each other. The conserved density $\rho$ is precisely a combination of these two, and a combination of two independent quantities can of course take either sign.

Now the knot is visible: **relativity forces you to choose the same derivative order for time and space.** Choose second order (KG) — a positive-definite probability density cannot be salvaged. That leaves only one untried road: **first order in both time and space**. This is exactly Dirac's entrance in the next section.
</Callout>

## Rehabilitation: not wrong, just misused

The KG equation was later fully rehabilitated — the problem was never the equation, but the reading "$\phi$ is a single-particle probability amplitude".

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Change spectacles.** Multiply $\rho$ by the charge $q$ and stop calling it a probability density: call it the **charge density**. A charge density is supposed to take either sign — positive particles contribute positively, **antiparticles** negatively. The scandal of "negative probability", under its new name, becomes a prediction: the negative-energy solutions of the KG equation are holding a seat for a particle of opposite charge. The equation was telling the truth all along; we were just listening in the wrong language.

In quantum field theory, $\phi$ is recast as a **field** (one degree of freedom at each spacetime point, whose quantised excitations are the particles), and the KG equation is the field equation for spin-0 particles. The pion (discovered 1947) and the Higgs boson (discovered 2012) satisfy exactly this "failed" equation of 1926.

</div>
<div slot="right">

**What is conserved is charge, not particle number.**

$$
Q=q\int\rho\,\dd^3x=\text{const}
$$

A typical relativistic process: a photon creates a particle-antiparticle pair,

$$
\gamma\;\to\;\pi^+ +\pi^-
$$

Particle number $1\to2$ (not conserved); charge $0\to(+1)+(-1)=0$ (conserved). The KG conservation law keeps precisely this ledger.

A clean corroborating clue: a **real** $\phi$ ($\phi^*=\phi$) inserted into the expression for $\rho$ gives identically zero — a real field describing an electrically neutral particle (such as $\pi^0$ or the Higgs) has no such density to speak of, and the "probability interpretation" never applied to it in the first place.

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="warning" title="But within this chapter, the problem remains open">
"Read it as a charge density" only becomes truly self-consistent in quantum field theory — that requires abandoning the fixed-particle-number framework, a matter for chapter 13 and beyond. On the home turf of **single-particle quantum mechanics**, we still need an equation with a positive-definite probability density, or we cannot even ask "where is the electron?" The KG autopsy report points the way: second order in time was the cause of death, so build an equation first order in time, first order in space, that still satisfies the relativistic dispersion relation. It sounds like an impossible assignment — Dirac pulled it off with a beautiful piece of algebra.
</Callout>

## What comes next

Cause of death established: the second time derivative killed positive-definite probability. Dirac's strategy strikes at the root — build an equation with **only first derivatives in both time and space**, which upon squaring returns to $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$. With ordinary numbers this cannot be done; Dirac discovered that with **matrices** it can. And the algebraic structure introduced purely to "take a square root" will hand over, free of charge, the most mysterious thing in quantum mechanics — spin.
