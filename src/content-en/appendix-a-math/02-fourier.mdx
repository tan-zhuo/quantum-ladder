The physical content of the Fourier transform in one sentence: **decompose a function into a superposition of waves of different frequencies**. In quantum mechanics, "frequency" is momentum ($p=\hbar k$), so the Fourier transform is precisely the **change-of-basis formula between the position and momentum representations**.

**Where the main text needs this:**
[2.10 The free particle](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/10-free-particle/) uses it to build wave packets;
[3.3 Hilbert space](/en/ch/03-formalism/03-hilbert-space/) interprets it as an expansion in a continuous basis;
[7.6 Fermi's golden rule](/en/ch/07-approximation/06-fermi-golden-rule/) and
the [Chapter 11 path integral](/en/ch/11-path-integral/02-propagator/) lean heavily on Gaussian integrals;
appendix [C.2 The split-operator method](/en/ch/appendix-c-numerics/02-split-operator/) uses the FFT to hop back and forth between the two representations.

## Transform conventions

The same "Fourier transform" differs between books by a constant or a sign in the exponent, so let us nail down this book's convention first.

**Mathematical convention** (symmetric version, $k$ is the wavenumber):

$$
\tilde f(k)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\ee^{-\ii kx}\,\dd x,
\qquad
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \tilde f(k)\,\ee^{\ii kx}\,\dd k
$$

**Quantum-mechanical convention** ($p=\hbar k$, momentum wavefunction written $\phi(p)$):

$$
\phi(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\,\ee^{-\ii px/\hbar}\,\dd x,
\qquad
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(p)\,\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}\,\dd p
$$

The prefactor $1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ is there so that both sides are normalised simultaneously (see Parseval below). In Dirac notation this is nothing but inserting a complete set of momentum eigenstates: $\phi(p)=\braket{p}{\psi}$, with $\braket{x}{p}=\ee^{\ii px/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Three traps when consulting other books">
1. Sign convention: some books use $\ee^{+\ii kx}$ for the forward transform. Physics is almost unanimous on the version above, whose payoff is that the plane wave $\ee^{\ii(kx-\omega t)}$ travels towards $+x$.
2. Constant placement: engineering texts often dump the whole $2\pi$ onto the inverse transform (no factor on the forward one). Change the convention and the constants in the transform-pair table change with it — check conventions before copying results.
3. Frequency variable: signal processing uses $\nu$ (in Hz), with exponent $\ee^{-2\pi\ii\nu t}$ and no prefactor at all.
</Callout>

## The δ function

The **Dirac δ function** is not an ordinary function but a "value picker": whenever it appears inside an integral, it pulls out the value of the integrand at the position of the spike:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\delta(x-x_0)\,\dd x=f(x_0)
$$

Picture it as a limiting spike whose width goes to zero while its area stays 1. Its most important representation is a **superposition of plane waves**:

$$
\delta(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ee^{\ii kx}\,\dd k
$$

— plane waves of every wavenumber added with equal weight, cancelling everywhere and piling up in phase only at $x=0$. This formula is the origin of the continuous-basis orthonormality relations $\braket{x}{x'}=\delta(x-x')$ and $\braket{p}{p'}=\delta(p-p')$.

Frequently used properties:

| Property | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Scaling | $\delta(ax)=\delta(x)/\lvert a\rvert$ |
| Composite argument | $\delta(g(x))=\sum_i \delta(x-x_i)/\lvert g'(x_i)\rvert$, where the $x_i$ are the simple roots of $g$ |
| Derivative | $\int f(x)\,\delta'(x-x_0)\,\dd x=-f'(x_0)$ |
| Step function | $\dd\Theta/\dd x=\delta(x)$ |

## Table of standard transform pairs

Listed in the mathematical (symmetric) convention. To switch to the ħ convention, set $k\to p/\hbar$ and multiply the result by $1/\sqrt{\hbar}$.

| $f(x)$ | $\tilde f(k)$ | Remarks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ee^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$ | $\sigma\,\ee^{-\sigma^2k^2/2}$ | Gaussian stays Gaussian; widths are reciprocal, $\sigma_x\sigma_k=1$ |
| $\ee^{ik_0x}$ | $\sqrt{2\pi}\,\delta(k-k_0)$ | Plane wave = a single momentum |
| $\delta(x-x_0)$ | $\ee^{-\ii kx_0}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ | A position eigenstate contains every momentum |
| Square pulse (equal to 1 for $\lvert x\rvert<a$) | $\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\dfrac{\sin ka}{k}$ | sinc-shaped; the mathematical prototype of single-slit diffraction |
| $\ee^{-a\lvert x\rvert}$ | $\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\dfrac{a}{a^2+k^2}$ | Lorentzian; exponential decay ⇔ power-law tails |
| $f(x-x_0)$ | $\ee^{-\ii kx_0}\tilde f(k)$ | Translation ⇔ attaching a phase |
| $f'(x)$ | $\ii k\tilde f(k)$ | Differentiation ⇔ multiplying by $\ii k$; the algorithmic basis of C.2 |

The first row deserves a second look: the narrower a Gaussian wave packet (small $\sigma$), the wider its momentum distribution — this is the [uncertainty principle](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/06-expectation-and-uncertainty/) showing up at the Fourier level, and the Gaussian saturates the bound exactly, $\sigma_x\sigma_p=\hbar/2$.

## Parseval's theorem and the convolution theorem

**Parseval's (Plancherel's) theorem**: the "total amount" is unchanged by the transform,

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert f(x)\rvert^2\,\dd x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert\tilde f(k)\rvert^2\,\dd k
$$

Physical translation: if $\psi(x)$ is normalised, then $\phi(p)$ is automatically normalised too — total probability is 1 in the position representation and still 1 after switching to momentum. That is why $\lvert\phi(p)\rvert^2$ can be directly interpreted as the probability density for momentum.

**Convolution theorem**: the convolution of two functions,
$(f*g)(x)=\int f(y)\,g(x-y)\,\dd y$ (shift $g$ everywhere and stack the copies weighted by $f$),
becomes a product after transforming:

$$
\widetilde{f*g}(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\tilde f(k)\,\tilde g(k)
$$

The use: convolutions are hard, products are easy, so "transform — multiply — transform back" is the standard play. Numerically this is exactly how FFT convolution works, and it is what the [C.2 split-operator method](/en/ch/appendix-c-numerics/02-split-operator/) does at every step.

## Gaussian integrals

The most frequently occurring family of integrals in quantum mechanics. The basic formula ($\operatorname{Re}a>0$):

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ee^{-ax^2}\,\dd x=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}
$$

<Derivation title="Gaussian integral with a linear term: completing the square" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

The target:

$$
I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ee^{-ax^2+bx}\,\dd x
$$

**Complete the square.** Write the exponent as a perfect square:

$$
-ax^2+bx=-a\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+\frac{b^2}{4a}
$$

**Shift the integration variable** $u=x-b/2a$ (the limits are unchanged) and pull out the constant factor:

$$
I=\ee^{b^2/4a}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\ee^{-au^2}\,\dd u
=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\;\ee^{b^2/4a}
$$

This one formula handles most situations: $b$ may be complex (for instance $b=\ii k$ — this is exactly where "the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian" comes from), and $a$ may carry an imaginary part (in the path integral $a\propto\ii$, i.e. the Fresnel integral; the result has the same form, understood as convergent in the limiting sense).

**Versions with polynomial factors** are generated by "differentiating with respect to the parameter":

$$
\int x^2\,\ee^{-ax^2}\dd x=-\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\int\ee^{-ax^2}\dd x
=\frac{1}{2a}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}
$$

Odd powers give $\int x^{2n+1}\ee^{-ax^2}\dd x=0$ (an odd function integrated over a symmetric interval vanishes).

</Derivation>
