"Special functions" is an intimidating name for something mundane: they are just **the solutions of a few common differential equations, computed once and for all by our predecessors, numbered and tabulated**. After separating variables in the Schrödinger equation for the standard potentials, the leftover ordinary differential equations are the same handful over and over — and each corresponds to one family of special functions. This section is organised family by family: which equation it solves, how it is defined, its recurrence relations, what the first few look like, and which chapter of the main text uses it.

First, a master index:

| Family | Physical setting | Appears in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hermite $H_n$ | Harmonic-oscillator eigenstates | [2.9](/en/ch/02-wavefunction/09-harmonic-oscillator/) |
| Legendre $P_\ell$ and spherical harmonics $Y_\ell^m$ | Angular momentum; the angular part of any central potential | [5.1](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/01-orbital-angular-momentum/), [5.2](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/02-spherical-harmonics/) |
| Laguerre $L_n^k$ | Hydrogen radial wavefunctions | [6.3](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/03-hydrogen-solution/) |
| Spherical Bessel $j_\ell,n_\ell$ | Free-particle radial solutions, 3D scattering | [6.1](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/01-central-force/), [6.2](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/02-radial-equation/) |

## Hermite polynomials

**The problem they solve**: the stationary equation of the harmonic oscillator. Substituting the dimensionless variable $\xi=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$ and peeling off the Gaussian decay factor $\psi=h(\xi)\,\ee^{-\xi^2/2}$, $h$ satisfies the **Hermite equation**

$$
h''-2\xi h'+2n h=0
$$

Only when $n$ is a non-negative integer is the solution a polynomial (otherwise the series never terminates and $\psi$ diverges) — this is exactly where the quantisation of the oscillator levels $E_n=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ comes from.

**Definition** (Rodrigues formula — one line generates the whole family):

$$
H_n(\xi)=(-1)^n\,\ee^{\xi^2}\frac{\dd^n}{\dd\xi^n}\ee^{-\xi^2}
$$

**Recurrence and derivative** (numerics and hand calculation both rely on these two lines — no need to memorise explicit forms):

$$
H_{n+1}=2\xi H_n-2nH_{n-1},\qquad H_n'=2nH_{n-1}
$$

**The first few**:

| $n$ | $H_n(\xi)$ |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $1$ |
| 1 | $2\xi$ |
| 2 | $4\xi^2-2$ |
| 3 | $8\xi^3-12\xi$ |
| 4 | $16\xi^4-48\xi^2+12$ |

**Orthogonality** (with a Gaussian weight function):

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_m H_n\,\ee^{-\xi^2}\dd\xi=2^n n!\sqrt{\pi}\,\delta_{mn}
$$

From this follow the normalised oscillator eigenstates
$\psi_n(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n n!}}H_n(\xi)\,\ee^{-\xi^2/2}$.
$H_n$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ with parity $(-1)^n$ and $n$ real zeros — correspondingly $\psi_n$ has $n$ nodes.

## Legendre polynomials and spherical harmonics

**The problem they solve**: the **angular equation** obtained after separating variables in any central potential $V(r)$ — it does not involve $V$, so the angular solutions are universal. The $\theta$ equation (setting $u=\cos\theta$) is the **Legendre equation**

$$
\frac{\dd}{\dd u}\!\left[(1-u^2)\frac{\dd P}{\dd u}\right]+\ell(\ell+1)P=0
$$

Requiring the solution to stay finite at the poles $u=\pm1$ forces $\ell=0,1,2,\dots$ — the origin of angular-momentum quantisation.

**Definition and recurrence**:

$$
P_\ell(u)=\frac{1}{2^\ell \ell!}\frac{\dd^\ell}{\dd u^\ell}(u^2-1)^\ell,
\qquad
(\ell+1)P_{\ell+1}=(2\ell+1)\,u\,P_\ell-\ell P_{\ell-1}
$$

**The first few**: $P_0=1$, $P_1=u$, $P_2=\tfrac12(3u^2-1)$, $P_3=\tfrac12(5u^3-3u)$.

Adding the index $m$ gives the **associated Legendre functions** $P_\ell^m(u)=(1-u^2)^{|m|/2}\,\dd^{|m|}P_\ell/\dd u^{|m|}$; pairing these with the $\varphi$-direction factor $\ee^{\ii m\varphi}$ and a normalisation constant yields the **spherical harmonics** — the common eigenfunctions of the angular-momentum operators $\hat L^2,\hat L_z$:

$$
\hat L^2Y_\ell^m=\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2\,Y_\ell^m,
\qquad
\hat L_zY_\ell^m=m\hbar\,Y_\ell^m,
\qquad m=-\ell,\dots,\ell
$$

**The most frequently used ones** ([section 5.2](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/02-spherical-harmonics/) has 3D pictures):

| $Y_\ell^m$ | Expression |
| --- | --- |
| $Y_0^0$ | $\sqrt{1/4\pi}$ |
| $Y_1^0$ | $\sqrt{3/4\pi}\,\cos\theta$ |
| $Y_1^{\pm1}$ | $\mp\sqrt{3/8\pi}\,\sin\theta\,\ee^{\pm \ii\varphi}$ |
| $Y_2^0$ | $\sqrt{5/16\pi}\,(3\cos^2\theta-1)$ |

**Orthonormality** (integrated over the whole sphere):

$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}Y_{\ell'}^{m'*}\,Y_\ell^m\,\sin\theta\,\dd\theta\,\dd\varphi=\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}
$$

The $p_x,p_y,p_z$ orbitals of chemistry are just real linear combinations of $Y_1^{\pm1},Y_1^0$.

## Laguerre polynomials

**The problem they solve**: the hydrogen radial equation. After peeling off the long-range decay $\ee^{-r/na}$ and the short-range behaviour $r^\ell$, the remaining polynomial part satisfies the **associated Laguerre equation**. The series-termination condition delivers the principal quantum number $n$ — this is where the hydrogen levels $E_n=-13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$ come from (full derivation in [section 6.3](/en/ch/06-hydrogen/03-hydrogen-solution/)).

**Definition** (Rodrigues formula; $L_q^k$ is a polynomial of degree $q$):

$$
L_q(x)=\frac{\ee^{x}}{q!}\frac{\dd^q}{\dd x^q}\!\left(x^q\ee^{-x}\right),
\qquad
L_q^{k}(x)=(-1)^k\frac{\dd^k}{\dd x^k}L_{q+k}(x)
$$

**The first few**: $L_0=1$, $L_1=1-x$, $L_2=1-2x+\tfrac{x^2}{2}$;
$L_0^1=1$, $L_1^1=2-x$.

<Callout type="warning" title="Two normalisation conventions coexist">
Laguerre polynomials come in a "mathematical convention" (the formula above, with the $1/q!$, used by Griffiths) and an "old physics convention" (an extra overall factor of $(q+k)!$, used by some older texts). The two differ only by constants, but the normalisation coefficients of the hydrogen wavefunctions change accordingly. When comparing two books, first check $L_1^1$: the mathematical convention gives $2-x$, the old convention gives $4-2x$ (off by a multiple). scipy's `genlaguerre` uses the mathematical convention.
</Callout>

How the hydrogen radial wavefunction is assembled ($a$ is the Bohr radius):

$$
R_{n\ell}(r)\;\propto\;\left(\frac{2r}{na}\right)^{\ell}
L_{n-\ell-1}^{2\ell+1}\!\left(\frac{2r}{na}\right)\ee^{-r/na}
$$

The polynomial part has $n-\ell-1$ zeros — exactly the number of radial nodes.

## Spherical Bessel functions

**The problem they solve**: the radial equation in regions of zero (or constant) potential. After setting $u=rR$, the radial equation becomes
$R''+\frac{2}{r}R'+\left[k^2-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}\right]R=0$,
whose solutions are the **spherical Bessel functions** $j_\ell(kr)$ (regular at the origin) and the **spherical Neumann functions** $n_\ell(kr)$ (divergent at the origin).

**Low-order explicit expressions** (all trigonometric functions over powers — hand-computable):

| $\ell$ | $j_\ell(x)$ | $n_\ell(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ | $-\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$ |
| 1 | $\dfrac{\sin x}{x^2}-\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$ | $-\dfrac{\cos x}{x^2}-\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ |

**Recurrence** ($f_\ell$ stands for either $j_\ell$ or $n_\ell$ — same recurrence for both):

$$
f_{\ell+1}(x)=\frac{2\ell+1}{x}f_\ell(x)-f_{\ell-1}(x)
$$

**Behaviour at the two ends** (deciding which solution to keep hinges entirely on these two lines):

$$
j_\ell(x)\xrightarrow{x\to0}\frac{x^\ell}{(2\ell+1)!!},\qquad
j_\ell(x)\xrightarrow{x\to\infty}\frac{\sin(x-\ell\pi/2)}{x}
$$

$n_\ell$ diverges at the origin like $x^{-\ell-1}$, so **a region containing the origin can only use $j_\ell$**; the sinusoidal oscillation at large distance (phase shifted by $\ell\pi/2$) is the reference zero for the "phase shift" concept in the partial-wave treatment of 3D scattering.
