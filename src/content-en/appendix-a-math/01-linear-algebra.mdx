## Vector spaces and inner products

A **vector space** is, plainly put, "a set plus two rules": the elements of the set (here called state vectors $\ket{\psi}$) can be added together and multiplied by complex numbers, and the result stays inside the set. The superposition principle of quantum mechanics — a superposition of two allowed states is still an allowed state — is at bottom the statement that "states form a vector space".

An **inner product** is a rule assigning a complex number $\braket{\phi}{\psi}$ to every pair of vectors; it measures how much two states "resemble" each other. Three properties are required:

| Property | Formula | In plain words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conjugate symmetry | $\braket{\phi}{\psi}=\braket{\psi}{\phi}^*$ | Swapping the order means taking the complex conjugate |
| Linear in the right slot | $\braket{\phi}{a\psi_1+b\psi_2}=a\braket{\phi}{\psi_1}+b\braket{\phi}{\psi_2}$ | Linear in the second argument |
| Positive definite | $\braket{\psi}{\psi}\ge 0$, with equality only for $\ket{\psi}=0$ | The inner product of a state with itself is its "length squared" |

Mind the second row: the physics convention is **linear on the right, conjugate-linear on the left** ($\bra{a\phi}=a^*\bra{\phi}$) — the opposite of most mathematics textbooks.

Two consequences you will use constantly:

$$
\text{Cauchy–Schwarz:}\quad |\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2\le\braket{\phi}{\phi}\braket{\psi}{\psi}
$$

This inequality is the [mathematical backbone of the uncertainty principle](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/). The normalisation condition $\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1$ corresponds to total probability 1.

**Orthonormal basis**: a set $\{\ket{e_i}\}$ satisfying $\braket{e_i}{e_j}=\delta_{ij}$. Any state can be expanded as $\ket{\psi}=\sum_i c_i\ket{e_i}$, with coefficients read off directly from inner products: $c_i=\braket{e_i}{\psi}$. Abstracted into a single identity, this is the **completeness relation**

$$
\sum_i \ket{e_i}\bra{e_i}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}
$$

— "inserting a complete basis anywhere changes nothing", the single most frequently used move in Chapter 3.

## The eigenvalue problem

A matrix $\hat A$ acting on a generic vector both rotates and stretches it. But every matrix has some "special directions": acting along them produces pure stretching, no change of direction. These directions are the **eigenvectors**, and the stretch factors are the **eigenvalues**:

$$
\hat A\ket{a}=a\ket{a}
$$

Finding them takes two steps:

1. **Eigenvalues**: solve the characteristic equation $\det(\hat A-a\hat{\mathbb{1}})=0$. An $n$-dimensional matrix gives a degree-$n$ polynomial with $n$ roots.
2. **Eigenvectors**: substitute each $a$ back into $(\hat A-a\hat{\mathbb{1}})\ket{a}=0$ and solve the linear system.

For 2×2 matrices there is a handy shortcut: the eigenvalues sum to the trace (sum of diagonal entries), and their product equals the determinant:

$$
a_+ + a_- = \operatorname{tr}\hat A,\qquad a_+ a_- = \det\hat A
$$

<Derivation title="Worked example: eigenvalues and eigenvectors of σ_x" level="基础" minutes={5} open={true}>

The Pauli matrix $\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ plays the lead role in [spin measurement](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/04-stern-gerlach/).

**Eigenvalues**: $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_x=0$, $\det\sigma_x=-1$, so $a_++a_-=0$ and $a_+a_-=-1$, giving $a_\pm=\pm1$ at once. (Or grind through $\det\begin{pmatrix}-a&1\\1&-a\end{pmatrix}=a^2-1=0$ the honest way.)

**Eigenvectors**: for $a=+1$ the equation $\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}=0$ gives $\alpha=\beta$, and after normalisation

$$
\ket{+_x}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\ket{0}+\ket{1}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Likewise $a=-1$ gives $\ket{-_x}=(\ket{0}-\ket{1})/\sqrt2$. Check orthogonality: $\braket{+_x}{-_x}=\frac12(1-1)=0$. ✓

This is exactly the result that keeps reappearing in the main text: measuring spin along $x$, the eigenstates are equal-weight superpositions of $\ket{0}$ and $\ket{1}$.

</Derivation>

## Hermitian and unitary matrices

First define the **Hermitian conjugate** (symbol $\dagger$, read "dagger"): transpose the matrix, then complex-conjugate every entry, $(\hat A^\dagger)_{ij}=\hat A_{ji}^*$. It satisfies $(\hat A\hat B)^\dagger=\hat B^\dagger\hat A^\dagger$ — the order reverses.

| Type | Definition | Physical role | Key properties |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hermitian | $\hat A^\dagger=\hat A$ | Observables | All eigenvalues real; eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues mutually orthogonal |
| Unitary | $\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat{\mathbb{1}}$ | Time evolution, change of basis, quantum gates | Preserves inner products $\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket{\phi}{\psi}$; eigenvalues are unit-modulus complex numbers $\ee^{\ii\theta}$ |
| Projector | $\hat P^2=\hat P=\hat P^\dagger$ | Measurement | Eigenvalues are only 0 and 1 |

The first two are linked by the exponential map: if $\hat H$ is Hermitian, then $\ee^{-\ii\hat H t/\hbar}$ is unitary — which is why the [solution of the Schrödinger equation](/en/ch/03-formalism/09-time-evolution-pictures/) automatically conserves probability.

Two core theorems about Hermitian matrices (proofs in [section 3.5](/en/ch/03-formalism/05-hermitian-operators/)):

1. **Eigenvalues must be real** — so measurement outcomes are real numbers.
2. **Spectral theorem**: a Hermitian matrix always admits an orthonormal set of eigenvectors forming a complete basis.

## Diagonalisation and spectral decomposition

**Diagonalising** a matrix means switching to the basis made of its eigenvectors — in that basis the matrix becomes diagonal and all its information sits in plain view. Concretely: stack the normalised eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix $U$ (for a Hermitian matrix, $U$ is automatically unitary); then

$$
U^\dagger \hat A\, U=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,a_2,\dots)
$$

An equivalent way to write it is the **spectral decomposition** — splitting the matrix into a sum of "eigenvalue × corresponding projector":

$$
\hat A=\sum_i a_i\ket{a_i}\bra{a_i}
$$

Its greatest use is defining **functions of a matrix**: just apply the function eigenvalue by eigenvalue,

$$
f(\hat A)=\sum_i f(a_i)\ket{a_i}\bra{a_i}
$$

For instance $\ee^{-\ii\hat H t/\hbar}=\sum_n \ee^{-\ii E_n t/\hbar}\ket{E_n}\bra{E_n}$ — that single line is the entire mathematical content of the standard routine "expand in energy eigenstates, then attach a phase to each term".

<Callout type="warning" title="When can two matrices not be diagonalised simultaneously?">
Two Hermitian matrices can be diagonalised by the **same basis** if and only if they commute ($[\hat A,\hat B]=0$). Non-commuting matrices (such as $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$) have no shared eigenbasis — which is precisely the algebraic root of the [uncertainty principle](/en/ch/03-formalism/06-commutators/): no state exists that is an eigenstate of both.
</Callout>

## Tensor products

When two systems A and B combine into one larger system, the big system's state space is not a "concatenation" but a **tensor product** $\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_B$: the dimensions multiply ($2\times2=4$), and the basis consists of all pairings $\ket{i}_A\otimes\ket{j}_B$ (often abbreviated $\ket{ij}$).

The tensor (Kronecker) product of matrices expands as "each entry of the left matrix times the whole right matrix":

$$
\hat A\otimes \hat B=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}\hat B & A_{12}\hat B\\ A_{21}\hat B & A_{22}\hat B\end{pmatrix}
$$

The operating rule in one phrase: **each factor minds its own system** —
$(\hat A\otimes\hat B)(\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{\chi})=(\hat A\ket{\phi})\otimes(\hat B\ket{\chi})$.
"Act on system A only" is written $\hat A\otimes\hat{\mathbb{1}}$.

<Derivation title="Worked example: write down a Bell state and show it cannot factorise" level="进阶" minutes={4}>

In the two-qubit basis $\{\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}\}$, take

$$
\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}
$$

Suppose it could be written as a product state $(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\otimes(\gamma\ket0+\delta\ket1)$; expanding gives the four components $(\alpha\gamma,\alpha\delta,\beta\gamma,\beta\delta)$. We need the middle two to vanish and the outer two not to: $\alpha\delta=0$ forces $\alpha=0$ or $\delta=0$, yet $\alpha\gamma\neq0$ demands $\alpha\neq0$ and $\beta\delta\neq0$ demands $\delta\neq0$ — contradiction.

So $\ket{\Phi^+}$ is **not** a product of any two single-particle states: that is the definition of an entangled state, discussed in detail in [section 9.3](/en/ch/09-quantum-information/03-entanglement-measures/).

</Derivation>
