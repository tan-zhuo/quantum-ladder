Fine structure is the atom's built-in splitting, beyond our control. In this section we take matters into our own hands: put the atom in a magnetic or electric field, turn the knob, and watch the levels crack on command.

This is both the finale of the chapter and a dress rehearsal: for the first time we will systematically face the situation "Hamiltonian = a solvable part + a small tail" — and the regular army that handles it, perturbation theory, is the entire content of the next chapter.

## The phenomenon: a spectrometer next to a magnet

In 1896, Pieter Zeeman in Leiden placed a sodium flame between the poles of an electromagnet and saw the D lines visibly broaden; with a stronger field and a better grating, the lines outright **split into several**. Within days his teacher Lorentz produced the classical explanation: the magnetic field adds to or subtracts from the circular-motion frequency of the radiating charge about the field direction, so every line should split into **three**, with spacing proportional to $B$ — a prediction (the "normal Zeeman effect") that earned teacher and student the 1902 Nobel Prize.

Trouble arrived promptly. More and more lines refused to split in threes: one sodium D line split into four, the other into six; mercury had lines splitting into nine. The patterns, defying all system, were resignedly named the **anomalous Zeeman effect** — "anomalous" meaning "we don't understand it". Pauli's torment by it in the 1920s is legendary: asked by a colleague why he looked so glum, he replied, "How can anyone be happy who is thinking about the anomalous Zeeman effect?"

Yet the answer is already clenched in our fist, back in Chapter 5: **the electron's spin magnetic moment carries a factor $g\approx2$**.

## How a magnetic field enters the Hamiltonian

The atom's magnetic moment has two sources — the circulation of orbital motion, and spin ([section 5.4](/en/ch/05-angular-momentum/04-stern-gerlach/)):

$$
\hat{\vec\mu}=-\frac{\mu_B}{\hbar}\left(\hat{\vec L}+2\hat{\vec S}\right),
\qquad
\mu_B\equiv\frac{e\hbar}{2m_e}=5.788\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV/T}
$$

$\mu_B$ is the **Bohr magneton**, the natural unit of atomic magnetic moments; the coefficient 2 is the spin $g$-factor. With the field along $z$, the added energy is:

$$
\hat H_Z=-\hat{\vec\mu}\cdot\vec B=\frac{\mu_B B}{\hbar}\left(\hat L_z+2\hat S_z\right)
$$

That 2 is the whole source of the "anomaly": without it (pure orbital moment), the energy shift would be a clean $\mu_B B\,m_l$, every level would split by the same amount, and every line would split in three — the normal Zeeman effect. The moment spin joins in, orbit and spin convert to magnetic moment at different "exchange rates", and the pattern scrambles.

## The bigger term calls the tune: weak field versus strong field

Two "small terms" now share the stage: fine structure ($\sim10^{-4}$ eV) and the magnetic term ($\mu_B B$, $5.8\times10^{-5}$ eV per tesla). **Handle the bigger one first, then treat the smaller as a disturbance** — the governing principle of this whole section. The dividing line sits at

$$
\mu_B B\sim\Delta E_\text{fs}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B\sim\text{a few tesla}
$$

which lands squarely in the middle of laboratory field strengths, so both limits are experimentally accessible.

<DualView>
<div slot="left">

**Weak field ($B$ well below a few tesla): Zeeman obeys fine structure.**

Spin–orbit coupling first locks $\vec L$ and $\vec S$ into the total angular momentum $\vec J$, about which both precess rapidly; the slow magnetic field feels only the **average component of the moment along $\vec J$**. The good quantum numbers are $(n,l,j,m_j)$, and the shift is:

$$
\Delta E=g_J\,\mu_B B\,m_j
$$

$g_J$ is the **Landé g-factor** — the "averaged exchange rate", depending on $l$ and $j$:

$$
g_J=1+\frac{j(j+1)+s(s+1)-l(l+1)}{2j(j+1)}
$$

For instance $g_J=2$ for $2\mathrm s_{1/2}$, $2/3$ for $2\mathrm p_{1/2}$, $4/3$ for $2\mathrm p_{3/2}$ — **every level splits by a different amount**, and the line patterns their transitions combine into are naturally many-splendoured. The "anomaly" is hereby fully decoded: it was only ever an alias for "the $g$-factor differs from level to level".

</div>
<div slot="right">

**Strong field ($B$ well above a few tesla): fine structure obeys Zeeman.**

The magnetic term overpowers spin–orbit coupling; the lock between $\vec L$ and $\vec S$ is torn apart, and each precesses independently about $\vec B$ (the Paschen–Back effect). The good quantum numbers switch to $(n,l,m_l,m_s)$:

$$
\Delta E=\mu_B B\,(m_l+2m_s)
$$

The pattern actually simplifies — the lines return to an approximate triplet (selection rules $\Delta m_l=0,\pm1$, with $\Delta m_s=0$).

**One system, and as the field knob turns from small to large, the good quantum numbers change shift**: $(j,m_j)\to(m_l,m_s)$. In the middle ground neither set works, and the only recourse is to diagonalise the Hamiltonian within the degenerate subspace by brute force — a sentence whose full meaning unfolds in [section 7.2](/en/ch/07-approximation/02-degenerate-perturbation/).

</div>
</DualView>

<Callout type="insight" title="The Zeeman effect is the astronomer's magnetometer">
The splitting is proportional to $B$ — so read it backwards: **measure the splitting and you have measured the field**. In 1908 George Hale aimed the Mount Wilson telescope at a sunspot and found Zeeman splittings in its spectrum corresponding to about 0.3 T — **the first magnetic field ever measured beyond the Earth** — proving that sunspots are cool regions where magnetism suppresses convection. To this day, stellar surface fields and the micro-gauss whispers of interstellar molecular clouds are measured by Zeeman splitting (or its polarisation signatures). For scale: at 0.3 T, $\mu_B B\approx1.7\times10^{-5}$ eV — about 0.007 nm on a 500 nm line. The gratings of the late nineteenth century were already up to it.
</Callout>

## The Stark effect: the electric field's turn

In 1913, Johannes Stark observed electric-field-induced splitting of hydrogen's Balmer lines. The electric coupling term is the dipole energy:

$$
\hat H_S=e\mathcal E\,\hat z
$$

(with $\mathcal E$ the field strength, along $z$). One profound difference from the magnetic case: $\hat z$ is an **odd-parity** operator — and that will push "degeneracy" to centre stage.

<Derivation title="The ground state: why the response is quadratic" level="基础" minutes={6} open={true}>

**First-order shift = the average of the perturbation in the original state** (perturbation theory's first lesson — accept it as a reasonable guess for now; Chapter 7 proves it):

$$
\Delta E^{(1)}=\braket{\psi_{100}}{\hat H_S\,\psi_{100}}=e\mathcal E\braket{\psi_{100}}{\hat z\,\psi_{100}}
$$

The ground state is spherically symmetric with even parity: $\lvert\psi_{100}\rvert^2$ is an even function, and multiplied by the odd function $z$ the integral is **identically zero**:

$$
\Delta E^{(1)}=0
$$

In physical terms: a ground-state hydrogen atom **has no permanent electric dipole moment** — the field finds no ready-made handle to grab.

**The second-order effect: polarise first, then pull.** The field tugs the electron cloud slightly off-centre, **inducing** a dipole moment $p=\alpha_\text{pol}\mathcal E$, which then couples back to the field:

$$
\Delta E^{(2)}=-\frac12\alpha_\text{pol}\mathcal E^2,
\qquad
\alpha_\text{pol}=4.5\,(4\pi\varepsilon_0)\,a_0^3
$$

(The coefficient 4.5 is a classic Chapter 7 exercise; take the result on account for now.) Magnitude: even at a laboratory-strong $\mathcal E=10^6$ V/m, the shift is a mere $2\times10^{-10}$ eV — four orders of magnitude below even hyperfine structure. **A non-degenerate state's response to an electric field is pitifully weak.**

</Derivation>

<Derivation title="n = 2: degeneracy promotes quadratic to linear" level="进阶" minutes={7}>

In the $n=2$ shell, $2\mathrm s$ and $2\mathrm p$ are degenerate (in the Coulomb approximation) — and they have **opposite parity**. That changes everything.

**Mixing costs nothing.** Putting the electron in the superposition $c_1\psi_{200}+c_2\psi_{210}$ costs no energy (the two are degenerate), and such a superposition has indefinite parity — **it can possess a permanent dipole moment**. All the field has to do is pick out, from the degenerate pool, the combination whose dipole points its way.

**Compute the matrix elements.** Within the degenerate subspace, the only non-zero matrix element of $\hat H_S$ is (by direct integration):

$$
\braket{\psi_{200}}{\hat z\,\psi_{210}}=-3a_0
$$

($\psi_{21\pm1}$ sit out because $\Delta m\ne0$: $\hat z$ does not change $m$.) In the $(\psi_{200},\psi_{210})$ basis the perturbation matrix is anti-diagonal; diagonalising gives the eigenstates and eigenvalues:

$$
\psi_\pm=\frac{\psi_{200}\mp\psi_{210}}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
\Delta E=\pm\,3ea_0\mathcal E
$$

**A linear response!** The shift is first order in $\mathcal E$ — the atom behaves as a "polar atom" with permanent dipole moment $p=3ea_0$. At the same $\mathcal E=10^6$ V/m:

$$
\Delta E=3ea_0\mathcal E=3\times(5.29\times10^{-11}\ \text{m})\times(10^6\ \text{V/m})\times e=1.6\times10^{-4}\ \text{eV}
$$

**Six orders of magnitude** above the ground state's quadratic shift — the power of degeneracy. The fate of the four $n=2$ states: $\psi_\pm$ move up and down, $\psi_{21\pm1}$ stay put — one level splits into three equally spaced tiers.

**The lesson** (carry it into Chapter 7): when a perturbation meets degenerate states, you may not take just any old eigenstate and average — you must first diagonalise the perturbation within the degenerate subspace to find the "correct zeroth-order states". That is precisely the subject of [section 7.2](/en/ch/07-approximation/02-degenerate-perturbation/).

</Derivation>

<Callout type="warning" title="Too strong a field tears the atom apart">
The Stark shift cannot be extrapolated forever. The electric field tilts the whole Coulomb potential: $V=-e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r+e\mathcal E z$ forms a barrier of finite height on the downhill side, and the electron can **tunnel out** — field ionisation. The ground state needs $\sim10^{10}$ V/m before this matters, but the ionising field strength plummets as $1/n^4$: a Rydberg atom at $n=30$ falls apart at a few hundred V/cm. Experimentally this is a gift rather than a nuisance: selective field ionisation is the standard technique for detecting Rydberg atoms one by one. And in the other direction: a laser's oscillating electric field produces the "AC Stark shift", and the level control behind optical tweezers for cold atoms and optical-lattice clocks runs entirely on this section's physics.
</Callout>

## Closing the chapter

Take inventory of what these seven hydrogen sections leave us: one exact solution (levels + wavefunctions), one complete ledger of symmetries and degeneracies, and one energy ladder — Bohr levels at $10$ eV, fine structure at $10^{-4}$ eV, Lamb and hyperfine at $10^{-6}$ eV, and field-induced splittings tunable by a knob. At every step down the ladder we were doing the same thing: **treating each new effect as a small perturbation of a problem already solved**.

## What comes next

Looking back over the second half of this chapter, we have frankly been driving without a licence: "the first-order shift equals the average of the perturbation", "when degenerate, diagonalise first" — all reasonable guesses acted on before being proven. Time to pay up.

More pressing still: the hydrogen atom is quantum mechanics' last free lunch. **One step further — helium, the hydrogen molecule, any many-body system — and exact solutions do not exist, in principle.** Physics does not grind to a halt; it runs on a systematic machinery of approximations: perturbation theory, the variational method, WKB. In Chapter 7, we build that toolkit piece by piece.
