The Schrödinger equation tells you how the wavefunction evolves. Feynman gave a second account of the same physics, and it sounds far more outrageous:

> To go from A to B, the particle takes **every possible path**. Each path contributes an amplitude $\ee^{\ii S/\hbar}$, and the total amplitude is their sum.

Every path — including the one that loops around the Earth first, the one that doubles back, the one whose velocity jumps.

<Scene name="path-integral" />

## The propagator is a sum

$$
K(x_b,t_b;x_a,t_a) = \int \mathcal{D}[x(t)]\ \ee^{\ii S[x]/\hbar},
\qquad
S[x] = \int_{t_a}^{t_b} L(x,\dot x)\,\dd t .
$$

Every path has **exactly the same amplitude magnitude**; only the phase differs. That is deeply counter-intuitive: an absurd path contributes as much amplitude as the classical one.

So what makes the classical path special?

## Stationary phase: cancelling and not cancelling

Near the classical path the action $S$ is **stationary**, meaning its first variation vanishes: neighbouring paths have nearly the same phase, so their amplitudes add **in phase** and reinforce.

Far from it, $S$ changes rapidly, neighbouring phases are scrambled, and the contributions **cancel**.

The scene encodes phase as colour. You will see a band around the classical path where the colour changes gently, and beyond it the colour spinning into noise. Almost all of the sum comes from that band.

<Callout type="insight" title="ħ is the only switch here">
The phase is $S/\hbar$. The smaller $\hbar$, the larger the phase difference for the same $\Delta S$, the more complete the cancellation, and the narrower the surviving bundle.

In the limit $\hbar\to0$ only the exact classical path is left: **the principle of least action is not an independent law of physics, it is the classical limit of the path integral**.

The ħ slider in the scene turns that statement into something you can drag: shrink it and the bundle tightens to a line; enlarge it and quantum fluctuations bring the whole bundle back to life.
</Callout>

## Why the formulation is worth learning

The path integral is fully equivalent to the Schrödinger equation (you can derive one from the other), but it wins outright in several places:

- **Field theory**: quantising gauge fields and deriving Feynman rules is far more natural this way;
- **Statistical mechanics**: substitute $t\to-\ii\tau$ and the path integral becomes a partition function, making quantum mechanics and statistical physics the same mathematics;
- **Topological effects**: Aharonov–Bohm phases, instantons and θ-vacua are cleanest when you sum by topological class;
- **Numerics**: quantum Monte Carlo samples directly in path space.

<Derivation title="From the path integral back to the Schrödinger equation" level="选读">

For an infinitesimal time $\epsilon$, the single-step propagator is

$$
K(x',t+\epsilon;x,t) \approx \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\ii\hbar\epsilon}}
\exp\left\{\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\left[\frac{m(x'-x)^2}{2\epsilon} - \epsilon V\!\left(\tfrac{x+x'}{2}\right)\right]\right\}.
$$

Substituting into $\psi(x',t+\epsilon)=\int K\,\psi(x,t)\dd x$, writing $\eta = x'-x$ and expanding to second order, the Gaussian integrals give

$$
\langle\eta^2\rangle = \frac{\ii\hbar\epsilon}{m},
$$

so that

$$
\psi(x,t+\epsilon) = \psi + \frac{\ii\hbar\epsilon}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi - \frac{\ii\epsilon}{\hbar}V\psi + O(\epsilon^2),
$$

which rearranges into

$$
\ii\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\psi + V\psi .
$$

Note that $\langle\eta^2\rangle \propto \epsilon$ rather than $\epsilon^2$: the typical path is **continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere**, just like Brownian motion. "Velocity" is simply not a well-defined quantity in this framework.

</Derivation>

## Think it through

1. How would you describe the double slit in this language? (Hint: the barrier kills almost every path and leaves two families.)
2. If a path has an action $100\hbar$ larger than the classical one, is its contribution "small" or "cancelled"? What is the difference between those two statements?
3. Typical paths are nowhere differentiable. What, then, is "the velocity of the particle" in the path integral, and how does that connect to the uncertainty principle?
