"Stationary states do not change" and "quantum systems evolve" are usually stated in the same breath, which sounds contradictory to a beginner.

Both are right; they simply have different subjects.

<Scene name="superposition" />

## The general solution is a weighted sum

Any initial state can be expanded in energy eigenstates:

$$
\ket{\Psi(0)} = \sum_n c_n \ket{n},
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\ket{\Psi(t)} = \sum_n c_n\,\ee^{-\ii E_n t/\hbar}\ket{n}.
$$

In this basis time evolution is **trivial**: every coefficient just rotates in phase, its modulus untouched. That is why finding energy eigenstates is worth so much effort — once you have them, evolving any initial state is a matter of multiplying term by term.

The probability density

$$
|\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \sum_{n,m} c_n^* c_m\,\psi_n^*\psi_m\,\ee^{-\ii(E_m-E_n)t/\hbar}
$$

therefore has time-independent diagonal terms, and only the **cross terms** oscillate, at

$$
\omega_{nm} = \frac{E_m - E_n}{\hbar}.
$$

<Callout type="insight" title="Bohr's frequency condition is not an assumption">
Bohr had to postulate $h\nu = E_2-E_1$ to explain spectral lines.

In Schrödinger's framework it is **derived**: the electric dipole moment $\langle x\rangle(t)$ of a superposition oscillates at $\omega_{21}$, and an oscillating dipole radiates — at that frequency, naturally.

Switch on the $\langle x\rangle(t)$ trace in the scene and you are watching that antenna swing. Keep a single level and it flatlines: eigenstates do not radiate, which is why atoms are stable.
</Callout>

## Three statements worth separating

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| "An eigenstate does not change in time" | Sloppy. The state vector changes (the phase turns); the **observables** do not. |
| "The global phase of $\ket{\Psi}$ is meaningless" | Correct. But the **relative** phase between terms does mean something, and it runs. |
| "A superposition has the average of the two energies" | Dangerous. The expectation value is $\sum|c_n|^2E_n$, but **every single measurement** returns some $E_n$; the average is never an outcome. |

## Beats: the difference of two frequencies

Superposing two eigenstates makes the density oscillate at the difference frequency. It is exactly the mathematics of two tuning forks beating.

$$
T = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{|E_2-E_1|}.
$$

You can check this directly in the scene: change which levels are superposed, or change the well parameters to move the gap, and time the period against the formula. The larger the gap, the faster it sloshes.

<Derivation title="Coherent states: the most classical quantum states" level="选读">

The oscillator has a special family of superpositions:

$$
\ket{\alpha} = \ee^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_n \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n}.
$$

Because the oscillator's levels are evenly spaced ($E_n = \hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$), every cross term oscillates at an integer multiple of $\omega$, and the superposition **does not disperse**: it keeps its Gaussian shape and swings back and forth like a classical ball, saturating the uncertainty relation at $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$ throughout.

This is the quantum state of a laser field and the cleanest example of a classical limit. The "Coherent state" preset in the scene loads it — note that it is the only superposition whose shape is preserved. Do the same in a square well and the packet falls apart; in the infinite well, where $E_n\propto n^2$, it reassembles completely after a revival time (a finite well only partially), which is called a quantum revival.

</Derivation>

## Think it through

1. Set $c_1 = 0.99$ and $c_2 = 0.14$. Does the **frequency** of the sloshing change? Does the **amplitude**? Guess first.
2. In the infinite well $E_n\propto n^2$, so the gaps are not constant. Which sloshes faster, 1+2 or 3+4?
3. An atom in an energy eigenstate does not radiate. Why, then, does a real excited atom decay spontaneously? (Hint: quantise the electromagnetic field too; the stationary state belongs to atom **plus** field.)
