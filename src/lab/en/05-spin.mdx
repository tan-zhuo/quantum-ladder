Spin 1/2 is the **smallest** non-trivial system in quantum mechanics: its state space is two-dimensional.

How small is that? Small enough that we can draw **every** state at once, leaving nothing out. No other system allows this — hydrogen's state space is infinite-dimensional and you can only look at one orbital at a time. The pure states of a spin-1/2 form exactly a sphere, and suddenly the abstract notion of "quantum state" is something you can push around with a finger.

Look first, calculate second.

## Why one point on a sphere is enough

A two-dimensional complex vector has four real parameters:

$$
\ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix},\qquad a, b \in \mathbb{C}.
$$

Two of them are fake. Normalisation $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$ removes one; the global phase — $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ee^{\ii\alpha}\ket{\psi}$ give identical results for every possible measurement — removes another. Two real parameters remain, and they are precisely the two angles of a sphere:

$$
\ket{\psi} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{0} + \ee^{\ii\varphi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{1}.
$$

<Callout type="warning" title="The half-angle is not a typo">
The angle in the exponent is $\theta$; the angle in the coefficients is $\theta/2$. That factor of two is the signature of spin 1/2: **going once around the Bloch sphere ($\theta: 0 \to 2\pi$) turns the state vector only half way round**, and you need two full turns to return. When you drag θ from 0 to 180° in the scene you travel half way around the sphere while the state goes all the way from $\ket{0}$ to $\ket{1}$ — orthogonal states sit at **antipodes**, not at right angles.
</Callout>

The matching geometric object is the **Bloch vector**

$$
\vec{r} = \braket{\psi}{\vec{\sigma}\,\psi} = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta),
$$

which is the arrow in the scene. Pure states always satisfy $|\vec r| = 1$ and lie on the surface; points **inside** the ball mean something too — they are mixed states, and they wait until [module 12](/en/lab/12-decoherence/).

## Precession: the field does not do what you expect

Put the spin in a field along $z$, so $H = -\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$. The resulting equation of motion is remarkably clean:

$$
\frac{\dd \vec r}{\dd t} = \vec\omega \times \vec r .
$$

A cross product means the rate of change is **always perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\vec\omega$**. Hence:

- $|\vec r|$ is constant (the state stays pure);
- $\vec r \cdot \hat\omega$ is constant (the angle to the field is locked);
- only the azimuth around the field increases, at a constant rate.

That is the cone in the scene. Classical intuition usually expects the field to bend the spin round until it points along the field — but that is what a **dissipative** system does (a compass needle in air with friction). Unitary evolution of an isolated quantum system does no such thing; it only turns. Laying the spin down along the field requires coupling to an environment, which is [module 12](/en/lab/12-decoherence/).

<Derivation title="Precession from the Schrödinger equation">

Take $H = -\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\sigma_z$. In the Heisenberg picture operators obey

$$
\frac{\dd \hat\sigma_i}{\dd t} = \frac{\ii}{\hbar}[H, \hat\sigma_i].
$$

Using the Pauli commutators $[\sigma_i, \sigma_j] = 2\ii\,\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k$:

$$
[\sigma_z, \sigma_x] = 2\ii\sigma_y, \qquad [\sigma_z, \sigma_y] = -2\ii\sigma_x, \qquad [\sigma_z,\sigma_z]=0 .
$$

Substituting gives

$$
\dot\sigma_x = -\omega\,\sigma_y,\qquad \dot\sigma_y = \omega\,\sigma_x,\qquad \dot\sigma_z = 0,
$$

and taking expectation values yields $\dot{\vec r} = \vec\omega\times\vec r$ with $\vec\omega = \omega\hat z$. The third equation, $\dot\sigma_z = 0$, is exactly the statement that the angle to the field never changes.

</Derivation>

## Measurement: projection, not inspection

Measuring the spin along a unit axis $\hat n$ can only return $\pm 1$, with probabilities

$$
P_\pm = \frac{1 \pm \vec r\cdot\hat n}{2}.
$$

The dashed line dropping from the arrowhead onto the measurement axis in the scene draws exactly the projection $\vec r \cdot \hat n$. After the measurement the state **collapses** onto the corresponding eigenstate — the arrow jumps to one end of the axis.

There is a trap here that catches nearly everyone: measuring again along the same axis necessarily repeats the first result. To see the statistics of $P_\pm$ you must **re-prepare** the same $\ket{\psi}$ every time. That is what the "Re-prepare ×200" button does, and the label spells it out on purpose.

## Taking the sphere into the laboratory: Stern–Gerlach

The Bloch sphere is a mathematical picture. What Stern and Gerlach did in 1922 was the first time that picture hit people in a visible form: a beam of silver atoms crossing an inhomogeneous magnetic field did **not** smear into a continuous band. It split cleanly into two.

<Scene name="stern-gerlach" />

Chaining the apparatus makes it more interesting. Split along $z$ with the first magnet and block the lower beam; measure $z$ again with a second and everything still comes out upper — measurement repeats itself. But make the second one measure along $x$ and the output is fifty-fifty again; add a third along $z$ and the lower beam, supposedly filtered away, **is back**.

Nothing classical does this. It says that measuring along $x$ is not reading off a pre-existing property; it genuinely changes the state.

## Three standard misreadings

<Callout type="misconception" title="“A direction on the Bloch sphere is the direction the spin points in space”">
No. The sphere is **state space**, not real space. $\theta = 90°$ does not mean the spin "lies flat"; it means that measuring along $z$ gives up and down equally often. A direction in real space only appears once a measurement axis is chosen.
</Callout>

<Callout type="misconception" title="“The electron is spinning”">
The name is a historical accident. Treat the electron as a classical rotating ball and its equator would move far faster than light. Spin is intrinsic angular momentum, and its existence follows naturally from the relativistic wave equation (the Dirac equation) — see [module 14](/en/lab/14-frontier/) and chapter 12 of the textbook.
</Callout>

<Callout type="misconception" title="“It was already up or down; we simply did not know”">
That is the local hidden-variable assumption, and it can be tested experimentally. Bell inequalities give the criterion, and in [module 08](/en/lab/08-entanglement/) you get to refute it yourself.
</Callout>
