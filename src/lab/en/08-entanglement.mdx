Everything odd so far could still, at a stretch, be explained away by "the particle carries some property we have not identified".

This module ends that option. The power of Bell's theorem is that it does not ask you to believe quantum mechanics. It needs only two assumptions that look self-evident — **locality** (a measurement here does not affect the outcome there) and **realism** (measurement reads off a property that already exists) — and proves that a certain measurable quantity must then be at most 2.

Experiment gives $2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$.

## An entangled state: definite as a whole, random in its parts

Take the singlet (total spin zero):

$$
\ket{\Psi^-} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\ket{\uparrow}_A\ket{\downarrow}_B - \ket{\downarrow}_A\ket{\uparrow}_B\right).
$$

It **cannot** be written as $\ket{\phi}_A\otimes\ket{\chi}_B$, which is the definition of entanglement.

Consequently:

- Alice looking at her particle alone gets ↑ and ↓ equally often, completely at random, whatever axis she picks;
- yet whenever the two use the **same** axis, the results are always opposite — perfectly correlated.

The two spheres in the scene are the reduced states of A and B. Both sit at the centre (maximally mixed), which says that locally there is no information at all. All of it is in the correlation.

<Callout type="warning" title="Entanglement cannot carry a message">
If the outcomes are strictly correlated, can we signal faster than light with them?

No. Alice cannot control whether she gets ↑ or ↓; her side is 50/50 noise. Bob's data alone is 50/50 too — whether or not Alice measured, and however she measured. Only when the two records are brought together **over a classical channel** and compared does the correlation appear.

The formal statement is the **no-signalling theorem**, and it is what keeps quantum mechanics on speaking terms with special relativity.
</Callout>

## CHSH: turning philosophy into a number

Alice picks one of two angles $a, a'$; Bob picks one of $b, b'$; each records $\pm1$. Define the correlation $E(a,b) = \langle A B\rangle$ and then

$$
S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b').
$$

**What local hidden variables predict**: if each particle carries predetermined values $A(\lambda), B(\lambda)$, then for every $\lambda$

$$
A(a)B(b) - A(a)B(b') + A(a')B(b) + A(a')B(b') = A(a)[B(b)-B(b')] + A(a')[B(b)+B(b')] = \pm 2,
$$

because one bracket is 0 and the other is $\pm2$. Averaging gives $|S| \le 2$.

**What quantum mechanics predicts**: for the singlet, $E(a,b) = -\cos\theta_{ab}$. Taking $a=0°$, $a'=90°$, $b=45°$, $b'=135°$, the four angles are $45°, 135°, 45°, 45°$, so

$$
|S| = \left|{-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right| = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.828 .
$$

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not import the photon angles">
Many references quote the optimal angles as $0°,\,45°,\,22.5°,\,67.5°$ — that is the **photon polarisation** convention, where the correlation goes as $\cos 2\theta$ and the angles are halved. This scene simulates spin 1/2, where the correlation is $\cos\theta$, so the optimal spacing is $45°$ rather than $22.5°$. Use the wrong set and $|S|$ tops out at 2.39 no matter how you tune it.
</Callout>

You can drag all four angles in the scene and watch $S$ respond. The defaults are the optimal set above.

<Derivation title="Where the cosine comes from" level="进阶">

For the singlet, the joint expectation along $\hat a$ and $\hat b$ is

$$
E(\hat a,\hat b) = \bra{\Psi^-}(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat a)\otimes(\vec\sigma\cdot\hat b)\ket{\Psi^-} = -\,\hat a\cdot\hat b = -\cos\theta_{ab}.
$$

A pretty property of the singlet is that it is anti-correlated along **every** direction (it is rotationally invariant), so only the relative angle matters.

The crucial difference is cosine versus kinked line: the best a local hidden-variable model can manage is piecewise linear, while quantum mechanics gives a cosine. They agree at $0°$, $90°$ and $180°$ and differ most around $45°$ — exactly where the four CHSH angles are placed.

</Derivation>

## The loopholes, and how they were closed

Early Bell experiments left two classical escape routes:

- **Detection loophole**: only a fraction of pairs are detected, and if the losses correlate with the hidden variable a classical model can still fit the data. Ion experiments first reached high enough detection efficiency in 2001, and superconducting-qubit and photon experiments followed.
- **Locality loophole**: if the two measurement choices have time to communicate, locality was never really tested. Aspect used fast switching in 1982; Weihs used space-like separation in 1998.

In 2015 three experiments (Delft, NIST, Vienna) closed both at once. The 2022 Nobel Prize went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger.

The only remaining gap is superdeterminism — the assumption that even the experimenters' choice of angles was fixed in advance. It cannot be falsified and it cannot be used to predict anything.

## Think it through

1. Set all four angles equal. What is $S$? Why does this choice test nothing non-classical?
2. Quantum mechanics reaches $2\sqrt2$, while the algebraic maximum is 4. Is there a theory that is more non-local than quantum mechanics yet still forbids signalling? (Keyword: PR box. Why nature stopped at $2\sqrt2$ has no accepted answer.)
3. If Alice measures first and Bob second, it is tempting to say "Alice's measurement collapsed Bob's particle". Change inertial frame and the order reverses. What is wrong with the question?
